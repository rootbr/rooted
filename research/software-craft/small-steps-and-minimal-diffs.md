# Research note — Small steps and minimal diffs

Topic `small-steps-and-minimal-diffs` of group F. Changing code, domain `change`, id prefix `CHG`. Formulation locators: TF (Tidy First? A Personal Exercise in Empirical Software Design, Beck 2023: Part I 'Tidyings'; Part II 'Managing' — 'Separate Tidying', 'Batch Sizes', 'Getting Untangled'; Part III 'Theory' — 'Structure and Behavior'); the openable formulation of the same practice is google/eng-practices 'Small CLs'.

The canon's formulation of this topic is Tidy First? Part II. The book cannot be opened, so its openable statement is google/eng-practices 'Small CLs'. Both give one shape to a change: it carries one logical change; structural tidying stays apart from behaviour change; it carries no drive-by reformatting or unrelated fixes; its tests come with it; each change builds and passes on its own; and large work is split by stacking or by horizontal and vertical slices. Each of these rules is stated in fetched official documentation from Google (small-cls.md, looking-for.md, pushback.md), the Linux kernel (submitting-patches.rst, 5.Posting.rst), git (SubmittingPatches, gitworkflows, CodingGuidelines, git-bisect-lk2009), LLVM (DeveloperPolicy, CodingStandards, CodeReview), CPython, Kubernetes and Node.js. Research reaches the topic only through relays and GitHub-hosted abstracts, and it supports the rules as follows. Tangled changes are common and corrupt history-based analysis: up to 15% of bug fixes in five Java projects, with at least 16.6% of files misassociated (Herzig and Zeller, relayed). Over 40% of review changesets at Microsoft can be decomposed (Barnett et al., relayed). Refactoring revisions often carry bug fixes, and floss-refactoring mistakes were frequent (Kim et al. ICSE 2011, fetched), although programmers frequently intersperse refactoring with other changes (Murphy-Hill et al. ICSE 2009, fetched). In a controlled experiment, decomposition gave fewer wrongly reported issues but no change in defects found (Di Biase et al., relayed). Pull-request size does not relate to time-to-merge (Kudrjavets et al. MSR 2022, fetched through a mirror). The cards therefore rest on reviewability, bisectability and history quality, not on speed or on defects found. The industry disagrees in seven places: line-count thresholds (100/1000 at Google, about 200 elsewhere, against the kernel's one logical change); the lower bound on splitting ('err on the side of too small' against 'Do not overdo it'); whether small cleanups ride along (the Boy Scout Rule and eng-practices' local-rename allowance against Kubernetes, CPython and the kernel); whether a new API ships with its use in the same change or in a separate patch of the series; how formatting is adopted (Black's one massive commit against LLVM/darker's touched lines only); horizontal against vertical increments; and LLVM's post-merge tolerance against 'every commit builds'. Six rules become cards: restructuring kept apart from behaviour; only the changed lines reformatted; moved code moved unchanged; one logical change per change; new code landing with its use; and each change building on its own. The size threshold and standalone style sweeps are pending for lack of a separating condition. Rhythm, series hygiene, tidy-first ordering, the benefits claim, over-splitting and splitting strategies are held because they cannot be checked on a diff. Tests in the same change already ship as TST-14.

## Formulation layer

The canon agrees on one shape for a change: one logical change per commit or PR, with structural tidying or refactoring kept apart from behaviour changes. Reformatting and unrelated fixes go in their own changes, tests ship with the code they cover, and every change in a series has to build and pass on its own. This is stated in openable form by google/eng-practices 'Small CLs' and its 'Style' and 'Tests' pages, the Linux kernel submitting-patches and 5.Posting guides, git's gitworkflows, SubmittingPatches and CodingGuidelines, LLVM's DeveloperPolicy, the CPython devguide, the Kubernetes PR guide, Software Engineering at Google chapter 9, and the 97 Things essays. All of these were fetched and their quotes checked word for word. Tidy First? (Separate Tidying, Batch Sizes, Rhythm, Getting Untangled, First/After/Later/Never, Structure and Behavior) could not be opened, and neither could Fowler's Refactoring, The Pragmatic Programmer, Working Effectively with Legacy Code, TDD by Example, A Philosophy of Software Design chapter 19, or Beck's 'make the change easy' tweet. These are recorded as marked paraphrases from memory and must never be a card's only anchor. The sources disagree in four places. (1) Martin's Boy Scout Rule ('check a module in cleaner') pulls against the minimal-diff guidance, which eng-practices and git only partly allow (small local cleanups, preparatory clean-up). (2) Martin and Ousterhout disagree on increment size in their public discussion ('keep the cycle time short' against 'a point of diminishing returns'), and on 'Abstraction-First incrementalism' against feature slices. (3) eng-practices says to 'err on the side of' too-small CLs, while the kernel warns 'Do not overdo it', and Kubernetes says 25 commits is 'cumbersome'. (4) Size thresholds differ: about 100 lines in eng-practices, about 200 in Software Engineering at Google, and in the kernel a single patch can be 'reasonably large' if it is one logical change. The research papers named as candidates belong to the evidence layer and are not repeated here, except Sadowski et al., which Software Engineering at Google cites for '35% of the changes ... to a single file'. Team batch policy (Accelerate, Continuous Delivery, trunk-based branching in Software Engineering at Google chapter 16) is out of scope and was not itemised.

## Reception layer

Practitioner and project-policy reception broadly agrees on the canon: one logical change per change, refactorings and reformatting kept apart from behaviour changes, tests alongside the code, and every commit in a series building and passing. This holds across the Linux kernel, git, LLVM, Kubernetes, CPython, Node.js, GitLab, Phabricator, the Google guides, and the stacking tools of Gerrit, GitHub (2026) and GitLab. Most of these were fetched from raw GitHub sources; WebSearch was unavailable, so HN and Lobsters threads and named-blog critiques could not be opened. The disputes are specific. (1) Velocity: Kudrjavets et al. (MSR 2022; abstract fetched through a GitHub arXiv mirror) find that pull-request size does not relate to time-to-merge. That contradicts 'small CLs are reviewed more quickly', and 2026 practitioner notes now separate the review-quality claim from the speed claim. (2) Lower bound and thresholds: the kernel ('do not overdo it', 500 patches), Phabricator ('no smaller'), Kubernetes ('do NOT make trivial edits') and the Google doc itself ('not so small that its implications are difficult to understand') all push back on 'err toward too small'. The 200–400 LOC figure comes from a 2006 SmartBear vendor study, and a 2026 note flags it as vendor-tied and mechanically biased. SWE at Google admits that 'some engineers at Google admittedly are not fans'. Other open splits: tests in the same change (Google, Node) or as a separate patch in the series (kernel); drive-by cleanups (the Boy Scout Rule, Google's allowance for small cleanups, git's 'side effect of real work') against strict separation (Kubernetes, Phabricator, CPython); one big reformat commit plus .git-blame-ignore-revs (Black) against formatting only the changed lines (darker, git clang-format, LLVM); squash-to-one-idea (Phabricator) against keeping layered commits (Kubernetes, git); every commit green (kernel, Node) against LLVM's post-merge 'okay to break the build occasionally'; and horizontal stubs (Google) against the kernel's rule that new code should be active immediately. Tool rules that check this mechanically are size labelers (pr-size-labeler 10/100/500/1000, fail_if_xl off by default), new-code-only linting (golangci-lint `new`, default false), diff-scoped formatters (darker, git clang-format), and trailing-comma rules justified by smaller diffs (Ruff COM812; ESLint comma-dangle, whose default is 'never').

## Evidence layer

Each candidate rule of the topic has an openable anchor in official project documentation, all fetched from raw.githubusercontent.com. One concern per change, refactoring kept apart from behaviour, no drive-by reformatting, tests in the same change, every change building and passing on its own, and splitting large work are stated by google/eng-practices small-cls.md, the Linux kernel process docs, git's SubmittingPatches/gitworkflows/CodingGuidelines, LLVM's DeveloperPolicy/CodingStandards/CodeReview, the CPython devguide and the Go and rustc contribution guides. The git, Sapling and Black/darker documentation describes the mechanisms: patch staging, commit splitting, `rebase --exec` and `blame --ignore-rev`. A local git-bisect test shows mechanically that a non-building intermediate commit leaves the culprit ambiguous. The research side could only be reached through the orchestrator's relayed snippets (Herzig & Zeller 2013, Barnett et al. 2015, Di Biase et al. 2019, Kamei et al. 2013, Sadowski et al. 2018) and through abstracts in neverworkintheory's tex/nwit.bib (Śliwerski 2005, Mahmoudi 2019, AlOmar 2022). These support tangling being common and harmful to mining and review, and change-level risk prediction, but not a measured defect benefit of small changes. WebSearch was exhausted, so Kawrykow & Robillard 2011, Tao et al. 2012, Murphy-Hill et al. 2012, Mockus & Weiss 2000, Arima et al. 2018 and the contrary Kudrjavets et al. 2022 ('small changes do not merge faster') are unfetched pointers only, and no openable research was found for the tests-in-the-same-change rule. The sources disagree on four points. (1) Line-count thresholds: Google gives 100/1000 lines and the SWE book about 200, while the kernel accepts a large patch if it holds a single logical change and warns against over-splitting. (2) A new API and its first caller: Google says same CL, the kernel's submitting-patches says two patches. (3) Formatting: LLVM/darker format only the touched lines, while Black and Langa favour one separate whole-repo reformat commit. (4) Small cleanups inside a change: Google allows them, and the Boy Scout Rule asks for them. Di Biase et al. found that decomposition did not change defects found or rationale understanding, which tempers the review-quality claims.
