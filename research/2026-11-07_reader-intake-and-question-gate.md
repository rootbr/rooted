# Reader intake and the question gate — evidence report

The skill `handing-over-to-readers` shapes what an agent hands to a human reader. Its operator decided two changes and commissioned the research behind them. A reader intake: an agent that does not know its reader holds a short interview — who he is, what he knows, what he expects, how deep he wants to go — before a long explanation. A gate before every question: before the agent asks the operator anything, a check establishes whether the answer is already in files, records or his earlier answers, and what the answer changes.

Fitting an explanation to valid information about the reader pays: explanations taught 1.52 against 0.99 on a 0–3 scale and drew about half the follow-up questions, while random information came out below none (0.80), a difference the study did not test on its own [1]. An estimate of the reader shifted up and one shifted down both taught less than a valid one (6.23 and 6.37 against 8.47 of 18), and the two errors were never tested against each other [81]. The explainer's own judgement and inference from one message do not yield that information: tutors detected 0 % of students' false beliefs [2], and a language model inferring expertise from the query alone was right in about 20 % of cases against 69–77 % after about 1.2 probing turns [3]. A topic-specific probe correlates up to r = .92 with a full knowledge test, a global self-rating about .29 [4, 5]. Asking costs the person asked: a poor question leaves him worse off than none [6], and after clarifying questions people accepted more wrong answers, 52 % against 39 % [7]. Prompted detection of when to ask sits near chance [8, 9], and an "ask only when needed" prompt left one model defaulting silently on every ambiguous objective task [10]; a separate agent that decides whether to ask came within 1.4 points of the fully specified result where one prompted agent stayed 9.6 points below it [98]. No opened study counts how many questions already had a written answer, and BM25 over up to two thousand question-answer pairs ranks the earlier answer first in about half to two-thirds of cases, with the false-match rate unmeasured [11, 12]; duplicate-question recall reported by one team fell by 20 to 33 points when another rebuilt the method on fresh data [106]. A PreToolUse hook on `AskUserQuestion` receives the whole card and can deny, rewrite or answer it, while a question asked in prose reaches only the `Stop` hook after the operator has read it [13].

## Method

Six researchers worked on 2026-11-07 from one commission, each on its own question set:

- r1, adapting to knowledge: whether fitting an explanation to the reader's knowledge pays, and how to diagnose it first;
- r2, eliciting a profile: user modelling, elicitation techniques, survey method;
- r3, LLM clarification: when a language-model agent should ask, how it elicits the reader, how it adapts depth;
- r4, cost of asking: interruption, mixed initiative, decision theory;
- r5, answer already known: finding an existing answer before asking a person;
- r6, hook mechanics: what Claude Code offers a plugin for a question gate and a reader intake.

Each finding carries a class: `direct` (a human working with an AI agent or reading its output), `transfer` (another population or task) or `consistent only` (it fits and does not test); r6 marks `documented` what the official documentation states and `not documented` its own inference. Only an opened source carries a finding. Each source entry states what was opened and its main limit; a source two researchers reported is one entry under the fuller status, and where their numbers differ the text names which file reports which. That pass left 33 leads unopened.

On 2026-11-14 five readers, l1 to l5, opened those leads, each from the same commission and with the same classes. 31 leads became sources [81–111], one raised source [41] from its abstract to its full text, and one stayed closed. A reader's entry names what it opened and where a lead's description did not survive the reading. The session then opened Adamczyk & Bailey [100] and the documentation pages [109, 110] itself, to check the quotes the rules below rest on.

111 sources carry findings: 66 opened in full text, 16 by a section, a summary page or another version of the text, 29 by the abstract alone. 14 leads stay unopened.

## Does fitting the explanation to the reader pay

Between people explaining to people, it pays when the information is valid. In 60 dyads at a text helpdesk, learners gained 1.52 with valid information about their knowledge, 0.99 without and 0.80 with random information (η² = .15), and asked 2.15, 4.35 and 4.70 follow-up questions per exchange (η² = .16) [1]. The information came from knowledge tests, not self-ratings. A replication with 80 dyads found adjustment to the individual rather than general sensitisation [14]. In 15 against 15 tutoring dyads, such information left declarative knowledge unchanged and improved transfer (η² = .25), reduced false beliefs (η² = .28) and reduced follow-up questions (η² = .17) [15]. All three are transfer, close to our written medium.

Which way the estimate errs was tested once. In 45 dyads the expert saw the layperson's test score shifted two points up or down on a six-point scale, or unshifted, and followed the shift in his explanations (η² = .57). The layperson's knowledge gain on a test of 18 was 8.47 with the valid score, 6.23 with the raised one and 6.37 with the lowered one [81]. The study tested the valid condition against the two shifted ones pooled (F(1,42) = 4.83, p = .03, η² = .10) and ran no test of raised against lowered and no equivalence test, with about 15 dyads in a cell. Follow-up questions were 1.40, 3.80 and 2.87; comprehension questions rose after the raised estimate (0.67, 2.40, 0.87), and the extra requests for missing information after the lowered one were marginal (p = .08). The conference abstract of the same authors reports the same pattern and is very likely the same data [82]; their review restates the result as equal harm in both directions and adds no data [83]. Transfer.

The error is asymmetric. Across 60 experiments and 5,924 participants, novices learned more with high assistance (d = 0.505) and experts with low assistance (d = −0.428), an interaction of d = 0.971 with I² ≈ 91 % [16]. Its authors advise giving help rather than withholding it when in doubt. A tutorial adapted by a first-step test gave d = 0.46 in knowledge gain by r1's reading of [17] and [4]. In the study that added the learner's effort rating, 30 students in 15 yoked pairs took three first-step items of one minute each; the adapted tutorial gave a marginal knowledge gain (2.27 against 0.67, d = 0.55, one-tailed p < .1) and a gain in a composite of performance and effort (d = 0.69, one-tailed p < .05), and the accuracy of the three-item test was not measured [85]. These are transfer from students.

With language-model assistants the evidence is direct and mostly correlational. In 25,033 Bing Copilot conversations, the assistant answered at a proficient or expert level in 77 %, and the user–assistant expertise gap correlated with satisfaction at r = −0.29 on complex tasks and −0.04 on simple ones (r3); an answer below the user's level lowered model-estimated satisfaction (R² = 0.11, r2) [18]. Expertise-matched answers raised satisfaction from 3.71 to 4.36 of 5 (r = .76) for 40 participants [19]. Probing raised comprehensibility from 3.4 to 4.6–4.7 of 5 by r1's reading and by 4.1 % by r3's, with factual accuracy unchanged [3]. Personalisation the user did not steer gave 58.1 % against 62.0 % accuracy overall in 13 participants, cut intermediates from 56.3 % to 51.3 % and lowered novices' alignment rating from 3.60 to 3.00 [20]. Claude 3 Opus refused about 11 % of questions from personas marked low-education and non-native against 3.61 % for the control, answered them condescendingly in 43.74 % (under 1 % otherwise), and SciQ accuracy fell from 95.6 % to as low as 79.8 % (transfer from stated personas) [21].

## How the reader's level can be learned

**Self-rating.** Across 22 meta-analyses, self-evaluation correlated with performance at M = .29 (range .09–.63, SD .11), more strongly for domain-specific ratings and for objective, familiar or simple tasks [5]. Self-assessed knowledge tracked motivation and satisfaction more than learning [22]. Bottom-quartile scorers placed themselves at the 62nd percentile while scoring at the 12th, which r1 uses for the direction of bias only [23]. A re-analysis of 929 adults by a heteroscedasticity test found no sign that low scorers judge themselves less accurately, so [23] backs a general upward bias and not a larger error among the least able; a replication and a comment dispute it, both unopened [84]. The overrating is strongest for knowledge of mechanisms, across 12 studies (consistent only) [24]. The rest are transfer, and none tests a rating about one topic.

**Inference from his messages.** In browser logs of over 500,000 users, experts used domain-lexicon terms in 37 % of queries against 26 %, and expertise was classified at .616 after one query and .718 after a session against a .566 baseline (transfer) [25]. From the query alone, a language model reached 16–22 % accuracy (r1) or 16–20 % for the strongest baseline (r3), with Fleiss κ 30.6–41.3 and "Unknown" in 28–83 % of cases (r2) and abstention on 89–94 % for some baselines (r3); a pasted jargon-heavy query fakes expertise [3]. Novices who quoted exam text looked expert to passive inference [20]. On a five-level scale, r3 reports an error of 1.60 at random, 1.16–1.35 for text-only baselines, 0.49 with ten of the same user's labelled queries and 0.40 with keystroke timing added; r2 reports .488 for text, .790 for behaviour, .398 for both, and inference matched self-report (p = .241) [19]. Three search behaviours (documents saved, query length, the rank of opened documents) predicted domain knowledge in 35 searchers, and the abstract gives no accuracy figure [92]. A system that built its model of a user from one open self-description and a set of stereotypes chose novels rated good in 102 of 141 cases (72 %) against 54 of 114 (47 %) for random choices, without a significance test; it asked a further direct question only after a prediction had failed, and did not model knowledge (transfer) [88].

**The explainer's own judgement.** Untrained tutors judged knowledge stated in the text with 72 % accuracy, beliefs outside it with 21 % and false beliefs (2.6 per student) with 0 % [2]. They assumed the correct mental model for 19 of 22 students; 14 held it. Experts predicted novices' task times worse than intermediates did, and debiasing did not help [26]. Teachers ranking their own students reached r = .63 across 75 studies [27]. All three are transfer.

**A probe.** Stating only the first step of each task correlated up to .92 with full tests, cutting test time by a factor of 4.9 and 2.5 [4]; judging proposed steps correlated .71 and .75 with problem solving at a time cut of 3.2 and 3.5 (r1, from [17] and [4]). About 1.2 probing turns raised a model's estimate of a human participant's expertise from 20 % to 69–77 % (r1) or 68.7 % and 76.7 % (r2); on simulated users r1 reports 77–87 % and r3 56–77 % [3]. r1 and r2 class this study direct, r3 transfer, because its ten humans role-played assigned levels; its authors report that overly difficult questions frustrate users. Five-minute GPT-4 elicitation predicted 388 participants' preferences better than their own written prompts in 6 of 10 settings, yes-or-no questions did so in every setting, and effort was rated equal or lower (direct for the dialogue) [28]. The question strategy decided a recommender's profile error (MAE 0.67 against 0.73) while 3.0 to 5.5 pages of questions were rated equally easy [29], and structured interviews ranked among the most effective requirements-elicitation techniques [30] (both transfer). The abstract of a systematic aggregation of 30 elicitation studies neither supports nor contradicts that ranking [91]. Which items are asked decides the effort: 351 new users needed 1.9 pages of ten films to sign up when shown popular ones and 7.0 when shown random and popular ones, with dropout at 11 % against 21 %, and a strategy that picked the most informative films was unusable in the pilot because users could not rate them; accuracy was measured offline alone [89]. In a web survey with question blocks in random order, later questions drew faster, shorter and more uniform answers (abstract) [93]. All three are transfer.

**What to model besides knowledge.** Adaptive systems model goals or tasks, knowledge, background, preferences and interests, and modelling individual traits had shown no benefit by 2001 (consistent only) [31]. Matching stated presentation preferences lacks the required crossover interaction [32]. Naming what matters improves learning of exactly that material: g = 0.54 specific against g = 0.04 general [33], and relevance instructions lowered learning of non-relevant segments [34]; in both the experimenter set the goal. Knowledge is per topic and ages: outside their domain, experts and non-experts searched alike (d < .10), and over three months 31–49 % of non-experts increased their domain vocabulary while 72–76 % of experts' stayed unchanged [25]. These are transfer.

## When an agent should ask, and what asking costs

**Detecting the need.** Four uncertainty detectors reached AUROC 0.37–0.63, and only semantic entropy and simulated intents beat random selection at every budget, doubling its gain at a 10 % budget [8]. Prompted ambiguity identification reached at best 54.25 % accuracy, and chain-of-thought raised one model's calibration error from 28.48 to 45.14 [9]. A gated classifier beat never and always asking by about 20 points of adjusted accuracy [35]. Claude Sonnet 4 missed 44 % of underspecified issues and asked needlessly on 8 % under a neutral prompt, 18 % and 3 % under strong encouragement, and obtained the same information as Qwen 3 Coder with 50 % fewer questions by exploring the repository first [36]. Prompting to ask lowered accuracy by 11.3 %, while a separate confidence step with stated rationale raised it by 22.3 % over that and 12.1 % over not asking [37]. A rule over environment observations predicted the need to ask at 70.4 % against 47.0 % for a prompted model [38]. Labelling by the next turn's outcome gained 5 % answer F1, consistent with single-turn labels training under-asking [39]. On 500 underspecified SWE-bench Verified issues, a separate agent that judged whether to ask resolved 69.4 % with both models tested, against 61.2 % and 61.6 % for a single agent prompted to check, 54.8 % and 55.4 % without asking and 70.8 % and 72.8 % fully specified [98]. One model's intent agent asked on 344 of 500 tasks and resolved the 156 it skipped at 76.9 % against 77.6 % without asking; the other kept flagging most tasks after an instruction to stop. A model trained to ask covered 0.679 of the missing details against 0.12–0.21 untrained, asked a needless question in 3.0 % of fully covered examples, and on clear tasks answered directly in 0.625 and 0.500 of cases where it had done so in all [99]. These are transfer, with a simulated user or none.

On 112 paired data-science tasks, a permissive "ask when needed" policy asked on 33–100 % of fully specified tasks, mostly style fishing (up to 88 %) or out of scope (up to 85 %), with restated information at 0–17 % [10]. The conservative policy cut that to 0–8 %, and GPT-5.4 then asked on 0 % of ambiguous objective tasks and defaulted silently on 100 % (direct). An ask-when-needed prompt for tool-using agents raised one model's rate of correct clarifying questions from 0.52 to 0.90 on instructions with missing information; questions not aimed at the planted ambiguity ran from 0.00 to 9.08 per instruction across eight models and two frameworks, and the paper tests nothing that reduces them (transfer) [94].

Decision theory gives two thresholds: below one the agent does nothing, above the other it acts, between them it asks, and a higher cost of interruption raises the threshold for acting (consistent only) [40]. The value of an answer depends on the consequences of the decision as well as on the probabilities, and the joint value of removing several uncertainties can differ from the sum of their separate values (abstract, consistent only) [101]. The nearest tested rule needed less human help than baselines at a guaranteed success rate: with plan success fixed at 0.76 it asked for help on 58 % of steps against 72 % for the uncalibrated baseline, and prompted self-reports of uncertainty did worst (transfer from robot planning) [41]. An assistant trained to ask about intent only when it pays raised satisfaction by 17.6 % and cut time by 10.4 % for 201 crowd workers, and cut time by 15.6 % against a prompted model that asked unnecessary questions (direct) [42].

**The cost to the person asked.** In 50 participants, the same task cost 3–27 % more time, twice the errors and 31–106 % more annoyance mid-task than at a task boundary [43]. In 16 students, an interruption at a boundary between subtasks drew 56 % less annoyance than at the worst moment inside one and conveyed 43 % more respect for the primary task, with no significant effect on the time on task or on the time to resume it; the interruption was an unrelated news quiz, the boundary lay inside a task, and several interruptions together were not tested [100]. Interruptions of 4.4 s tripled sequence errors and of 2.8 s doubled them [44]. People interrupted every two minutes worked faster (20.3–20.6 against 22.8 min) at unchanged quality, with stress at 9.1–9.5 against 6.9 on a 1–20 scale [45]. Acceptance of clinical reminders fell 30 % per extra reminder per encounter and 10 % per 5 points more repeats [46], and clinicians override 49–96 % of drug safety alerts [47]. Click-through reached 33–70.2 % on SSL warnings, a class known for false alarms, against 10–25 % on malware warnings [48], and attention to warnings fell over a week [49]. These are transfer. Coding-agent users accept 93 % of approval prompts by vendor telemetry [50], and 3 of 16 users read prompts carefully while scoped standing consent passed 41.9 % of calls automatically (direct) [51]. Of 22 people who watched a recorded agent act for them, most accepted its choices between surprises; they avoided more manipulative designs than when acting alone (bad defaults 33.3 % to 80.0 %), mostly by aborting on unexpected behaviour, noticed fewer manipulations off the agent's path, and examined an action almost always when explicitly prompted (direct, no significance tests) [97]. A position paper asserts that agent design wears the overseer down and measures nothing [96]. In one online course, questions before instruction went with more dropout and no overall benefit, as a review reports [86].

**The quality of the question.** Correct bookmarks per page were 0.07 after a low-quality question, 0.73 with none and 1.17 after a high-quality one [6]. Satisfaction was 3.33 with no question or two high-quality ones, and 2.97 after only low-quality ones (r4) or 2.97–3.28 in sessions containing one (r2); a question at every query raised session time from 67 s to about 105 s, and showing only high-quality questions gave the best rate of gain, 0.0331 against 0.0277 [52]. An oracle-chosen question raised MRR from 0.282 to 0.568 (r3) and P@1 by over 170 % (r4), while a learned selector worsened 19 % of facets [53]. A bare "no" answer lowered nDCG@20 by 18.5 % [54]. Specific questions outperformed generic ones [55], engagement depended on the query, question and options [56], and survey completion falls with burdensome questions [57] and with length and difficulty across 25,080 surveys [58]. All are transfer. In 65 users comparing two one-page documents side by side, the one written after three follow-up questions was preferred 41 to 25 (p = .028); once both could be refined the lead was 18 to 13 (p = .155). 84.6 % said they would answer questions for better results, and 60 % disagreed that answering was annoying (direct, a self-selected sample, one session) [95]. A prompt rewritten by a checklist scored above one that asks clarifying questions in an author-run comparison whose checklist prompts already held the details the questions would elicit, so it bears on neither side [87].

## What asking does to the person asked afterwards

In 180 participants deciding six everyday scenarios, answering an assistant's clarifying questions raised satisfaction and lowered scrutiny (direct) [7]. Review time fell from 81.0 s to 60.4 s (d = −0.42) by r3's reading and from 80.7 s to 55.0 s (p = .003) by r4's. Acceptance of erroneous answers rose from 39 % to 52 % (p = .034). A violation in a sub-decision a question had covered was accepted in 60 % against 43 % elsewhere (r3) and reported in 23 % against 37 % (p = .019).

Stating the reason for each data request raised its perceived value but lowered trust and anticipated satisfaction in 493 participants, and most justifications lowered disclosure by more than two standard errors [59]. Explanations attached to an AI's advice did not raise team accuracy and raised acceptance regardless of the advice's correctness [60]. After habituation, a dialog that made the user retype or swipe the decisive field produced 44–74 % informed first responses against at most 20 % for other designs, in 872 participants [61]. For a small favour an empty reason gained compliance as a real one did (.93 and .94 against .60 with none); for a larger one the empty reason did no better than none (.24 and .24), and the real one reached .42 as a trend (.05 < p < .10) [102]. All four are transfer.

## Finding an earlier answer before asking

**Where answers live.** Of 334 information-seeking instances by 17 developers, coworkers were the source in 83 (25 %), and "why was the code implemented this way" stayed unsatisfied in 44 % [11]. Developers holding design documents still asked when they doubted them. In surveys of 157 and 187 developers, 82 % agreed that rationale takes much effort; 51 % of design documents were kept current, and answers from teammates were rarely written down [62]. Engineers chose sources by accessibility in 68 % of 148 reasons and turned to people 40 % of the time [63]. Perceived accessibility of a channel tracked how often engineers used it, and its rated technical quality did not (abstract) [103]. Of 81 architects, 62.9 % said they document design rationale completely; they document constraints (82.7 %) and assumptions (79.0 %) most and trade-offs (49.4 %) and weaknesses (35.8 %) least, and name lack of time or budget as the main barrier (60.5 %) [105]. Programmers in change tasks put 44 types of questions to their code and tools, and in one session a pair took a partial search result for the whole answer [104]. All six are transfer.

**Retrieval at small scale.** On duplicate-question collections of about half a million documents, BM25 reached nDCG@10 0.299 on CQADupStack against 0.153–0.350 for the 2021 dense models and 0.370 with a cross-encoder, and 0.789 on Quora against 0.835–0.854 [64]. Over 719 question-answer pairs a BM25-based engine reached MRR 0.669, and over 1,786 pairs it put the right answer first in 50.4 % and in the top five in 73.4 % of queries [12]. Semantic matching within one project's forum reached 75–100 % precision at a high threshold [65]. On Stack Overflow a combined similarity over title, body, topics and tags put the master of a known duplicate among the top 20 of two million questions in 63.8 % of cases and among the top 5 in 42.3 %; cosine similarity of title and body reached 52.8 % and 38.4 % [107]. Rebuilt by another team on fresh data, the same method reached 43.3 % and 28.2 %, a second method fell from 49.9–66.1 % to 24.7–38.3 %, and recall fell further as the collection grew [106]. A classifier of given pairs labelled four classes of relatedness at 82.10 % precision and 82.28 % recall, and a Jaccard baseline was right on 72.9 % of balanced duplicate pairs [108]. None of the three ran a question that has no earlier answer. All are transfer.

**Agent memory.** ChatGPT answered questions about facts users stated in earlier sessions at 0.577 against 0.918 reading the same history offline, and one memory system answered knowledge updates at 0.208 [66]. BM25 trailed dense retrieval over that history by 9–19 points of Recall@5, and adding extracted facts to the keys lifted it by 5–8 points. Extracted observations beat raw turns in one benchmark (F1 41.4 against 31.7) [67], while the other reports that facts or summaries lose information [66]. Claude 3 Sonnet followed a stated preference after 10 turns in 13.41 % of cases, 99.03 % with a reminder and 92.15 % with retrieval [68]. Multi-turn performance dropped 39 % on average [69]. These are direct.

**What a record must carry.** The value of a rationale field depended on its category and the later activity [70]. About half of GitHub repositories using decision records hold one to five [71]. An LLM applied 1,317 accepted decision records to code with 0.83–0.91 accuracy from their Decision, Context and Consequences, with the Status field excluding superseded records, and 28.26 % of its errors came from context the record left implicit [72]. A person's own earlier answer is itself noisy and ages: 118 people who rated the same 100 films three times disagreed with themselves at RMSE 0.557–0.765, most on mid-scale ratings, and 36 of them at 0.68–0.82 seven months later [90]. All four are transfer.

The page says that SDK callbacks follow the `command` hook's default timeouts and does not say whether a `command` hook that times out blocks the call as well.

A hook that exits with code 2 stops the call before permission rules are evaluated, and a hook's decision does not bypass deny or ask rules [110]. A mod that handles `tool.check` answers after the PreToolUse hooks have run. For `AskUserQuestion` a mod's approval does not skip the prompt. Whether a mod can approve an `AskUserQuestion` call that a hook blocked is `not documented`; the general rule would allow it on a personal login.

## Where the findings pull against each other

**How much to explain to an unknown reader.** Withholding help from a novice cost more (d = 0.505) than redundant help cost an expert (d = −0.428) [16]. An assistant at a proficient or expert level fared well with every user, under-estimation correlated with lower satisfaction on complex tasks (r = −0.29) [18], and models condescended to users marked less educated in 43.74 % of answers [21]. The first measures students' learning, the second model-estimated satisfaction.

**Asking to learn the level, against what asking costs.** About 1.2 probing turns raised expertise accuracy from about 20 % to 69–77 % [3], and a short probe correlates with a full test up to .92 [4]. After clarifying questions people accepted wrong answers at 52 % against 39 % and reviewed for 60.4 s (r3) or 55.0 s (r4) against about 81 s [7]. A poor question left 0.07 correct bookmarks against 0.73 with none [6]. Users preferred a document written after three follow-up questions 41 to 25, and 60 % of them did not find answering annoying [95].

**Self-report against inference.** Global self-ratings correlate .29 with performance [5], while inferred expertise matched self-reported expertise (p = .241) in a study whose ground truth was self-report [19].

**Prompting an agent to ask.** Strong encouragement cut Sonnet 4's missed questions from 44 % to 18 % [36]; prompting to ask lowered clinical accuracy by 11.3 % [37]; a conservative policy produced 100 % silent defaults in one model [10]. A separate agent for the decision to ask reached 69.4 % where a single prompted agent reached 61.2 % [98], and training a model to ask bought coverage of missing details with needless questions on clear tasks [99].

**A stated reason in the question.** The skill's present card states what the answer changes. A stated reason for a request lowered trust and disclosure [59], and an attached explanation raised acceptance of right and wrong advice alike [60]. A stated rationale inside the agent's own decision to ask raised its accuracy by 22.3 % [37]. An empty reason gained compliance with a small request as a real one did and none with a larger one [102].

**When to ask, against asking together.** An interruption at a boundary cost less time and fewer errors in one study [43] and changed only how it felt in another [100]. Neither tested several questions asked together, and the value of a set of answers is not the sum of their single values [101].

**Retrieval and record form.** BM25 matched or beat 2021 dense retrievers on large duplicate-question collections [64] and trailed dense retrieval by 9–19 points over chat history [66]. Extracted observations beat raw turns in one benchmark [67] and lost information in the other [66].

## What the findings do not cover

Each gap is one a researcher names.

- No study tests a question gate or reader intake with an agent's operator; selective against constant asking was compared with real users only in web search [52].
- No opened study counts how many questions to a person already had a written answer [11, 62], and none measures a lookup's false "answered before" rate on a new question [12, 106, 107, 108].
- r4 found no study that tests showing the person asked what his answer changes; the nearest tested a stated reason for a data request [59] and for a favour [102].
- r1 found no study that asks the reader for his own goal before reading [33, 34], and how many probe items suffice is unknown [17]: three items steered an adaptation whose accuracy was not measured [85].
- No opened study tests several questions asked together against the same questions asked apart [43, 100].
- The language-model expertise studies used role-played or self-reported expertise and mostly simulated users [3, 19, 20].
- Many language-model sources carry only an arXiv id, several from 2026, and r1 calls the main expertise-probing study a preprint [3, 7, 10, 19, 72, 51].
- The documentation gives no per-call latency or token cost of hook model calls and no `tool_response` shape for `AskUserQuestion` [13].

## A measurement on one operator's past sessions

The findings above come from other populations. One measurement was made on the skill's own setting: the stored sessions of one operator who works with coding agents.

**Method.** A script read 197 session transcripts from 24 projects on one machine and cut them into 1,142 turns; a turn runs from one message of the operator to his next, and the 128 messages he queued while an agent was working do not split a turn. For each turn it kept the end of the agent's last message and the start of the operator's next one. It flagged a turn end whose last two paragraphs, code removed, hold a sentence that ends in a question mark, and one that holds a block of options with gains and costs. A check of ten extracted records against their transcripts found all ten right. A language model then classified each of the 110 flagged turn ends from those two texts alone, in six batches, one reading each.

| | turns |
|--|--|
| all turns | 1,142 |
| end with a question in prose | 86 (7.5 %) |
| of those, a question put to the operator | 79 |
| a decision among options, or the approval of a stated step | 66 |
| a plan to confirm before starting | 7 |
| a clarification the agent needs to go on | 6 |
| an offer of further work | 0 |

**What the questions were.** Of the 79 questions put to the operator, the classification found 18 (23 %) that the agent could have decided itself: it said which way it would go, and the step was its own to take and to undo. In 28 (35 %) the agent's message already held the answer or said where it stood.

**What the operator did.** He approved without adding anything in 41 cases (52 %), answered with content of his own in 15, picked another option or changed the plan in 10 (13 %), turned to something else in 8, and showed that the question was unnecessary in 4 (5 %); one question closed a session. Of the 18 questions the agent could have decided, he approved 13 and changed the plan in 2. Of the 7 plans put up for confirmation, he changed 2.

**Cards.** The same sessions hold 13 question cards in 7 turns. The hook refused 4 of them, each for the missing reason why the question is asked.

**What this shows and what it does not.** In this operator's sessions a question in prose is rare and is almost always the decision that closes a report; a clarification ended 6 of 1,142 turns, and no turn ended in an offer of further work. A check at the end of a turn would meet 79 questions, of which 18 were the agent's to decide; among those 18 a decision by default would have gone against the operator twice. The bounds are narrow: one operator, whose own instructions forbid offers and ask for a plan to be confirmed; one model as the only rater, reading the last 3,000 characters of a message and the start of the reply; "could have decided" judged from the agent's message alone; a question above the last two paragraphs unseen; a reply the operator queued mid-turn uncounted. The transcripts, the extracted records and their classification stay outside the repository; this section holds counts alone.

## Bearing on the skill's present rules

- `references/explaining.md` § The reader's questions item 3 rests on Wittwer, Nückles & Renkl 2008 [81], opened in full: an estimate of the reader that was too high and one that was too low both taught less than a valid one, and the study never compared the two. The rule states both figures and names neither error as the safer one.
- The rule that questions are batched at the boundary of a task (`references/rules.md` § What the research adds) has two halves. The boundary is measured: fewer errors and less time in one study [43], less annoyance and more perceived respect at unchanged time in another [100]. Asking together is tested in no opened study; it follows from the cost of each switch, and the rule says so.
- The gate proposes and the agent decides (`SKILL.md` § 4). Reported recall of duplicate-question retrieval did not survive a rebuild on fresh data [106], no study measures a false match, and a person's own earlier answer ages [90]; a ledger record is therefore read with its date, and the hit rate of the lookup has to be counted on its own ledgers.
- The gate is a PreToolUse hook. On a login without managed settings an installed mod can approve a call that a hook blocked [110]; the skill's README states this limit.
- Retyping the decisive field raised informed first responses to 74 % against at most 20 % [61]. r4 names this the first research support for the typed answer of rule 18, which the reading-contract note marked as resting on none; it is transfer from security dialogs, and the inhibition delays users who want to proceed.
