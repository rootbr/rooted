# The method this repository adopts, read against its sources and its uses — evidence report

The skill `scientific-method` decides how a statement becomes a rule of this repository: `references/method.md` names four kinds of statement, six standings of a claim, seven rules each with a check question, and one acceptance path. Before changing it, the owner of the repository wanted three things settled. Is the formulation sound and complete — what in it is missing, wrong or stricter than it needs to be? Does it fit tasks beyond admitting a rule to an agent skill — engineering and design decisions, debugging, the evaluation of prompts and agents, product experiments, the appraisal of someone else's research, decisions under uncertainty — and where does another procedure take over? And do the texts it cites say what it takes from them? This report compiles what twelve researchers found on those questions, one cluster of a question map each.

What the readings yield:

- The sentence that the seven rules "hold what the main lines of the philosophy of science share" is not supported by the passages cited for it and is disputed rule by rule: the cited entry reports the loss of consensus and then two answers that each keep a positive core, the one the provenance map cites among them, and the same line of `method.md` already calls the rules a convention. Bayesians set acceptance aside, and the sentences behind rules 3 and 6 each take one side of a live dispute without naming it; Kuhn's and Lakatos's rejection of refutation concerns paradigms and research programmes, where rule 7 withdraws single claims (F1.1, F1.2, F1.3, F4.5, F6.8, F10.11, F12.5; appraisal SRC1, SRC12, LOG2).
- `method.md` names no test and no error rate for telling an effect from chance. Rule 3's own good example, nine of ten runs against four of ten, passes a one-sided exact test (p = 0.0286) and misses the two-sided 5% level (p = 0.0573), with a power of 0.66 at ten runs per arm; the threshold of H-03, the teaching example of the record format, lets a technique with no effect pass about two times in five. The figures are the researcher's computation, recomputed by the session and by the appraisal (`research/scientific-method-review/rule3_stats.py` prints them); no false-pass rate was computed for the repository's seven real tests. The files ask that the result of a statistical claim carry its test and its error rates (F5.4, F5.5, F4.4, F5.3, F2.8, F3.4; appraisal ASM1, ASM2, EV2, LOG16).
- A failed test bears on more than the claim. The claim carries its measure, comparison and scope, and rule 7 withdraws it on a failure repeated within the same bounds; yet the acceptance path sends "failed repeatably" to "refuted" with no check of any auxiliary, and the record has no field for a grader or for the check an auxiliary passed. In model evaluation the prompt, the harness and the grader move scores by more than 20% in some cases (F1.8, F3.1, F3.2; appraisal ASM4 as debated, EV16).
- No check question asks whether a measure measures what the claim is about: a definition used as a measure carries an untested validity claim, and 53.4% of the benchmark articles one review surveyed presented evidence for the construct validity of their measure. A reader of surface form alone reproduces 55%–67% of the labels of five public label sets, human votes among them, with no chance baseline given (F2.5, F5.1, F2.7, F11.3, F11.4; appraisal SRC2). The appraisal leaves this conclusion unchanged.
- The clause "whoever its author" sets aside a predictor for an external test: industry-sponsored studies report favourable results more often (RR 1.27), an effect that by its source's own discussion runs partly through choices a reading can see. Model evaluators favour their own outputs and models of one company share their errors, so a reader that saw nothing of the work but shares the author's model family is not shown to be independent in its errors; the evidence is on capability benchmarks, and carrying it to a reader of records is a transfer (F4.7, F7.5, F11.8, F11.6, F11.1, F11.2, F11.5; appraisal ACH7, EV17). F10.10 keeps the equal-origins clause for the origin of hypotheses.
- Rule 4 admits an external test once its design is in view. In replication projects of the social sciences about half of the significant original effects reached significance again (55.1% of 274 claims in the SCORE project); whether those studies would pass rule 4 is unmeasured, and a failed replication does not show the original false, so the figure is a transfer and not the error rate of the rule. A fabricated trial can show the same view, and for most studies part of the test is not in view at all; a reading still yields a forecast and a consistency check (F7.1, F7.2, F7.4, F7.13, F7.3; appraisal LOG6, ACH8, EV4, BEN4).
- Support can outlive the model it was obtained on: a service name switches versions within months, and models are retired after one to two years, so a record ages whether or not its test is rerun. That a second failure "can rarely occur" does not follow from retirement. A review term is the researcher's proposal (F3.12, F8.1, F11.12, F8.2, F8.8; appraisal RED5, SRC7).
- Decision theory, GRADE and the law keep the strength of evidence apart from the decision to act, and set the bar for acting by the cost of an error, reversibility and the value of information. Whether the acceptance path of `method.md` joins the two is disputed between files; no case of harm from the join is given, and one trace shows a rule in force without standing (F4.9, F9.1, F9.2, F12.7, F12.9; appraisal ASM6, EV15).
- A claim about one event — a diagnosis, the cause of an outage, the effect of a fix — is judged by traces that discriminate among named rivals. By the session's reading of `method.md`, such a claim meets rule 2's comparison and the repetitions of rules 3 and 4 only when a deterministic reproducer exists, while rules 1 and 2 and the log of rule 6 carry over. How strong such an inference is remains disputed, and the gap is a defect only while the method keeps its claim to apply to every statement alike (F10.1, F10.2, F10.3, F10.4, F5.12; appraisal ASM20, EV19).
- Pre-recording in rule 6's sources is a public, dated statement, while the method's is a commit in the repository's history. Whether a party the author does not control holds those commits was not checked, and the registries the standard comes from are not public or frozen without exception. Author-designed registration did not show a fall in positive results where result-blind review showed a large one, a contrast that carries a selection confound; the run log needs the harness, not the author's diligence (F6.1, F6.2, F3.13, F6.3; appraisal ACH10, BEN1, LOG15).
- Applied to itself, the method has 10 claim records, of which 7 were tested and 6 stand as supported, all six on the repository's own tests; 1,326 rule lines predate the method and carry no record. The record check lets a hypothesis with one failed run be deleted without a finding, which the session confirmed by a run of the check on a throwaway repository (F12.1, F12.2, F12.3; appraisal LOG11, SRC3).
- The provenance map does not evidence derivation: one commit changed the source of a definition and left the definition unchanged, which is one line of the map and the researcher's inference (F1.12; appraisal LOG9). Rule 6 restates de Groot (1956), whom it does not cite (one source, F6.6); and a verbatim hit shows the words are there, not that they support the line (F1.11).

[Sources](#sources) holds 242 entries merged across the twelve findings files; each researcher opened its sources and marked how each was seen (full text, abstract, or secondary through another text), and the compiler of this report re-opened none of them and took every quoted word from the findings files.

The report was appraised by seven critique methods, an adjudicator and one debate (`research/scientific-method-review/appraisal.md`, with the rejected findings beside it). The verdict: weakened, with medium confidence in its overall direction; eleven of the twelve main conclusions are weakened, the one on measure validity is unchanged, none is unsupported, and no critical finding stands. The twelve bullets above are restated as the appraisal asks, each naming the appraisal findings it answers; the sections from [What was investigated and why](#what-was-investigated-and-why) on stand as the researchers and the compiler wrote them, so where a section and a bullet differ, the bullet is the later reading. Two findings of the appraisal bear on everything below. Each quote and each verdict rests on one reading: the researchers and the authors of the question map were instances of Claude Opus 5.5 at effort high, the compiler one at effort xhigh, and no reader of another model family and no person re-read the quotes in context. And the standing label of a finding counts its sources, not their strength, so support from a single preprint wears the same label as support from two reviews.

## What was investigated and why

The method governs what may enter a skill as a rule, and the owner is about to decide whether to change it. Three decisions hang on the review. The first is whether the formulation is sound and complete, judged rule by rule. The second is how far it reaches: the repository already uses it for skill rules, and the question is whether the same path fits engineering and design decisions, debugging, the evaluation of prompts and agents, product experiments, the appraisal of another's research and decisions under uncertainty, or whether some other procedure has to take those over. The third is fidelity: the method holds others to opening a source before relying on it, so its own provenance map has to meet the same standard.

The question map that started the work asked, among its ten frontier questions, whether the main lines of the philosophy of science share a core at all, whether the method should be put to its own test, where a claim's standing ends and the decision to act begins, what "refuted" picks out when a test indicts a bundle, whether a test run, judged and written by language models is still a test, whether the rules catch a measure that measures the wrong thing, whether singular claims need a second path, what opening a source is worth in 2026, whether the cited texts say what the method takes from them, and whether a rule should re-earn its standing as models are retired. Each frontier question is answered in the clusters below; the files cite them as "frontier 1" to "frontier 10".

## Method

The question map. Nine question-generation methods — structural decomposition, perspective shifting, inversion, causal chains, temporal and scale, hidden assumptions, field intelligence, adjacent disciplines and meta-questions — produced 345 questions. A coverage audit added 22 and flagged 151 duplicate pairs among the 345; a synthesis merged the duplicates and near-duplicates into 214 questions in 12 clusters, with ten frontier questions and a list of open terrain (`research/scientific-method-review/question-map.md`).

The research. Twelve researchers took one cluster each and wrote one findings file, `research/scientific-method-review/findings/NN-<slug>.md`; the files hold 148 findings (12, 13, 13, 12, 12, 13, 13, 10, 11, 13, 14 and 12 in the order of the clusters). Each finding names the questions it answers, its evidence with author, URL and how the source was seen, a standing in a fixed vocabulary — `two or more independent sources`, `one source`, `disputed`, `my inference` — and a line "For the method" that the file marks as the researcher's own inference and opens with a verdict: keeps, missing, wrong, over-strict, the text says otherwise, or needs another procedure. Each researcher was to check every quote against the downloaded source; the downloads sit in the `scratch-NN/` directories, which the compiler did not open.

The quote check. A script compared the 51 citations of the method's provenance map and README with their sources. Its input is `maintenance/method-citations.json` of the skill `scientific-method`; its result on 2026-11-21 records all 51 as `found`, two of them (M-7/3 and M-7/5) differing only in letter case. File 01 reports the same check as 49 `found` and 2 `near` by punctuation and notes that line M-A changed during its run, so the two counts belong to different states of the check; the compiler did not run the script again. The revision dates of the SEP entries, listed in the preamble of `maintenance/method-provenance.md`, agree with those the findings files give for every entry both name.

The compilation. The compiler read `method.md`, the twelve findings files whole, the map's overview, frontier questions and open terrain, the quote check and the SEP version list, and opened no source. Findings of different clusters that say the same thing are reported together in the section of the earlier cluster, and the later section points there: F3.1, F4.5 and F6.8 are reported in cluster 1, F5.1 in cluster 2, F6.3, F8.1 and F11.12 in cluster 3, F5.3, F7.5, F9.1, F9.2, F11.6 and F11.8 in cluster 4, F6.9 in cluster 5, F10.12 and F12.6 in cluster 9, and F12.8 in cluster 10. Every statement below traces to a finding id; the kind column of [Candidate changes](#candidate-changes) and the grouping of the open questions are the compiler's reading. By the method's own table, as F12.12 reads it, a model-generated question map is an origin of hypotheses, not a finding; the compiler reads the researchers' "For the method" lines the same way, as inferences the files mark as such.

Limits. A large share of the sources were seen as an abstract only, and the files say so entry by entry. Several primary texts could not be opened (see [Open questions](#open-questions)). The map names non-Anglophone methodology of science, agents watched in real sessions, and work after November 2026 as open terrain; in the compiler's reading no findings file takes up the first two.

## Cluster 1 — Philosophical lineage and fidelity to the cited texts

The cited texts support calling the seven rules a convention and contradict calling them what the main lines share: the passages cited for the opening report no consensus, and named lines reject named rules (F1.1, F1.2). Several rules take one side of a live dispute without saying so — on prediction against accommodation, on the stopping rule, on what a failed test indicts — and some cited formulations say otherwise than the rule they back (F1.3 to F1.9). Most citations carry their line, but a verbatim hit is not support, and the map records concordance rather than derivation (F1.10 to F1.12).

F1.1. The passages cited for the method's opening deny a shared core, so the sentence of `method.md` "They hold what the main lines of the philosophy of science share" is contradicted by its own sources (two or more independent sources). Hoyningen-Huene in SEP "Scientific Method" §7, the paragraph cited at M-0/2: "there is no longer any consensus among philosophers and historians of science about the nature of science" and "what has been left to characterize science is only its fallibility"; the same entry, §1: "The lack of a singular method across all disciplines of science is a reason for its reliability as a practice, not a mark against it." SEP "Science and Pseudo-Science" §8, cited at M-0/3–4: "Philosophers and other theoreticians of science differ widely in their views on what science is." and "almost complete disagreement on the general criteria that these judgments should presumably be based upon". For the method, the researcher infers (wrong): these passages carry the claim that the rules are a convention and contradict the claim that the lines share them, since the agreement they report is on particular verdicts and the seven rules are general criteria; the method should present the rules as a reasoned choice among the lines.

F1.2. Named lines reject named rules: Kuhn and Lakatos reject refutation as what withdraws a theory (rule 7), Bayesians set acceptance aside (the standings) and hold the stopping plan irrelevant (rule 3) (two or more independent sources). SEP "Thomas Kuhn": "Nor do they regard anomalous results as falsifying those theories." and "Kuhn’s account argues that resisting falsification is precisely what every disciplinary matrix in science does." SEP "Imre Lakatos" §2.2, cited at M-7/5: "All theories, in this sense, are born refuted and die refuted.", then "the unit of scientific evaluation is no longer the individual theory (as with Popper), but the sequence of theories, the research programme". SEP "Bayesian Epistemology", on whether a credence suffices for believing or accepting: "But to many Bayesians, that issue seems only secondary, or better forgone as argued by Jeffrey (1970)." SEP "Philosophy of Statistics" §3.2.2: "Some will find this dependence unacceptable: the intentions and plans of the researcher are irrelevant to the evidential value of the data." For the method, the researcher infers (the text says otherwise): M-7 cites Lakatos for revision while the passage denies that refutation settles a theory's fate, and rule 3's "decided before the test" takes the error-statistical side of the stopping-rule dispute; both should be recorded as choices.

F4.5, reported here from cluster 4, finds the same dispute behind rule 6, and adds a position between the sides (standing: disputed — error statisticians hold that the protocol and the stopping rule change what the data show, likelihoodists and subjective Bayesians that only the likelihoods count, and the likelihood principle separates them). Romeijn, SEP "Philosophy of Statistics" §3.2.3, "Excursion: optional stopping", gives Mayo's side, "the impact of data on the hypotheses should depend on the stopping rule or protocol that is followed in obtaining it", and the other, "the likelihoods of hypotheses for the observed data completely fix the evidential impact of those data on the hypotheses". Gelman and Shalizi (2013), §1 and §4.4: "crucial parts of Bayesian data analysis, such as model checking, can be understood as “error probes” in Mayo’s sense" and "falsification is about plots and predictive checks, not about Bayes factors or posterior probabilities of candidate models". For the method, the researcher infers (the text says otherwise): under error statistics "recorded before the test" bears on the evidence, under the likelihood principle it only guards against fitting, so a Bayes-factor reading of a test stopped early could give a different standing, and `method.md` should name the side it takes. F1.2 reads the choice in rule 3, F4.5 in rule 6.

F1.3. Rule 6's ground — that a later observation tests the hypothesis and the observation it grew from does not — is predictivism, which is disputed, and Mill, whom the map cites for "hypothesis", rejects it (standing: disputed — predictivists such as Popper, Lakatos and the use-novelty view hold that a prediction outweighs an accommodation; Mill and Keynes, and Howson as SEP reports him, hold that timing adds nothing once prior grounds are counted; in the reform debate Devezer et al. deny that preregistration has statistical force and Wagenmakers affirms it). SEP "Prediction versus Accommodation" §1 and §7: Mill "categorically denied this claim"; Keynes: "The peculiar virtue of prediction or predesignation is altogether imaginary". Mill, A System of Logic, 7th ed. (1868), III.xiv §6: "Such predictions and their fulfilment are, indeed, well calculated to impress the uninformed" and "Though twenty such coincidences should occur, they would not prove the reality of the undulatory ether". Devezer et al. (2021): "Statistical rigor can neither be ensured by preregistration nor would be compromised by not preregistering a plan." Wagenmakers (2021, author's blog revised 2021-03-25): "it is possible to provide a statistical underpinning for the idea that data-dredging differs from foresight, both in the frequentist and in the Bayesian paradigm". For the method, the researcher infers (keeps rule 6, on another ground): rule 6 stands as a guard against selective reporting (Fisher through Mayo at M-6/6; HARKing at M-6/4), and its sentence on origin and test should be recorded as the repository's choice on a disputed point.

F6.8, reported here from cluster 6, calls the same sentence a use-novelty rule whose necessity is disputed and which serves severity (standing: disputed — that inspiring data cannot test, with de Groot and Simmons et al., against reuse being valid when inference conditions on the selection, with Mayo as SEP reports her, Devezer et al. and Rubin, the latter side needing the selection stated formally). Barnes, SEP "Prediction versus Accommodation" §6.4: "Here novelty and severity appear to coincide—but Mayo observes that there are cases in which they come apart." Devezer et al. (2021) §3: "one can use the data many times in a valid statistical procedure" and "The problem arises if proper conditioning on prior information or decisions is skipped." Rubin (2020): the time at which hypotheses are deduced from a priori theory "does not affect the credibility of hypothesis tests". Against: Simmons et al. (2020), "will almost inevitably end in p-hacking", and de Groot (F6.6). For the method, the researcher infers (over-strict): cheaply so where fresh runs are cheap, as in agent tests, and costly for one-off incidents and appraisal; rule 6 could admit a post-hoc claim on its own data when a procedure accounts for the selection (conditioning, a held-out set), and let rule 3 decide.

F1.4. Mill's chapter carries the test condition, but in context his "hypothesis" admits what the method calls opinion, and his "proof" is stronger than "supported" (standing: one source, with the edition variant seen in a second). For the method, the researcher infers (the text says otherwise): Mill defines a hypothesis by insufficient evidence, not by a named refuting outcome, and his proof excludes every alternative (the Method of Difference) and yields an "inductive truth", where rule 7 leaves every standing open.

F1.5. The "National Academy of Sciences" definitions are a course page's adaptation, and its hypothesis and law contradict rule 7 and the table of standings (standing: one source). ## Cluster 2 — Kinds of statement and the logical form of claims

The four kinds hold for the repository's own examples, but several kinds of claim have no place among them or in rule 2: statistical claims as against universal ones, existential claims, estimates and claims about a model settled by proof, and mixed statements that need splitting before they are sorted (F2.4, F2.8 to F2.11). A definition that serves as a measure, and a fact read off an instrument, each carry a validity claim the method lets pass as agreement, and no check question asks for it (F2.5 with F5.1, F2.7). The repository's own agreement test on the kinds bounds less than its claim (F2.12).

F2.1. "Exit status zero means success" is a constitutive convention: the standard defines what counts as success, and whether a program conforms is a claim (two or more independent sources). The Open Group, POSIX.1-2024, Shell Command Language, the `break` utility: "EXIT STATUS 0 Successful completion." Rescorla, SEP "Convention", on Marmor and Searle: "a constitutive convention helps “constitute” a social practice, in the sense that it helps define what the practice is and how to engage in it correctly". For the method, the researcher infers (missing): the norm row should tell a constitutive norm from a regulative one, and "this tool conforms" is a claim (S-3's one-run test).

F2.2. Prescribed practices are often found to work no better; evidence grading keeps evidence and recommendation apart, but the method does not say which governs action when they conflict (two or more independent sources for the separation; which entry governs is my inference). Prasad et al. (2013), "A decade of reversal", abstract: "Of the 363 articles testing standard of care, 146 (40.2%) reversed that practice". Guyatt et al. (2008), "GRADE: an emerging consensus…": "High quality evidence doesn’t necessarily imply strong recommendations, and strong recommendations can arise from low quality evidence." ### Decision 2 — how far does the method reach?

Engineering and design decisions. The decision whose hypothesis waits fits an architecture decision record (F9.6, F10.12), with a link from a refuted premise back to the decision missing (F9.6, F3.11) and no place for several owners (F9.5). For engineering and design assurance another procedure takes over: assurance cases with defeaters and a named post that accepts the risk (F10.9, F12.8). The technical documentation of a high-risk AI system is a procedure of its own (F8.9).

Debugging and incidents. For incident response another procedure takes over: mitigation first, tests by likelihood and risk, and a log, with rules 1, 2 and the log of rule 6 carrying over (F10.2). A singular claim needs a second path (F10.1, F10.3) until a deterministic reproducer returns it to the ordinary one (F10.4); a diagnosis among rival causes is judged by a competing-hypotheses matrix (F5.12). Rule 6's ban on testing with the inspiring data is costly here (F6.8).

Evaluation of prompts and agents. ## Where the findings disagree

Each entry gives both sides as the files state them. Disputes between sources that a single finding reports are listed after the disputes between files.

Between files:

- D1. Where the provenance departs from its texts. F1.10: the departures are confined to M-0/1–4, M-2/4, M-S/1–3, M-4/5–7, M-7/4–5 and M-A/2, and M-7/3 carries Popper's reproducible effect. Against it: F3.3, the count of two failures at M-7 is not Popper's; F6.1 and F6.6, M-6 drops "public" and "dated" and leaves de Groot uncited; F9.3, M-3 cites one side of the Fisher–Neyman–Pearson contrast.
- D2. Whether the observation a hypothesis grew from may test it. F1.3 keeps rule 6 on another ground, as a guard against selective reporting, and asks that the sentence be recorded as a choice. F6.8 calls the sentence over-strict and would admit a post-hoc claim when a procedure accounts for the selection.
- D3. The stopping rule. F1.2 and F4.5 read rules 3 and 6 as taking the error-statistical side against the likelihood principle, which they ask to be named. F5.8 and F6.9 take that side as given and ask that the stopping rule be recorded.
- D4. What pre-recording buys. F1.3: Devezer et al. deny it statistical force, Wagenmakers affirms it. F6.2: author-designed registration showed no fall in positive results, result-blind review a large one. F6.11: Szollosi et al. call it redundant under good theory, Simmons et al. and Nelson et al. needed for transparency. F6.12: Simonsohn holds dropped whole studies rare and disclosure enough, the registered-report comparison points to large underreporting, the units differing.
- D5. What withdraws a theory. Rule 7 and F1.10 follow Popper's reproducible effect. F1.2 reports Kuhn and Lakatos rejecting refutation as what withdraws a theory, and F3.8 Lakatos holding it a mistake to give up a progressive programme with no better one in view.
- D6. How many failures refute. F1.5 (the course page) and F1.9 (Feynman) refute on one failure, against rule 7's wait. F3.3 finds no count of two in Popper. F3.4 would count no failures but pool runs until they exclude the effect, while F3.5 would let a rule with a costly failure leave force at the first one.
- D7. Which severity quantity the record carries. F4.4: power at the threshold or an interval. F5.3: Mayo separates pre-data power from severity judged on the data obtained, and the record should hold the latter.
- D8. What validates a model judge. F11.3, F11.4 and F7.8: agreement with human labels or labels made outside the models, with a known error rate. F2.7 and F5.1: agreement with people is reliability, not validity, and judges with high agreement can track surface form. Within F11.3, Zheng et al. find judges matching humans on chat preference and Bavaresco et al. a wide variance over 20 tasks.
- D9. Equal origins. F10.10 keeps the clause: a track record can order hypotheses but tests no claim. F4.7, F7.5, F11.8 and F11.6: who ran and paid for a test predicts its result, and a model that is origin and judge is not one origin among equals. F4.7 itself holds the clause for where a hypothesis comes from and denies it only for weighing a test.
- D10. Standing and force. F4.9, F9.1 and F9.2: the acceptance path joins a claim's standing to a rule's force and should split them. F12.7: the decision in force while its hypothesis waits already plays GRADE's recommendation on low certainty, so the method has that split and lacks only the checks for reporting bias. F9.6 and F10.12 keep the decision whose hypothesis waits.
- D11. Publication bias in appraisal. F7.6 and F12.7: appraisal needs a body-level step that checks publication or reporting bias and inconsistency. F7.12: a publication-bias correction cannot be a condition of accepting an opened test, since the methods do not substantively improve the results; the defence sits before the result.
- D12. Thresholds fixed beforehand. F2.8, F5.5 and F9.11: a rejection region, a smallest effect of interest or a harm margin fixed before the test. F5.6: where no theory supplies one, such thresholds are arbitrary, and a baseless one should be marked as the owner's decision.
- D13. A record whose model is retired. F3.12: the rule stands as a decision, its hypothesis waiting for a retest. F8.1: `fit` turns to `transfer` until a rerun. F11.12: a test on the successor is a new test, marked as a transfer.
- D14. Rule 4's "see the same" and "a rerun confirms". F8.8: over-strict, since no sampled run gives the same result, and a rerun should confirm within a recorded tolerance. F7.10: wrong the other way, since a rerun confirms the run, not the claim, and a biased instrument reruns as well.
- D15. Expert judgement. F11.10: "opinion: stays out" files expert judgement with a guess, though it decides whether evidence applies and is testable. F10.10: counting a track record as evidence would let origin stand in for a test.
- D16. The remedy for line 16. F1.1 and F10.11: present the rules as a reasoned choice, the owner's decision. F12.5: line 16 and H-01 are claims by the method's own table and should be tested against a before/after arm.
- D17. What a reading of a paper is worth. F7.3: readers forecast replication with success rates of 76% and 78% (a secondary report) and can recompute reported statistics. F2.13: agreement between experts on an overall risk-of-bias judgement is slight. F7.1: reading summary results misses most fabricated data.

Within single findings, between sources:

- D18. Self-preference of model evaluators: bias on subjective summaries (Panickssery et al.) against earned quality on verifiable tasks (Chen et al.); both find a harmful part (F11.6).
- D19. Inductive risk: Rudner and Douglas make the acceptable risk a value judgement; Jeffrey and Levi keep values out of acceptance (F4.9, F9.4).

- D21. Thick terms: Hare and Blackburn hold them separable into evaluation and description; Williams, Putnam and Kirchin hold them fused (F2.6).
- D22. Inference about one past event: Cleland holds testing by smoking guns not inferior; Turner holds it inferior because rivals stay underdetermined more often (F10.1).
- D23. Adaptivity: Dwork et al. and Singh et al. find adaptive reuse and selective disclosure inflating scores; Recht et al. find the ImageNet drop caused by harder images, not adaptivity (F10.8).
- D24. Context and replication: Van Bavel et al. find contextual sensitivity associated with replication success after adjustment; Inbar's letter, not opened, attributes it to subdiscipline (F3.9).
- D25. Exploration: sources agree that exploration must be labelled; Devezer et al. doubt that any minimum standard fits all exploratory studies, and Scheel et al. call the confirmatory–exploratory distinction a false dichotomy (F6.7).
- D26. Testing a methodology: Laudan holds a methodological rule a means–ends claim open to test; Knowles holds such rules not rules; Lakatos judges a methodology by the history of science (F12.5).
- D27. The experimenter's regress: Collins holds it broken by negotiation, Franklin by strategies of calibration (F11.4).
- D28. Catastrophic and irreversible outcomes: expected utility for every risk against precautionary or regret rules under severe uncertainty (F9.10).
- D29. Generalizability: Yarkoni's critique and remedy against the commentary "There is no generalizability crisis", seen only in a search listing (F8.4).
