# Research note — Component cohesion and change coupling

Topic `component-cohesion-and-change-coupling` of group K. Architecture, domain `architecture`, id prefix `ARCH`. Formulation locators: Clean Architecture ch. 13 'Component Cohesion' (REP, CCP, CRP) and ch. 12 'Components'; The Pragmatic Programmer 20th ed. Topic 10 'Orthogonality'; SWEBOK Guide V4.0 ch. 2 'Software Architecture' and ch. 3 'Software Design' (coupling and cohesion, modularity); ISO/IEC 25010 'modularity'.

The canon puts component cohesion at the level of release, build and deployment units on the three principles of Clean Architecture ch. 13. REP says the granule of reuse is the granule of release. CCP says to gather what changes for the same reasons and at the same times. CRP says not to force a component's users to depend on what they do not use. The book itself holds the three in tension. The canon adds the Pragmatic Programmer's orthogonality test and the SWEBOK and ISO/IEC 25010 definitions of coupling, cohesion and modularity. The evidence supports seven cards. Official documentation carries the release half of REP in two cards. The first says a released version is an immutable snapshot (SemVer item 3, Go Modules Reference). The second says a published component reuses only released versions: Cargo rejects path-only dependencies, npm advises against local paths in published packages, and Go replace and Cargo patch directives are ignored outside the main module or workspace root. The split criterion comes from the Go module documentation: a separately versioned component only where the code is versioned independently, or where a client API needs fewer dependencies than the rest. It is contested. One side is the release cost of many units (Go wiki, SemVer version lock). The other side is the cost of clients carrying dependencies they do not use (Soto-Valero et al.: 75.1% of 723,444 Maven dependency relationships bloated). The Go wiki states the condition that separates the two sides. Research carries CCP at the component level. Co-changes that span subsystems go with more bugs than co-changes inside one (Kouroshfar, ICSE 2013). Anti-patterns detected from structure plus history go with error- and change-proneness across 19 projects (Mo et al., TSE 2021). Dependency structure alone misrepresents co-change (Geipel and Schweitzer, TSE 2012). Four studies qualify the co-change rule. Palomba et al. show co-changing variants of one role that correctly sit apart, Herzig and Zeller find up to 15% of bug fixes tangled, Kirbas et al. find the defect correlation varies, and Beck and Diehl, as relayed, find structure clusters better than history. The co-change card therefore ships with one shared reason for change as its separating condition. CRP at the build level rests on three sources. Gradle and CMake documentation say to expose a dependency to consumers only when its types appear in the public API. Maven documentation, with Soto-Valero's measurement that 61.8% of inherited dependencies go unused, supports the parent-inheritance card. The Go blog and the Google Go style guide argue against catch-all util and common packages. Held as not checkable: the tension diagram, the maturity shift, the top-down claim, the cohesive-group half of REP, the definitional SWEBOK and ISO principle, and the history-wide co-change analysis. These feed the Limits and Validators of the cards. Pending: divergent change at the component level, and Dahan's warning against extracting coincidentally similar code into a shared library, both without research. Ownership notes for the orchestrator follow. The api-versus-implementation, parent-inheritance and catch-all cards border the boundary-enforcement-and-package-structure topic, whose spine fetched the same Gradle, CMake and Go pages. The stable-public-dependencies rule (Rust C-STABLE) is left to the stable-dependencies-and-abstractness topic, which already drafts it. Declared-boundary checks (ArchUnit, import-linter, Packwerk, Spring Modulith) and cycle rules belong to the neighbouring topics.

## Formulation layer

The canon puts the component level on three cohesion principles. Clean Architecture ch. 13 names them REP (the granule of reuse is the granule of release), CCP (gather the classes that change for the same reasons and at the same times) and CRP (do not force a component's users to depend on classes they do not use), and holds them in tension, so a project satisfies two and gives up the third. The same idea appears in other formulations. Martin's 97-Things SRP essay applies it to independently deployable components. The Pragmatic Programmer's orthogonality test asks how many modules change when one requirement changes, and it measures fix locality from version control. SWEBOK V3/V4 define coupling and cohesion and recommend loose coupling and maximal cohesion, and ISO/IEC 25010 states modularity as limiting the effect of a change to one component. The 'unit of release' rule has fetched official sources: the Go Modules Reference, the Go module docs and wiki, Semantic Versioning, the Rust book on workspaces and the PyPA glossary. Co-change mined from history, and checks between components, are covered by fetched tool docs: code-maat logical coupling, ArchUnit slices and modules, and the import-linter independence contract. The book texts of Clean Architecture, The Pragmatic Programmer, Refactoring and A Philosophy of Software Design could not be opened. They appear as WebSearch summaries (relayed) or as paraphrases from memory, and the six-search cap left the candidate papers (Gall 1998 and the others) unsearched in this layer. Two disagreements are recorded. Udi Dahan's 'Beware the Share' warns that extracting coincidentally similar code into a shared library couples components that used to be independent, which pulls against REP's reuse orientation. The Google One-Version Rule removes per-component internal versioning, which differs from REP and SemVer. The Ousterhout–Martin discussion disagrees about entanglement only at method level.

## Reception layer

At the component level, industry practice enforces declared boundaries mechanically: tools check declared dependencies, exposed API packages, cycle-free slices, and unused or undeclared build dependencies. The defaults vary by tool. Packwerk is opt-in per package. Maven analyze only warns by default (failOnWarning false). dependency-cruiser's no-circular has severity error in its recommended set. Co-change mined from history is documented as a working tool practice (Code Maat defaults: 5 revisions, 5 shared revisions, 30% degree, changesets over 30 modules excluded; CodeScene 2.4.2 as relayed: 10/10/50%/50 files). The Limits are shaped by tangled commits (Herzig and Zeller, relayed: up to 15% of bug fixes tangled) and by cross-commit and cross-repository aggregation. Three named positions contest or qualify the canon. Google's One Version Rule relies on one version per dependency rather than separately released versions, which bears on REP. The Go modules documentation recommends a single module per repository for maintenance simplicity, which pulls against fine-grained components. Sam Newman holds that lockstep releases can come from the process (release trains) rather than from wrong boundaries. The authors of the Shopify Packwerk retrospective report conflicting views on static boundary enforcement. Two items record the canon's own tension: Martin's REP/CCP/CRP tension diagram (unfetched), and a practitioner blog, relayed, that calls CCP and REP directly contradictory. I could not open the Packwerk retrospective, CodeScene's documentation, InfoQ, or Software Engineering at Google. Their statements are relayed from search summaries. I did not search the candidate evidence papers (Gall, Zimmermann, D'Ambros, Mo, Kazman, MacCormack) because the search budget went to reception, so the evidence layer must cover them. A search for criticism aimed specifically at CCP/CRP in microservices found none.

## Evidence layer

Co-change that crosses a component boundary is the best-supported rule of the topic. Fetched abstracts back it: co-changes across subsystems carry more bugs than co-changes within one (ICSE 2013), architecture anti-patterns detected from structure plus history predict error- and change-proneness in 19 projects (TSE 2021), and dependency structure alone misrepresents co-change (TSE 2012). Gall 1998, Zimmermann 2005, D'Ambros 2009 and MacCormack 2006 came only through WebSearch summaries; the D'Ambros summary gives the study's question but not its findings. Common reuse at the component level rests on the Soto-Valero studies, opened from a KTH thesis PDF on GitHub: 75.1% of 723,444 Maven dependency relationships are bloated, and 68.3% of library bytecode can be removed for a client workload. The Go modules documentation and wiki state the component-split criterion: a separate module where the code is versioned independently, or where a client API needs a smaller dependency set. Release-and-version-as-one-unit rests on SemVer (fetched) and the Go module definition (fetched); how far releases keep the promise is disputed, with Raemaekers et al. finding breaking changes widespread and Ochoa et al. 2022 finding 83.4% of 119,879 upgrades semver-compliant. Co-change as a grouping criterion is also disputed. Beck and Diehl 2013 found structural clustering beat evolutionary clustering. Palomba et al. show a co-change pair whose co-location would create a Blob. Kirbas et al. 2017 found that the strength of the correlation between evolutionary coupling and defects varies. SWEBOK V4, the ISO/IEC 25010 text itself, MacCormack's findings and the trivial-package and remodularization studies remain unfetched or relayed. None of them should anchor a card alone.

## Spine — candidate rules in source order

1. **A component is a unit of release, build or deployment, such as a module, crate, distribution package or service, and a well-designed component stays independently deployable** — A component is the smallest entity deployed as part of a system, and well-designed components keep the ability to be deployed, and therefore developed, independently. Toolchains define the unit the same way. A Go module is a collection of packages released, versioned and distributed together. A Rust crate is a unit of compilation and linking as well as of versioning, distribution and runtime loading. A Python distribution package is the versioned archive that distributes a release.

2. **A component version once released is never changed, so a release script or workflow does not move, delete or re-publish an existing version tag or artifact and any change ships as a new version** — Once a version of a component is released, its contents are not modified, and any modification is released as a new version.
