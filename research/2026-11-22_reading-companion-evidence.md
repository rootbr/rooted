# What the reading methodology of reading-companion rests on — sources re-opened and evidence found to 2026-11-22

The skill reading-companion helps an adult work through a book: it sets up a workspace and then runs as a companion that points, checks and explains while the reader does the retrieval himself. Its rules follow from the reading methodology in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/reading-companion/references/reading-methodology.md`, and each claim behind a rule has a line in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/reading-companion/maintenance/reading-methodology-provenance.md`. On 2026-11-22 every source those 36 lines cite was opened again, each line received the record that the scientific method of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/method.md` asks for, and a search looked for tests that bear on each claim. Of the 36 lines, 3 are claims supported under other conditions (fit: transfer), 3 are norms, and 30 are decisions of the repository owner, each naming a hypothesis; the 30 hypotheses stand in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/reading-companion/maintenance/pending-evidence.md` and name 169 tests. Eleven records claimed support; independent readers held 3 of them to all seven rules of the method, and the other 8 are the owner's decisions. The re-opened sources show 52 places where the repository's text says other than its source: 21 in the provenance lines, corrected there, and 31 in the run set, for which 27 exact edits are prepared and wait for the owner's decision. Researchers raised 112 proposals of further tests; a skeptic let 27 stand as stated, found 85 weaker, and found none that fell. No test ran with a language-model companion and a whole book.

Main results:

- Three claims are supported, on these tests and within the bounds of their conditions: written recall after a passage beat rereading it (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Experiment 1, 68% against 54% after 2 days, t(39) = 6.97), a night's sleep before relearning beat a day awake (Mazza et al. 2016, 15.20 against 11.25 of 16 words a week later, U = 20, p < .001), and an if-then plan beat a goal intention alone (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, d = .65, 95% CI .60 to .70, 94 tests).
- Eight of the eleven records that claimed support are decisions of the owner with a hypothesis, six of them because the source the claim came from was also offered as its test (rule 6).
- The one proposal inside its line's scope, Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024, bears against the size the methodology gives implementation intentions: over 642 tests the effect on behaviour change is d = .27 [.23, .31], which the authors call small.
- Three tests bear against a claim under other conditions and stand as stated: retrieval practice did not beat rereading 10 weeks later (Sigayret et al. 2026, F(1, 66) = 0.13, p = 0.72) or on a published research article (Redifer et al. 2026), and a preregistered replication found no effect of contingent tutoring (Smit et al. 2025, F(3, 281) = 0.97, p = .407).
- The owner is asked two things: whether to apply all 27 prepared edits, the five that bring a figure or quoted wording alone, or none; and whether the Feynman check of § 4 should say "aloud", since in Lachner, Jacob & Hoogerheide 2021 spoken explaining gave g = 0.336, 95% CI [0.158, 0.513], p < .001, and written explaining gave g = -0.070, p = .535.

## What was investigated and why

The methodology states the claims behind the companion's rules: how forgetting works, which reading modes and techniques to use, how to distribute reviews, how notes become the reader's own, how to form a reading habit, and how a companion should help understanding (§§ 2–13). Each claim has one line in the provenance map, keyed by section and claim. A line gives the citation as the methodology held it, the claim it backs, the tests and documents found, and a record by the format of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/record-format.md`.

The run asked two questions of each line. The first was whether each cited source says what the repository's text says. The second was which test or document bears on the claim behind the rule, and whether the claim can stand as supported, as a norm, or only as the owner's decision with a hypothesis.

The run does not answer whether the companion itself produces any of these effects, because no test ran with a language-model companion and a whole book. It does not decide the prepared edits to the run set, which wait for the owner. It reports the proposals the researchers raised beyond each line's record and does not enter them into the lines.

## Method

Each cited source was opened again on 2026-11-22 and read as fully as access allowed: `full` means the text itself, `abstract` means only the abstract or a catalogue record, and `relay` means a text that quotes or summarises the source. The quoted words and figures of the provenance map and of the list of hypotheses, 1,944 in all, stand with their sources in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/reading-companion/maintenance/citations.json`, and `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/maintenance/scripts/check_quotes.py`, run over the texts fetched on 2026-11-22, finds every one. 74 sources stayed unopened or were read through another text.

One researcher grounded each line: read its cited sources, searched for further tests within a fixed budget, and wrote the line's record. A test is a comparison with a measured outcome under stated conditions. A norm is a document whose issuer prescribes a practice; three lines rest on one. A review that relays other studies and runs no comparison of its own counts as a document and not as a test. A claim with no test of its own comparison stands as the owner's decision, and its hypothesis names the measure, the comparison, the conditions and the outcome that would refute it.

Each of the eleven records that claimed support was read by an independent reader who saw nothing of the work behind it. The reader answered the check questions of the method's seven rules (facts apart from decisions, refutability, severity, reproducibility, bounds of transfer, no fitting, revision) and opened the cited sources. A record that failed a rule was amended and read again, with two rounds of amendment at most. A record that still failed, or that could not be amended within the evidence, was lowered to a decision with a hypothesis.

A skeptic re-read every proposal against its source. For each, the skeptic restated the test, gave a verdict (stands, weaker, fell), named its bearing (`against` the claim, `supports` it, `bounds` it as the same question under other conditions, `new` as a tested finding no line covers, or `none`), and said whether the test lies inside the line's scope. This note states each proposal in the skeptic's wording and with the skeptic's bearing.

After the skeptic, the 21 places in the provenance lines that said other than their sources were corrected, and all 36 lines stand corrected to the skeptic's reading. The run set, meaning the files the skill reads in a run, is unchanged.

The run has these limits:

- One researcher grounded each line, so a test one researcher missed has no second search behind it.
- Each search ran within a budget, so the tests found are those the budget reached.
- Some sources were read by abstract or through a text that quotes them; their figures are as the abstract or the relay prints them.
- The readers and the skeptic were models of one family, the same as the researchers.
- No test ran with a language-model companion and a whole book, so every record transfers from other conditions.

## The records that claimed support

§8 Implementation intentions holds all seven rules at the first reading. It is supported, on these tests, within these bounds, with fit: transfer. The test it rests on is Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006: over 94 tests with 8,461 participants an if-then plan added to a goal intention beat the intention alone, d = .65, 95% CI .60 to .70, and for academic goals d = .72, CI .56 to .87, over 9 tests (§ B Results). University students took part in 79 of the 94 tests, the goals lay in health, academic, laboratory and other domains, and none planned the application of a thesis read in a book with a language-model companion helping to form the plan.

§4.1 Read by chapters with retrieval holds all seven rules at the second reading, after one amendment. The first reading failed rule 3, because the record gave no p-value and its second experiment gave the testing arm 35 minutes against 20 for rereading, and rule 7, because it left out tests against the claim. The amended record computes two-sided p from t and its degrees of freedom (2.4e-8 and 1.4e-7 for Experiment 1, 1.2e-5 for Experiment 2), places Experiment 2 under other conditions, names the tests against with their conditions, and narrows the bounds. It separates the tests in the claim's direction from those against by the conditions each ran under, which is not a tested moderator. The claim is supported, on these tests, within these bounds, with fit: transfer. The test it rests on is Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Experiment 1: 120 undergraduates, after a first 7-minute reading of a passage of 256 or 275 words, recalled 68% of idea units after one written free recall against 54% after one rereading at 2 days, t(39) = 6.97, d = 0.95, and 56% against 42% at 1 week, t(39) = 6.41, d = 0.83; after 5 minutes rereading led, 81% against 75%, t(39) = 3.22 (Method, Results). The test ran in a laboratory on short passages with recall written once and not checked against the text, while the skill's scope is an adult who reads a whole chapter, writes its theses in his own words and checks them against the text beside a 2026 Claude model. The tests that found no benefit over restudy ran on texts of about 500 words and a narrated video in online samples (Sigayret et al. 2026), on a published research article (Redifer et al. 2026), and on problem solving from worked examples (the 2015 studies relayed by Karpicke & Aue 2015).

§5.2 Let at least one sleep pass holds all seven rules at the third reading, after two amendments. The first two readings failed rule 4, because the reader could not open Mazza et al. 2016 or Bell et al. 2014; the amended record names an open copy of each with pages, the copy of Mazza et al. being a third party's download of the article. The claim is supported, on these tests, within these bounds, with fit: transfer. In Mazza et al. 2016, 40 adults relearned 16 Swahili–French word pairs 12 hours after learning, across a night's sleep or a day awake; a week later the sleep group recalled 15.20 against 11.25, U = 20, p < .001, d = 2.93, and six months later 8.67 against 3.35, t(36) = 7.61, p < .001 (Results). In Bell et al. 2014 the overnight advantage on a test 10 days later did not reach significance, t(67) = 1.3, p = .19. The tests used word pairs learned in one sitting, and the sleep arms learned at 9 p.m. and the wake arms at 9 a.m., while the skill's scope is an adult reading whole chapters with a Claude companion. The count of 177 articles in Berres & Erdfelder 2021 rests on its abstract.

The other eight records are decisions of the owner, each with its hypothesis:

- §2 The reliable signal of knowledge failed rules 3, 6 and 7 at the first reading. No test varies the closed-book attempt; the nearest, Hausman & Kubik 2023, gave gamma .21 against .16, t(88) = 0.47, p = .32. Koriat & Bjork 2005 and 2006, from which the claim came, were also offered as its test, and the reader held that the claim should stand as a hypothesis.
- §4.5 Questions that ask to apply failed rule 6 at the first reading. Agarwal 2019 is the claim's origin and the only test of applying against recalling, t(47) = 6.73; the other tests the record named, among them Pan & Rickard 2018 and An et al. 2025, compare other conditions.
- §4.5 A comparison failed rules 4 and 6 at the first reading. The reader could not open Alfieri et al. 2013; the version of record prints d = .37 against sequential study (Table 6, p. 106), so rule 4 alone would not have lowered the record. Alfieri et al. list Gentner et al. 2003 Experiment 2 (d = 0.58) and Loewenstein et al. 2003 (d = 0.55) among their pooled experiments (Table 1), so the tests are not independent of the source, and Roelle & Berthold 2015 was read by abstract only.
- §5.1 Distribute the reviews failed rule 6 at the first reading, because Cepeda et al. 2006 is its origin, and rules 4 and 7 at the second. Rawson & Kintsch 2005 was read by abstract, which compares each rereading only with a single reading. Greving & Richter 2019 found massed rereading ahead on comprehension 4 minutes later, 0.47 against 0.35, z = -2.16, p = .031, no difference a week later, 0.30 against 0.26, z = 0.75, p = .455, and wrote that their Hypothesis 1 "was not supported".
- §13 Constructing and self-correcting failed rule 6. Bisra et al. 2018 and Chi & Wylie 2014 are its origin. The only later test, Asher et al. 2026, found Socratic tutoring 0.24 SD ahead of explanations on practice-aligned questions only, t(392) = 1.98, p = .048, with no test against the correct-answer arm, 99 learners in the Socratic arm, and sessions of 13.3 minutes against 6.8 to 7.3.
- §13.4 Feedback elaborates failed rules 6 and 7. Van der Kleij et al. 2015 and the thesis behind it are its origin. During reading, Swart et al. 2019 found right/wrong feedback at g+ = 0.39 against 0.25 for elaborated feedback, Q(3) = 2.86, p = .41; the thesis's B = .40, p = .057, fails the record's own threshold of p < 0.05; and the support after reading rests on 6 against 12 contrasts.
- §13.6b Know when to simply explain held all seven rules at a first reading and failed rule 6 at a second. Sinha & Kapur 2021 and Schwartz & Bransford 1998 are its origin, and the support for building the explanation on the reader's words is one fidelity subgroup of seven at p = .02, uncorrected.
- §13.7 Stop re-explaining failed rule 7. The record left out Salden et al. 2010 Experiment 2, a classroom study with no difference on the immediate test (Zs < 1) and a delayed difference, t(32) = 2.10, d = .74, where the adaptive arm received more worked examples, 3.65 against 2.97, p = .09; it also left out that skilled high-knowledge readers benefited from a high-cohesion text (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007). Salden's laboratory contrast pools fixed fading with problem solving, so adaptive against fixed fading stands only as means, 7.13 against 5.21, and the asymmetry of Tetzlaff et al. 2025 (d = 0.505 against -0.428) has no test.

## What the re-opened sources say

21 places where a provenance line said other than its source are corrected in the provenance map. 31 places stand in the run set, which is unchanged; each is listed below with the text's wording and the source's. 27 exact edits are prepared, 26 in the methodology and 1 in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/reading-companion/assets/AGENTS.template.md`: 5 bring a figure or a quoted wording to the source and 22 bring a claim to it. They wait for the owner's decision.

- § 3, Adler's four questions: the text puts "What is the chapter about as a whole?" and three reworded questions to a chapter; Adler & Van Doren put four questions to a book, "WHAT IS THE BOOK ABOUT AS A WHOLE?", "WHAT IS BEING SAID IN DETAIL, AND HOW?", "IS THE BOOK TRUE, IN WHOLE OR PART?" and "WHAT OF IT?" (chapter 5, pp. 46–47).
- § 3, the syntopical set: the text says students who wrote an argument from several sources did better on inference and analogy tasks than those who wrote a summary, a narrative or an explanation; Wiley & Voss 1999 say "students who wrote arguments from the web sources gained a better understanding than other students", the others including students given a textbook chapter.
- § 3, the syntopical set: the text has the reader set the authors' terms side by side; Adler & Van Doren, through a page that quotes them, ask for "a neutral terminology of the subject that all, or the great majority, of the authors can be interpreted as employing".
- § 4 item 5, which § 3 cites: the text gives d ≈ 0.50 over 57 experiments for comparing two cases against studying them one by one; that d pools 336 tests against all other forms of case study and instruction, and against the same cases studied in sequence Alfieri et al. 2013 print d = .37, 95% CI [.31, .44], over 201 tests (Table 6).
- § 3, speed reading: the text says RSVP and Spritz worsen comprehension above ~250 wpm; Rayner et al. 2016 name no such threshold, write that "speeding text presentation beyond normal reading rates consistently led to reduced levels of comprehension and memory", and report Spritz at 200 and 209 wpm with poorer literal comprehension.
- § 3, speed reading: the text calls the speed–accuracy trade-off physiologically irremovable; the review places the limit in language, reading being "limited by our ability to attend to, identify, and understand words rather than our ability to see them", and states "It is unlikely that readers will be able to double or triple their reading speeds".
- § 3, speed reading: the text calls growth of language skill and vocabulary the only loss-free way to read faster; the review names practice and language skill as "the way to maintain high comprehension and get through text faster" and says "modest improvements are possible".
- § 4, opening: the text says only two of ten techniques have high utility in controlled studies; Dunlosky et al. 2013 give "an overall assessment for each technique" of the evidence.
- § 4, opening: the text places highlighting and rereading at the bottom as the chief sources of the illusion of knowledge; the review puts them among five low-rated techniques, says nothing of an illusion of knowledge, and writes "these techniques do not consistently boost students' performance".
- § 4 item 1: the text says over 2 days rereading lost ~56% and testing ~13%; Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Fig. 3, shows forgetting over 1 week, 52% for four readings and 14% for three tests, proportions for which the source names no test, and after 2 days the tested group recalled 68% against 54%, t(39) = 6.97.
- § 4 item 2: the text calls self-explanation a confirmed elaboration technique; Dunlosky et al. 2013 rate it moderate because "the evidence for their efficacy is limited".
- § 4.5: the text says a prediction teaches through the surprise at a wrong one; Brod 2021 writes that predicting "derives parts of its effectiveness from leading learners to retrieve information from memory and engage in semantic elaboration" and that incorrect predictions "additionally" evoke surprise.
- § 4 item 5, reached from the § 4.5 comparison line: the same sentence and the same source figures as the fourth place above.
- § 4, desirable difficulty: the text says retrieval, interleaving and generation improve long-term retention at the price of visible slowing, marked high; Bjork & Bjork 2011 say such conditions "often optimize long-term retention and transfer" and show the slowing for interleaving and tests, not for generation.
- § 6, table of note types, literature notes: the text keeps them until the permanent note is created; Ahrens, as two pages of reading notes quote him, has literature notes kept in the reference system.
- § 6, heading: the text names the three types fleeting, literature and permanent; Ahrens's own three types, as the same notes give them, are fleeting, permanent and project notes, with literature notes a subcategory of permanent notes.
- § 8: the text says the effects are large and concentrated on launching; Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 call the effect "a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude", find it for initiation (d = .61), shielding, disengagement and conservation of capability, and the 100% against 53% the text cites is Orbell, Hodgkins & Sheeran 1997 as Gollwitzer 1999 reports it.
- § 9: the text gives ~66 days to automaticity marked high; the figure is the median time to 95% of each person's asymptote among those whose curve fit (Lally et al. 2010), and the times rest on four studies (Singh et al. 2024), where the mark high asks for a meta-analysis or a consistent series of controlled studies.
- § 12: the text says only the transfer of abstract principles in close contexts is reliable; Barnett & Ceci 2002 write that "evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial, but critical conditions for many key questions are untested" and that far transfer is "documentable" (p. 634).
- § 13: the text says self-generated prompts beat ready-made explanations; Bisra et al. 2018 report "a statistically detectable advantage (g = .35) of self-explanation over instructional explanations", with prompts scripted by instructors.
- § 13: the text says a companion that delivers a polished explanation forfeits the benefit; Bisra et al. 2018 write that "a substantial portion of the benefit reported for self-explanation appears to be alternately available from instructor-provided explanations" (g = .673 against no explanation, g = .354 against instructional explanations).
- § 13.1: the text says help before the reader commits undercuts the effect, marked moderate; none of the opened sources reports this, van de Pol et al. 2010 make diagnosis a precondition of contingent support, and the one experiment of the model found no difference in achievement gains (van de Pol 2012, ch. 7, Table 6).
- § 13, move 3: the text says frequent help erases the benefit; van de Pol et al. 2015 found low-contingent support more effective than high-contingent support when independent working time was short, on the multiple-choice test with task effort controlled.
- The workspace template AGENTS.template.md: the text says helping too readily erases the gain; the same source finds no gain from less frequent help as such, and the result held "only true when students' task effort was controlled for".
- § 13, move 4: the text gives knowledge of results d ≈ 0.05 and elaborated feedback d ≈ 0.49; the thesis version of the meta-analysis computes Hedges-corrected effect sizes, its 0.49 pools contrasts against no feedback (0.61, k = 9), right/wrong feedback (0.54, k = 21) and correct-answer feedback (0.39, k = 23), and the 0.05 is against no feedback alone (Van der Kleij 2013, ch. 4).
- § 13.5: the text lists scores, tallies and praise as feedback that draws attention to the self; Kluger & DeNisi 1996 "did not find an effect for normative FIs", correct-incorrect feedback correlated -.13 with the effect before exclusions and not after, and the cues that weakened the effect after all exclusions are praise, discouraging feedback and threats to self-esteem.
- § 13.5: the text calls the mechanism solid, marked high; Kluger & DeNisi write that the propositions are supported "only with an overall pattern of results and not with detailed evidence at the single-variable level".
- § 13.6: the text gives the worked-example effect as d ≈ 0.55, marked high; Atkinson et al. 2003 report Cohen's f of .23 and .27 for fading against example–problem pairs, and Barbieri et al. 2023 report g = 0.48, p = 0.01, for worked examples in mathematics.
- § 13, move 6: the text says unguided struggle overloads working memory and does not build understanding; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 write that free exploration "may generate a heavy working memory load that is detrimental to learning", as what the theory suggests, with no test of their own.

## Tests for and against a claim

39 proposals bear against a claim or in its direction: 20 against and 19 in its direction. The skeptic let nine of them stand as stated, all of them against.

§2 Forgetting is interference. The hypothesis holds that a chapter is recalled better at a later test when no source on a similar subject is read right after it. In Seban, Šikl, Prošek & Urban 2026, 161 undergraduates read a 617-word text for 8 minutes and then, by random assignment, rested in guided autogenic training (37), scrolled social media (38), did arithmetic (43) or read a 635-word content-similar text (43). On the knowledge test a week later the similar-text group scored 30.93% against 21.08% for rest, Mdiff = -9.85, 95% CI [-17.30, -2.40], t(157) = -2.61, p = .010, d = -0.53 (Results). The participants were students, the material one short text and the interval 8 minutes. The skeptic found missing that this is one of several uncorrected contrasts, that the group main effect on knowledge was F(3, 157) = 2.56, p = .057, that the memory test showed no group effect, F(3, 157) = 0.98, p = .404, that rest was autogenic training, and that the similar text described another bird's behaviour.

§3 Speed reading is refuted. The hypothesis holds that a reader who reads faster by a speed-reading technique understands less than at his normal rate. In Gu et al. 2024, 18 participants read three GRE passages of 444–472 words in a within-subjects design; with an LLM-based rendering (GP-TSM) the task took 8 min 7 s against 9.25 min for plain text, p = 0.019, and comprehension was higher, p = .021 (Tukey HSD). The skeptic found missing that the time gain of about 12% is far below the refuter's "by half or more", that the time includes answering, that each condition used a different passage, and that a second rendering, WF-TSM, scored below plain text, p = .045, without being faster.

§5.2 Let at least one sleep pass. The run set's § 5 item 2 says sleep makes memory "resistant to interference — high". Bailes et al. 2020 had 97 students learn word pairs 12 hours before a retest: recall was better after sleep, F(1, 95) = 4.44, p = 0.038, and interfering pairs cut recall by 16.9% after sleep and 14.6% after wake, interaction F(1, 95) = 0.38, p = 0.54 (Results). The study had no review after the interval. The skeptic found missing that the authors cannot accept the null and that small protective effects lie within their 90% confidence interval; the test bears against the run set's clause and not against the line's claim.

§8 Implementation intentions. This is the one proposal inside its line's scope. The methodology says the effects are large and concentrated on launching. In Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024, a random-effects meta-analysis of 642 tests, the overall effect is d = .36 [.33, .40], the trim-and-fill estimate .35, and the Robust Bayesian estimate d = .15 [.08, .22]; behaviour change gives d = .27 [.23, .31] (k = 301), cognitive performance .47, affect regulation .66, and social judgment .52 (Table 1). The authors call the effect "small according to conventional guidelines". The skeptic found that the low end, behaviour change, is the launching that § 8 calls large.

§13.3 Default to under-helping. The run set's § 13 move 3 says frequent help erases the benefit. In van de Pol et al. 2015, 30 teachers and 768 students aged 12–15 worked in small groups; independent working time was observed, not assigned. Its interaction with occasion was .08, p = .42, on the multiple-choice test and -.12, p = .27, on the knowledge assignment, and the three-way effect with contingency reached .22, p = .04, only with task effort controlled (Tables 8 and 9). The skeptic found missing that working time moved with the scaffolding condition, that the measure was a pre-to-post score, not a retention test, and that the three-way result partly favours the claim.

§13 Honest limits. The hypothesis holds that reading with the companion raises test scores by d = 0.4 to 0.8. In Bastani et al. 2025, nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school students in about fifty classes had four 90-minute math sessions; practice scores rose 48% with a GPT-4 chat and 127% with a prompted GPT-4 tutor, both p < 0.01, and on the unassisted exam the chat arm scored -0.054 (SE 0.022), 17% below control, and the tutor arm -0.004 (SE 0.013) (Table 1). The skeptic found missing that the tutor's prompt carried teacher-written solutions and common mistakes, and that the bearing on the 0.4 floor holds only for high-school mathematics and GPT-4, not an adult reading a book.

§4 Desirable difficulty, first test. The hypothesis holds that recall, interleaving and generation beat the same study without them a day or more later. In Redifer et al. 2026, 213 undergraduates studied a published research article with combined testing strategies or by rereading; retrieval practice did not improve the final test over rereading, raised cognitive load, and load mediated the effect (abstract). The source names no test in its abstract. The skeptic found missing that the abstract does not state the delay, that the arms combine strategies, that question generation is not answer generation, and that the result is a null, not a reversal.

§4 Desirable difficulty, second test. In Sigayret, Parmentier & Silvestre 2026, Experiment 1, 67 undergraduates on Prolific studied two texts of about 500 words, one with corrective-feedback questions and one with re-exposure; 10 weeks later condition had no effect, F(1, 66) = 0.13, p = 0.72. In Experiment 2, 129 adults watched a narrated animation and then recalled or rewatched it; two days later retrieval gave 7.44 against 6.51 ideas, U = 1773, p = 0.17, r = 0.14 (§ 3). The skeptic found missing that Experiment 2's material was a video, that completion was 39.6% and 30.3%, that the authors say the nulls "should not be taken as evidence against the testing effect per se", and that the engagement explanation was not tested.

§13.3 One graduated, contingent question. The hypothesis holds that one question at the lightest level that could close a gap, raised after failure and lowered after success, beats help at a level fixed in advance. Smit et al. 2025 replicated Wood, Wood & Middleton 1978 with 285 three-year-olds assigned to demonstration, verbal, swing or contingent instruction on a construction task: contingent 5.07, demonstration 4.33, verbal 6.23, swing 5.38 correct unaided operations, F(3, 281) = 0.97, p = .407, BF = 17.9 for the null, and contingent against the rest t(281) = -0.26, p = .795. The skeptic found that the test ran with 3-year-olds, human tutors and a construction task, and that it undercuts the contingent-shift principle and not the line's citation of Chi et al. 2001.

## Open questions

Every one of the 30 hypotheses waits for a test under the skill's conditions. For several, no test found compares even the hypothesis's own pair: no test varies the closed-book attempt (H-2-The-reliable-signal-of-knowledge); no tested arm keeps the invariant that the reader writes the note with the source closed (H-6-The-critical-invariant); no test sets an AI that edits only the reader's own work against an AI that writes the notes (H-7-Why-the-line-falls-exactly-here); no test sets a chain of closed questions against a conversation with a delayed comprehension test (H-13-5-Never-a-quiz); no test starts a new field with an overview, a glossary or an introductory text against none (H-10-The-Matthew-effect); and no test gives an interval for reading a book with a companion against reading it alone (H-13-Honest-limits).

74 sources stayed unopened or were read through another text. Among those the lines cite for a claim: Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976, read through Smit et al. 2025; Ahrens 2017, read through two pages of reading notes; Orbell, Hodgkins & Sheeran 1997, read by abstract with its figures through Gollwitzer 1999; Rawson & Kintsch 2005 and Roelle & Berthold 2015, read by abstract; Redifer et al. 2026, read by abstract and a summary of the publisher's page; Seban, Urban & Šikl 2024, whose full text could not be fetched; and Adler & Van Doren's chapter on syntopical reading, read through a page that quotes it. Whether §5.1 could stand supported on Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021 alone was raised and not tried.

The tests that would bear on the skill's own claims would run with adults reading whole chapters with the skill's companion and a test a day or more later:

- written recall of a chapter's theses, checked against the text with the companion, against rereading for the same time (§4.1);
- a first review after a night's sleep against a review on the same day (§5.2);
- an if-then plan formed with the companion against a goal intention alone, measured by applying a thesis within the planned week (§8);
- explaining aloud to the companion against explaining in writing (§4.2);
- one graduated, contingent question against an explanation of the correct reading at once (§13.3);
- the reader's score with the companion against the same reading without it, with a 95% interval (§13 Honest limits).
