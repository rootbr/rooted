# What the techniques of discovering-subtopics rest on — sources re-opened and evidence found to 2026-11-22

The skill `discovering-subtopics` builds a broad map of questions on a topic with ten methods, each run as a separate agent, and its provenance map `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/maintenance/methodology-provenance.md` holds 22 lines, one per source reference behind a question-generating technique of methods 01 to 06, 08 and 09; methods 07 and 10 cite no source. For every line the cited sources were opened again or read through a named substitute, the claim behind the technique was stated, and tests of that claim and documents that prescribe the technique were searched for. All 22 lines close as decisions of the repository owner, each naming a hypothesis, and no line took the standing `supported`. The 22 hypotheses in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/maintenance/pending-evidence.md` name 67 tests in 21 lines and none in one, and none of the tests ran inside the scope of the claims: a Claude agent of the skill writing a question map. Eleven places were found where the repository's text says other than its source: 8 in the provenance lines, corrected there, and 3 in the run set, which is unchanged. The search raised 38 proposals, and a skeptic re-read each against its source: 16 stand as stated, 22 are weaker than stated, and none fell. By the skeptic's reading 4 bear against a technique as the skill uses it, 2 in a claim's direction, 10 on the same question under other conditions, 20 are tested findings no line covers, and 2 bear on no line. The 780 quoted words and figures the lines rest on are held in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/maintenance/citations.json`, and `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/maintenance/scripts/check_quotes.py` finds every one in the texts fetched on 2026-11-22.

- No technique of the skill rests on a test of its own claim: each of the 22 lines is the owner's decision, and the 67 tests its hypotheses name ran on people, on other models or on other tasks.
- Two government handbooks prescribe the assumption check that method-06 uses; a norm of this kind shows that the practice is accepted and leaves open whether it adds questions to a map.
- Four tests bear against a technique as the skill uses it — morphological analysis, Six Thinking Hats, perspective-based reading behind stakeholder mapping, and causal-model building behind systems thinking — and two lean towards a claim, the analogy pass and the pre-mortem, each in people or on another task.
- The tests under other conditions set conditions on several claims: for the strongest models a cross-domain source raised rated originality only when it was distant, and a persona's wording or stereotype set the direction of its effect.
- Twenty tested findings no line covers give the owner candidates for new hypotheses, among them a one-line instruction to go beyond categories already used and a relational-analogy step before fields are chosen.
- Three places in the run set say other than their sources and one prompt asks for more questions than its own budget allows; three prepared edits wait for the owner's decision.

## What was investigated and why

The run set of the skill is `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/references/methodology-inventory.md` and the method prompts under `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/agents/`. Method 01, structural decomposition, runs Starbursting (5W1H), morphological analysis and SCAMPER, with its gotcha G-02 on the Eliminate transformation and its length targets. Method 02, perspective shifting, runs Six Thinking Hats, stakeholder mapping, rolestorming and cross-domain analogy. Method 03, inversion, runs the Munger-Jacobi inversion, the pre-mortem and reverse brainstorming. Method 04, causal chains, runs 5 Whys, systems thinking and TRIZ contradiction analysis. Method 05 runs a temporal lens and scale shifting; method 06 runs first principles, assumption mapping and a blind-spot probe; method 08 is the adjacent-discipline sweep and method 09 the meta-questions. Method 07, field intelligence by live web search, and method 10, the coverage audit, cite no source.

The provenance map cites, for each technique, the source the run set names: a founding book such as de Bono 1985 or Altshuller 1984, an article, a web page or a speech. Three lines cite no external source: G-02, the length targets and the blind-spot probe, which the run set calls house heuristics, defaults and technique.

By `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/method.md`, a rule of a skill is a prescription with two premises: a goal, which is the owner's decision, and the claim that the technique serves the goal, which the method examines. Two questions were asked of every line. First, does the cited source say what the line says? Second, which test or document bears on the claim that the technique yields questions the map would otherwise miss?

The run does not answer whether any technique, in the skill's agents with 2026 Claude models, adds questions the map would miss: no test of that ran. It does not rank the techniques, and it does not change the run set.

## Method

Re-opening and grounding. Eleven researchers took two lines each. For every line a researcher opened the cited sources, stated the claim behind the technique, searched for tests of it and for documents that prescribe it, and wrote the line's text, its record in the format of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/record-format.md` and its hypothesis. Every quoted word and figure a line uses stands in `citations.json` with its source. 49 sources stayed unopened or were read through another text; most are books and articles behind publishers' walls, read through library records, publishers' pages or texts that quote them, and the lines say which.

What counts as a test. A test is a comparison whose groups, measure, sample and result are in view in the opened source (rules 3 and 4 of the method). A source that passed peer review is accepted by its abstract, and the record says that the abstract alone was seen; a relay, a text reporting another, is marked as such. A test whose people, models, task or measure differ from the claim's scope bears on the claim under other conditions, and a result there is never written as support.

What counts as a norm. A norm is what an accepted document prescribes, opened with its issuer and version. A norm shows that a practice is accepted; whether the practice yields more questions is a separate claim, which needs a test.

The records. A technique whose claim waits for its test is in force as its owner's decision, and its record names the claim as `H-<line id>` in the list of hypotheses, with its measure, its comparison, its scope and the outcome that would refute it. The method has an independent reader answer the seven check questions for a record that takes the standing `supported`; no line took it, so no record went to that reader.

The skeptic. The 38 proposals the researchers raised were re-read against their sources by a skeptic, who gave each a verdict (stands, weaker, fell), a bearing (against, supports, bounds, new, none) and a note of what the proposal leaves out. Every proposal is stated in this note in the skeptic's wording and with the skeptic's bearing.

The corrections. Fifteen lines were returned for revision with the skeptic's reading, and 13 of them were corrected. The eight places in the provenance lines where the text said other than its source are corrected there; the three in the run set wait as prepared edits.

Limits. Each line had one researcher, so a test that researcher missed was searched for by no one else. Each search ran under a budget of searches. Many of the tests are preprints of 2024 to 2026 that have not passed peer review, read in full. The researchers, the writers of the corrections and the skeptic are models of one family, so a blind spot they share is not caught by one checking another. No test ran inside the scope of the claims.

## The norm

Two documents prescribe the technique of the line `method-06 Assumption mapping`. The US Government's A Tradecraft Primer, March 2009, under Diagnostic Techniques, Key Assumptions Check, pp. 7–9, prescribes: "2. Articulate all the premises, both stated and unstated", and "3. Challenge each assumption, asking why it “must” be true and whether it remains valid under all conditions." Among its questions to ask it prints: "If the assumption proves to be wrong, would it signifcantly alter the analytic line? How?", the misspelling the primer's own [36].

The UK Ministry of Defence's Red Teaming Handbook, 3rd Edition, June 2021, issued by its Development, Concepts and Doctrine Centre, states in §5.24 that "Assumptions check is a technique in which both implicit and explicit assumptions" of a problem are examined, prescribes "3. Analyse the description and highlight assumptions that have not been explicitly stated", and asks of each "How valid is this assumption? ... What is the impact if it is invalid?"; §8.7 adds an assumptions review "similar to the assumptions check technique" [37].

These are norms: they show that intelligence and defence analysis accept the practice. The line also names a test of the technique, Hender, Dean, Rodgers and Nunamaker 2002 [97], so its record is the owner's decision and its claim, that the assumption-mapping section adds questions the same agent without it does not write, waits as `H-method-06-Assumption-mapping`.

## Tests against a technique

Morphological analysis, method-01. Daly, Seifert, Yilmaz and Gonzalez randomly assigned 102 first-year engineering students to individual brainstorming (32), design heuristics (35) or morphological analysis (35); each worked alone on one design problem for 25 minutes after about 25 minutes of instruction [42]. Brainstorming produced more concepts than morphological analysis, p < 0.001, by a general linear model with Tukey post hoc tests. Two blind raters scored the diversity of each concept set on a 7-point scale: without controls the brainstorming sets were more diverse, p < 0.0001, and with the number of concepts as covariate no group differed, all p's > 0.10. The conditions differ from the claim's: novice people sketching product concepts in a fixed time, with the matrix set against brainstorming, where the claim removes the matrix section and keeps the agent's other techniques. The skeptic finds the proposal standing as stated and adds the unadjusted diversity result, which goes further against the claim; the authors' guess that the technique may help once intuitive generation is exhausted is a speculation of their Discussion, not a result.

Six Thinking Hats, method-02. Two abstracts describe one design [59][60]: 118 participants were randomly allocated across the six men, the six hats and a placebo control, brain-breathing, read a synopsis of their tool, and restated an ambiguous problem in as many ways as they could, scored for fluency, quality, flexibility and originality. In the journal abstract the six men gave greater fluency and flexibility and lower quality than both the hats and the placebo, and no difference between the hats and the placebo is reported. In the conference abstract the six men gave greater fluency than both, the placebo gave greater quality than the six men, the hats showed a trend for better quality than the six men, and no group differed in flexibility or originality. Neither abstract names a test or gives statistics. The conditions are people restating one problem, with the hats set against a placebo procedure rather than against the same agent without the hats at an equal question budget. The skeptic reads the proposal as weaker than stated: 118 is the total across three groups, so about a third used the hats; the journal abstract reports no comparison of the hats with the placebo, so the absence of a gain there is not a tested null; only the conference abstract states that the groups did not differ, and only in flexibility and originality; and the two abstracts disagree on the six men's flexibility.

Stakeholder mapping, method-02. A replication of perspective-based reading had 18 undergraduates of the University of São Paulo at São Carlos inspect two requirements documents, ATM and PG, one with a checklist and the other from one of three perspectives, designer, tester or end user [65]. A balanced ANOVA on effectiveness, the percentage of seeded defects found, gave a main effect of technique of p = .354; by document, ATM gave 11.41 against 7.21 (p = .143) and PG 15.28 against 15.63 (p = .911), and a team permutation test gave p = 0.51. Two perspectives correlated significantly on PG, the one document where the perspectives showed no gain, and from that the authors hypothesise that the benefit needs perspectives that target different defects. The conditions are human inspectors finding seeded defects, with the perspectives set against a checklist rather than against the same reader without the step at an equal budget. The skeptic reads the proposal as weaker: it leaves out the original NASA study the same paper reports and reanalyses, which found the perspectives significantly more effective for teams and for individuals on the generic documents [66], so the paper's two runs point in opposite directions; the null comes from 18 subjects, a sample the authors name as a threat to external validity; and the hypothesis about different defects rests on two documents and is untested.

Systems thinking, method-04. The abstract of Frantz, Blotner and Petersen compares self-reported measures of a systems-thinking mindset with performative measures of the skill, reasoning about a system and metrics derived from participants' causal-model diagrams, across ecological and economic domains [90]. Building causal loop models increased the self-reported measures and not the performative ones, and the metrics from participants' own models correlated with some but not all measures. The abstract gives no sample, no comparison condition, no numbers and no test. The conditions are people reasoning about systems, not a model writing questions. The skeptic finds the proposal standing as stated, with three reservations: the design and the sample stay unverified because the full text could not be opened; the analogy between self-report and a count of `[Systems:*]` tags is the researcher's own; and the hypothesis already measures uncovered questions judged by a blind reader, so the bearing against holds only as an analogue in people.

Two proposals bear in a claim's direction by the skeptic's reading.

The cross-domain analogy pass, method-02. Shen, Druckmann and Zou took 50 research problems from a biomedical dataset of 266 papers and compared three settings: no domain constraint; a cross-domain setting that first names other domains and then searches them; and a relational-analogy setting that extracts the problem's objects and relations, writes analogies with object mappings and shared relations to domains the model picks itself, and then searches [70]. The models were Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash at temperature 1.0. Over 150 pooled generations per problem the three settings found 7.40, 7.90 and 27.56 unique domains and 86.42, 98.96 and 133.32 unique solutions. A Claude Sonnet 4.5 judge scored as novel 1.6%, 21.6% and 50.4% of Claude's solutions, 8.8%, 27.2% and 58.8% of GPT's and 8.6%, 37.9% and 68.8% of Gemini's; the figures carry 95% confidence intervals, and the source names no test for the differences. In a human study of 50 pairs four annotators judged the analogy solution the more novel in 78% (κ = 0.445), and judged reasonable 67% of the analogy solutions (κ = 0.155) against 86% of the cross-domain ones (κ = 0.416). The skeptic reads the comparison of the analogy setting with the no-domain setting as a comparison with and without an analogy pass, which supports the claim outside its scope: solutions to biomedical problems scored by an LLM judge, not question maps. The proposal states more than the source shows: the analogy setting also picks its own domains, so the source shows that direct domain naming collapses onto few domains, not that disciplines chosen by relational likeness repeat across runs; supplied or sampled disciplines are never tested; and the reasonableness figures come from pairs whose generating model is not named, with low agreement on the analogy side.

The pre-mortem, method-03. The abstract of Peabody's thesis reports three experiments [79]. In the first, 52 Army cadets in teams ran six time-constrained field exercises, half with the premortem and half with the standard military plan evaluation, and the premortem teams had fewer fouls and less fixation with no change in time. In the second, 72 individual participants from university organizations evaluated their group's plan for an engineering task with the premortem or the Worst-Case Scenario Method; the numbers of reasons and solutions showed no significant difference, and their distributions across categories differed significantly. In the third, 82 participants, as groups and as individuals, evaluated a complex and unfamiliar plan and generated more reasons with the premortem, and the distribution of reasons across categories again differed significantly. The abstract gives no test statistics and does not say whether the third experiment's difference in reasons was significant. The skeptic reads the abstract as leaning towards the claim outside its scope: the third experiment favoured the premortem on the number of reasons, and the different category distributions mean that the premortem drew reasons the worst-case frame did not, the nearest the source comes to the claim's count of unmatched questions. The proposal is weaker than stated: it calls the participants students, where the second experiment's came from university organizations and the third's are not described; and whether the skill's reverse brainstorming matches the Worst-Case Scenario Method the skeptic did not verify.

## Tested findings no rule covers

Twenty proposals report a tested finding that no line covers. Each names a candidate the owner may register as a hypothesis; none is a rule of the skill.

- Starbursting. Candidate: methods are scored by the count of what they yield or by coverage, since a rating of usefulness can diverge from the count. Test: 85 master students, 73 records analysed, each used 15 trigger questions derived from the six words and tailored to adaptive systems and solo brainstorming on two case studies; the trigger questions gave more requirement fragments, pairwise t-tests p ≤ 0.0001 at alpha 0.01, and were rated less useful, 3 against 4 on a 1–5 scale, one-tailed t-test p = 0.0003 [38]. Conditions: people working on UML models. The skeptic: stands; the claim that the more abstract questions yielded most fragments rests on a figure with no test and is confounded with question order.
- Morphological analysis. Candidate: the specificity of questions is scored beside coverage. Test: two raters blind to condition rated 439 concepts 1–7; morphological analysis was more elaborated than brainstorming, p = 0.02, design heuristics more than brainstorming, p = 0.05, and the two techniques did not differ, p = 0.91, by a linear mixed model with Tukey tests [42]. Conditions: sketched product concepts by students. The skeptic: stands; elaboration is the detail of a sketch, and reading it as the specificity of questions is an extrapolation; rated creativity did not differ.
- SCAMPER Eliminate. Candidate: a one-line removal cue in the other method prompts raises the share of maps that hold removal questions. Test: within the improvement cells of an experiment of 369 people the cue raised the share listing a subtractive idea from 21% to 48%, χ2 = 13.63, P < 0.001, and within the make-it-worse cells from 28% to 50%, χ2 = 9.71, P = 0.002 [50]; GPT-4o chose additive solutions in 337 of 354 accurate symmetry answers against 119 of 255 for humans, χ2(1) = 185.59, p < 0.001 [52]. Conditions: people on a miniature golf task; one model on a grid puzzle. The skeptic: weaker; 369 spans all four cells; the cue was tested on people alone, so its effect on a model is untested.
- Length targets. Candidate: a list-then-diversify step inside a method agent against the single pass. Test: a chain-of-thought prompt that lists 100 titles, makes them bolder and more different and then expands them gave pools of mean cosine similarity 0.255 against 0.377 for the base prompt, and the authors state that pool differences are significant by bootstrapping and permutation, p < 0.01; estimates of the idea space were about 4700 against about 3700, with no test [57]. Conditions: GPT-4 product ideas. The skeptic: weaker; the two arms ran on different model versions, the p-value is a blanket statement, and the estimates come from runs that dropped about 15% of the chain-of-thought runs.
- Six Thinking Hats. Candidate: the share of hat questions that duplicate method-01's 5W1H questions in the synthesis is measured, and the hats' share of the budget is cut if most do. Test: among 118 people the six men gave greater fluency, flexibility and originality than the placebo and the hats, the hats gave more original responses than the placebo, and the six men and the placebo were easier to use than the hats; the abstract names no test [58]. Conditions: people restating a given problem. The skeptic: stands; the abstract does not say against which tool the six men's users found theirs more useful.
- Stakeholder mapping. Candidate: "name at least one non-typical or extreme party" as a hypothesis for question maps. Test: three arms of 20 GPT-4-Turbo user agents for one product gave latent needs per agent of 8.825 when created automatically, 9.875 when steered by a prompt towards non-typical users and 10.875 when created manually as extreme lead users; only the manual arm against the automatic one is significant, t(38) = 2.318, p < 0.05 [64]. The skeptic: weaker; the arm that matches a prompt instruction, the steered one, has no test against the unsteered arm, so such an instruction is untested.
- Stakeholder mapping. Candidate, a lead: follow-up questions asked after reading an answer add more coverage than perspectives alone. Test: in the ablation with gpt-3.5-turbo asking, heading soft recall was 86.26 for the full pipeline, 84.49 without perspectives and 77.97 without the conversation, at an equal number of questions, with GPT-4 outlines 92.73, 92.39 and 88.75; the source names no test for the ablations [63]. The skeptic: stands as a lead; removing the conversation also removes the reading of grounded answers.
- Rolestorming. Candidate: personas are chosen for their spread. Test: on the alternative-uses task, Gemma-4-31B-it gave originality 10.79±0.32 with the task alone, 11.89±0.48 with random personas, 12.00±0.28 with personas selected for dispersion and 13.61±0.17 with personas generated by evolution, 95% intervals over five seeds; the source names no other test [68]. The skeptic: stands; the quoted gain comes from evolutionary generation, the selection arm's originality interval overlaps the random arm's, evolution lowers utility from 4.28 to 4.08, and the work is a preprint under review.
- Munger-Jacobi inversion. Candidate: a condition-by-condition "what if not" variant against the current inversion. Test: two randomly selected classes of 34 students; with the scientific approach and the what-if-not strategy posttest problem-posing scores were 24.91 against 18.06 for conventional teaching, t-test sig. 0.00 [75]. The skeptic: stands; the control is conventional teaching, so the strategy is not isolated, one class per arm makes a t-test over students ignore assignment by class, and the source gives two grades and two standard deviations for the same group.
- Munger-Jacobi inversion. Candidate: a backward pass that asks what must hold for the topic's claims, as a source of questions for method-03 or method-06. Test: reasoning backward to necessary conditions raised accuracy at detecting missing information in all six model and dataset pairs, GPT-5 from 81.19% to 90.10% on 101 MATH500 problems and GPT-3.5-turbo from 44.76% to 72.38% on 105 GSM8K problems; the source names no test [76]. The skeptic: stands; for GPT-3.5-turbo on MATH500 accuracy on complete problems fell from 80.65% to 75.81%.
- Pre-mortem. Candidate: a "will certainly fail" wording is tested against a "has failed" wording before the tense of G-01 of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/discovering-subtopics/agents/method-03-inversion/prompt.md` is kept. Test: in the first experiment temporal perspective showed little influence while outcome uncertainty strongly affected the nature of explanations, and explanations of sure events were longer and more episodic; the abstract gives no figures and no test [77]. The skeptic: stands; the measure is the nature of explanations, and what G-01 says the skeptic did not verify.
- Reverse brainstorming. Candidate: anti-answers that name how the topic would harm or humiliate the people it serves, against generic failure anti-answers. Test: among 47 novice designers in two course sections, 46% gave empathetic beneficial ideas after harmful and humiliating ideas against 0% after silly ones on one challenge, and 53% against 21% on the other, the source names no test of these shares; humiliating ideas predicted empathetic ones, χ2(1) = 8.65, p = 0.003, and χ2(1) = 10.40, p = 0.001, in the parsimonious models [83]. The skeptic: stands; condition is confounded with section and order, the full models give p = 0.06 and p = 0.01, and there is no arm without anti-answers.
- 5 Whys. Candidate: a check that each chain's last level names a structure, or an ACV means-end ladder beside 5 Whys. Test: in 216 simulated interviews 5-Whys matched the scripted truth in 26 of 36 runs on each script, 72.2%, against 94.4% and 83.3% for ACV and 91.7% and 72.2% for JTBD; the source names no test [87]. The skeptic: weaker; the proposal set ACV's reluctant-script figure beside the 5-Whys terse-script figure, and G-01 of the method-04 prompt already asks each level for "what structure makes this so", so the proposed check largely restates it.
- TRIZ contradiction analysis. Candidate: an instruction that a method agent list its own question categories and then write outside them. Test: across five models one-line prompts that ask the model to go beyond obvious or conventional categories raised novelty over the controls, p = 1.16×10^-34, with the highest of them at 0.525 against 0.468 and 0.474 [46]. The skeptic: weaker; the highest mean novelty of the 25 prompts is a TRIZ prompt's, 0.542, the tested prompts are single sentences of divergence wording, and the procedure of categorising first appears only as an example.
- Temporal lens. Candidate: a stated far horizon against the open "where is it heading?" wording. Test: people who imagined a task a year ahead did better on insight and abstract creative generation, the abstract names no test [94]; the best scenario prompt raised diversity, p 1.26×10^-9 as printed, with about half as many ideas [46]. The skeptic: weaker; five of seven strategies beat the controls on diversity, the table's columns align uncertainly, and neither test varies the horizon, so the proposal is a transfer; an unopened study on a 50-year against a 5-year horizon is a lead [104].
- First principles. Candidate: a probe that strips the topic's parts of their usual function. Test: people trained in the generic-parts technique solved on average 67% more problems than an untrained control group; the abstract gives no sample size and names no test [96]. The skeptic: stands; the technique's definition comes from outside the abstract and is unverified.
- Scale shifting. Candidate: a run checks whether `[Scale:Long]` questions come out more abstract and less checkable than `[Scale:Short]` ones, which bears on G-03 of method-05. Test: the same six experiments as the temporal lens [94]. The skeptic: stands; the manipulation primes the solver's own time perspective, so the carry-over to questions is a transfer.
- Blind-spot probe. Candidate measure: the share of map questions absent from the topic's canonical sources. Test: of 219,655 ideas that five agent frameworks with five models wrote from five seed papers, 10.5% held a research question absent from the seeds against 90.4% with a new method; the source names no test [99]. The skeptic: stands; the labels come from LLM annotators who agreed on 74.0% of question labels, and the contrast describes two parts of the same ideas, not two prompting arms.
- Blind-spot probe. Candidate measure: a Vendi-type diversity score over the probe's questions. Test: portfolios of 300 combinations scored 56.5 for Claude Opus 4.7 prompted independently, 197.9 in one batch, 225.2 for a learned sampler and 250.4 for uniform random; no test or interval [100]. The skeptic: stands; each arm is one portfolio, and the score runs over atom triples from a fixed catalogue, so its use over question embeddings is an adaptation.
- Adjacent-discipline sweep. Candidate: the fields the sweep names are counted over a set of runs, and a variant that states the topic's relational structure before choosing fields is tested. Test: pooled over three models the domain Vendi score was 2.64 with no domain, 2.84 with an instruction to search other domains and 5.68 with relational analogies, and about 5% of generated domains were unique without analogies; each arm carries a 95% interval, and the source names no test for the difference [70]. The skeptic: stands; by model the instruction moved the score from 1.64 to 1.78, from 1.77 to 1.77 and from 1.91 to 1.76, and the collapse is measured within a problem, not across topics.

Two proposals bear on no line by the skeptic's reading. On SCAMPER Eliminate, Llama 3.1 70B chose the additive Lego solution in 19 of 1000 answers and Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-3.5 Turbo shortened most summaries they revised, two-sided binomial tests p < 0.001, while Claude 3.5 Sonnet was additive in 1000 of 1000 Lego answers and the authors conclude an additive preference across most models and tasks [51]; the source compares neither the yield of the seven transformations nor how often Eliminate is skipped in questions, and the proposal picked the arms that fit. On reverse brainstorming, briefs carrying a negative adjective drew ideas from five models that differed from those of positive briefs on every metric, pooled t-tests p < 0.003, with directions shown only in a chart and refused outputs removed [46]; a negative adjective in a brief is not a pass that writes anti-answers and inverts them, and the novelty and rarity that rose are the metrics nearest the claim's measure.
