# Research note — Seams and characterization tests

Topic `seams-and-characterization-tests` of group F. Changing code, domain `change`, id prefix `CHG`. Formulation locators: WELC (Working Effectively with Legacy Code, Feathers 2004: ch. 4 'The Seam Model'; ch. 6 'I Don't Have Much Time and I Have to Change It' — sprout method and class, wrap method and class; ch. 13 'I Need to Make a Change, but I Don't Know What Tests to Write' — characterization tests; Part III 'Dependency-Breaking Techniques', ch. 25); SEG13 (Software Engineering at Google ch. 13 'Test Doubles', where it treats seams).

The canon is Feathers's Working Effectively with Legacy Code: the seam model, sprout and wrap, the ch. 9-12 cases, the ch. 13 characterization algorithm and the ch. 25 dependency-breaking catalog. Its other formulation is Software Engineering at Google ch. 13, where a seam is introduced by dependency injection. Both are trade books, so they are relayed or fetched as formulation only. Seven rules have admissible evidence and can be checked on a diff. (1) A seam choice with a sourced separating condition: code the change adds or rewrites takes the dependencies its tests replace explicitly. The pytest and Vitest docs, java.time.Clock, Google's Go best practices and the Uber Go guide back this. Runtime patching, static setters and module mocks are kept for code the change does not edit, as the Mockito javadoc ('code you cannot change easily') and unittest.mock 'Where to patch' describe. (2) A change that adds pinning tests fixes no oddity those tests pin. This rests on google/eng-practices small-cls, Herzig and Zeller on tangled changes, Mostafa et al. on undocumented behavioural incompatibilities, and the Randoop and ApprovalTests docs. (3-7) Five snapshot and golden-file rules: committed and written only by an explicit update mode, never by CI; rewritten only where the change explains the difference; free of run-varying values; short enough to review; named for their content. These rest on Jest, insta, Vitest, gotest.tools, rustc-dev-guide, Go's own -update tests, eslint-plugin-jest rule pages, language specs on hash and map ordering, and research on unstable, flaky and fragile snapshot tests (Arcuri et al., Luo et al., Cruz et al.). Research also backs the previous version as the regression oracle: Barr et al. survey §5.3, Orstra, EvoSuite and Randoop. It warns that executing code is not yet a net (Shamshiri et al.: 55.7% of faults found overall but 19.9% per suite; Vera-Pérez et al. on pseudo-tested methods). Those findings feed held rules rather than cards. The sources disagree in four places. Henney's essay objects to tests that 'assert that the code does what the code does', which is exactly what a characterization test does. Google's and Uber's Go guides oppose global swaps and test-only interfaces, which WELC's Introduce Static Setter, Skin and Wrap and Extract Interface use. SEG13 and PowerMock call dependency injection optional where runtime replacement exists, while pytest and Vitest say to make dependencies explicit. Go's standard library keeps package-level test hooks that the Uber guide rejects, and no source separates those two cases. No study, official document or fixture backs sprout and wrap, the minimal mechanical dependency-breaking edit, or pinning before a behaviour-changing change, so those stay pending. Several WELC techniques are already carried by shipped cards of neighbouring topics: DSN-12, DSN-18 and DSN-19 for Extract Interface; DSN-35 for widening visibility; TST-06 for Skin and Wrap; TST-08 for where to patch; TST-12 for Subclass and Override; TST-21 for restoring patched state; CHG-04 and CHG-05 for refactoring under tests.

## Formulation layer

The canon for this topic is Feathers's WELC. The book cannot be opened from here, so its seam model (ch. 4), sprout and wrap (ch. 6), the ch. 9–12 cases, the ch. 13 characterization algorithm and the ch. 25 catalog come through verbatim-looking third-party transcriptions on GitHub: the markusi7 book notes, a ThoughtWorks training README, and posts by Seemann and Carlo. Those items are marked relayed and have not been checked against the printed book. Software Engineering at Google was fetched in full from the abseil.github.io raw source: ch. 13 on seams and dependency injection, ch. 12 on unchanging tests under pure refactorings, ch. 14 on A/B diff triage and A-A noise runs, and ch. 1 on Hyrum's Law. Official documentation was fetched for: the language-specific seams (Go Code Review Comments and Google Go style, TypeScript structural typing, PEP 544, unittest.mock 'Where to patch', the Rust book and reference, the go command docs, jest.mock); snapshot and golden-file practice (Jest, insta, eslint-plugin-jest no-large-snapshots, Go's own -update golden tests); and putting tests in a change of their own before a refactoring and keeping bug fixes out of refactoring changes (google/eng-practices small-cls). Not opened: the printed WELC and Fowler's Refactoring (both unfetched), martinfowler.com, and the JSS snapshot-testing article. WebSearch was exhausted, so research appears only as the orchestrator's relayed snippets (Orstra, EvoSuite, Shamshiri et al.). Where the sources disagree: Henney (97 Things #80) objects to tests that only restate what the code does, which is what a characterization test deliberately does. Google's Go guidance (consumer-side interfaces, no back doors only for tests, no hand-written wrappers around generated RPC clients, swapping global state for tests counted as an unsafe API) pushes against WELC's Extract Interface on the implementor, Skin and Wrap, Introduce Static Setter, and WELC's lean toward test coverage over encapsulation. SEG ch. 12's test-via-public-API position pushes against WELC's 'make the private method public'. Martin and Ousterhout disagree on whether mocks change design. Subclass and Override and Pass Null do not carry over unchanged to Go or Rust, and the preprocessing seam is excluded.

## Reception layer

WebSearch was unavailable for this run, so reception comes from GitHub-hosted material. That includes the official guides of Google (eng-practices and the Go style guide), the Go wiki, the Uber Go guide, the pytest, Python, Jest, Vitest, Rust Book and rustc-dev-guide docs, and the docs of the snapshot and golden-file tools (insta, expect-test, inline-snapshot, syrupy, gotest.tools/golden, ApprovalTests). It also includes static-analysis rule pages (eslint-plugin-jest and its Vitest port, Sonar S5803, gochecknoglobals), practitioner essays (Henney and Attapattu in 97 Things, Kladov's 'How to Test', Searls's testdouble wiki, the Ousterhout–Martin discussion, the Mockito wiki, the PowerMock and bouk/monkey READMEs) and three research abstracts. The industry agrees on how goldens and snapshots are handled: they are committed and reviewed as code, kept small and deterministic, never written by a CI run, and updated only through an explicit flag (-u, --bless, -update, UPDATE_EXPECT, INSTA_UPDATE) followed by a review of the diff. Pinning tests before a refactoring and keeping refactorings out of bug-fix changes are both stated in Google's published review guide. Several positions are disputed. Extracting interfaces or wrappers 'for mocking' is discouraged by the Go wiki and Google's Go style guide but recommended by the Mockito wiki, and Ousterhout and Martin disagree on whether mocks change interfaces. SEG13 says dependency injection matters less in dynamic languages, while the pytest and Vitest docs tell users to make dependencies explicit instead of patching. Mockito accepts partial mocks for interim legacy refactoring, which Searls calls an anti-pattern. The Go standard library uses package-level test-hook variables, which the Uber guide and gochecknoglobals reject. Henney criticises tests that assert 'the code does what the code does' and incidental formatting, which is what characterization and golden tests do by design. Jest's docs say to fix a bug before re-recording, which contrasts with recording an observed oddity as-is. Hacker News and Lobsters threads, Kent C. Dodds's and Justin Searls's snapshot-testing posts, and the JSS 2023 snapshot-testing article could not be opened.

## Evidence layer

The language and tool documentation covers each seam form with an openable source in all five languages. Parameter and interface seams are backed by java.time.Clock, Go Code Review Comments and the Go FAQ, the TypeScript type-compatibility page, PEP 544 and the Rust book on traits. Module and link seams are backed by unittest.mock 'Where to patch', jest.mock, mockall with cfg(test), and the Randoop replacecall agent. Research on automated regression oracles backs pinning the current behaviour as the oracle: Orstra (relayed), EvoSuite, Randoop, Elbaum's carving, DSpot (13/19 test improvements accepted) and the Barr et al. TSE 2015 survey §5.3. Two studies warn that tests which only cover code are not yet a safety net: Shamshiri et al. (55.7% of faults found overall, 19.9% per suite) and Vera-Pérez et al. (pseudo-tested methods). Arcuri et al. and Luo et al. tie unstable tests to time, network and nondeterminism. For snapshot and golden files, Jest, insta, Go's testdata and -update convention, and eslint-plugin-jest show that snapshots are committed and reviewed, are not written in CI, and are updated only explicitly. Cruz, Rocha and Valente (JSS 2023, read through the author's GitHub-hosted dissertation) record fragility and blind updating as the main drawbacks. Three relayed candidates were dropped because their snippets do not state a rule claim (Zaidman et al. EMSE 2011, Pacheco et al. ICSE 2007) or their attribution is unclear (LLMShot); the JSS article itself was not opened. No empirical study was found for sprout or wrap, for human-written characterization tests in Feathers' sense, or for recording an oddity without fixing it. That last rule rests only on adjacent evidence (behavioural backward incompatibilities, tangled changes, Google's small-CL guidance). The sources disagree in three places: the Go style guides against interfaces and back doors added only for testing; Wang et al. against hand-rolled subclass-and-override; and Go's global-state guidance and Zhang et al. against replacing global or module state.
