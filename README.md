Skills for the full evidence-based preparation pipeline: research any topic with source verification, map its complete question space before you start, then write AI agent context whose every rule says what it rests on — a tested claim, a norm, a definition or its owner's decision — instead of vibes. [Research shows](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988) that, in repositories that keep their own documentation, LLM-generated context files do not raise agent task success, whose measured change of −0.5% and −2% is not significant, while raising inference cost by 20–23% — and the repository overviews they typically contain are specifically unhelpful. Rooted admits rules by the scientific method: a claim rests on a test that could have refuted it, a norm is named as a norm and a decision as a decision. Software-development and code-review skills apply the same discipline to everyday coding.

- **Rules that say what they rest on.** A rule that claims a technique works stands as a finding only on a test that could have refuted it: a study opened and read, or a test of our own committed to the repository. A rule needed before its test exists is in force as the repository owner's decision, and its claim waits as a hypothesis in the skill's list of hypotheses. `python3 -I plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/scripts/check_record.py --root . --stats` prints the records by kind and the claims by standing.
- **Lean context over static dumps.** For example, instead of an `/init`-style project-structure blob in `CLAUDE.md` (a file class the research above finds unhelpful in repositories that keep their own documentation [[15]](#references)), orientation happens on demand via [`overview.sh`](overview.sh) with an explicit stop-instruction. The agent doesn't preemptively slurp the whole tree into context, so it doesn't fill its working memory with project content it doesn't need and later lose focus on the actual task.

## Methodology

The scientific method judges a claim about how the world works by its test. The claim is stated so that an observation could refute it. It is put to a test that would have found the error, had there been one. Anyone can repeat the test, or open its description, and see the same result. The claim is accepted to the degree, and under the conditions, that the test supports. A new test can revise it.

A claim of any origin is judged the same way: a paper, practice, a model and one's own guess are equal as origins, and a test committed to this repository counts the same as a published one.

The method is taken from the philosophy of science. Seven rules hold what its main lines share, and Rooted adopts them as the convention by which it admits its own rules. Each rule is named below with the thinkers it follows, and the texts are listed under [Scientific method sources](#scientific-method-sources).

### Kinds of statement

Every statement names its kind, and each kind rests on a ground of its own.

| Kind | What it is | What it rests on | Example |
|--|--|--|--|
| decision | a choice: a goal, a priority, a taste, a threshold | the owner's word | "figures on a page are calm" |
| definition | the name of a thing | agreement | "a card is one checkable rule" |
| norm | what an accepted document prescribes: a standard, a specification, a regulation | the document, opened, with its issuer and version | "exit status zero means success" |
| claim | a statement about how the world works | a test | "with this paragraph the agent reads the file before editing more often" |

A norm shows that a practice is accepted; whether the practice works better is a separate claim.

### Standings of a claim

| Standing | What it is | Where it goes |
|--|--|--|
| opinion | no observation is named that would refute it | stays out of the rules |
| hypothesis | the refuting outcome is named, and the claim awaits its test | the list of hypotheses |
| supported | it passed a test that could have refuted it | a rule, with the bounds of its test |
| law | independent tests under varied conditions support it | a rule with wide bounds |
| leading | for a claim about one event: a trace on record tells it from every named rival | a record that holds for that event and against those rivals |
| contested | the tests disagree and no tested condition separates them | the list of hypotheses, both sides recorded |
| refuted | a repeatable test within the same bounds went against it | withdrawn; the record stays |

A fact is the material of a test: an observation anyone can repeat or open. The result of a run is a fact; a conclusion drawn from it is a claim.

### The seven rules

| Rule | What it requires | Check question | Follows |
|--|--|--|--|
| 1. Facts apart from decisions | every statement names its kind and is presented as that kind: a taste as a decision, a norm as accepted practice, a tested claim as a finding | Is this about the world, about our choice, about a name, or about what a document prescribes? | Hume, Popper |
| 2. Refutability | a claim names what is compared with what, by which measure and under which conditions, and the outcome that would refute it | Which result would make us give it up? | Popper |
| 3. Severity of the test | a test supports a claim when it could have come out against it: the same work done with the technique and without it, and enough repetitions to tell the effect from chance, their number fixed before the test; a comparison of shares names its test and its p-value or interval, and the record says what checked that the measure follows what the claim is about | Had the claim been false, would the test have shown it? Does the measure move when what the claim is about changes? | Popper, Mayo |
| 4. Reproducibility | another person can repeat the test: a test of our own runs by a command from the repository, and a published one is opened and its comparison, measure, sample and result are found in it; a source that passed peer review is read by its abstract, and the record says so | Would another person, following the record, see the same? | Popper, the Royal Society |
| 5. Bounds of transfer | a supported claim holds for the conditions of its test; beyond them it is a hypothesis again, and applying it there is a decision marked as a transfer | Does the place where the rule is applied match the place where it was tested? | Hume, Deaton and Cartwright |
| 6. No fitting | the prediction, the measure and the threshold are recorded before the test; the record holds every run, the failed ones included; a condition added to save a refuted claim is a new hypothesis | What was recorded before the result was seen? | Popper, Lakatos, Kerr |
| 7. Revision | a standing follows the tests in both directions, and a test that went against a rule stays on record; a run that failed its own checks moves no standing; a claim about a model's behaviour at the standing `supported` or `law` names the term at which its record is read again | What changes in the record if a new test goes the other way? | Popper, Duhem |

### How a rule enters

Every new statement takes one path.

```
a new statement: which kind is it?
├── decision ────► enters under its owner's name
├── definition ──► enters as an agreement
├── norm ────────► enters as a norm
└── claim about the world
    ├── about what happens whenever the conditions hold
    │   └── are the measure, the comparison, the conditions and the refuting outcome named?
    │       ├── no ───► opinion: stays out
    │       └── yes ──► hypothesis
    │           └── is there a test that could have refuted it, ours or someone else's?
    │               ├── no ──────────────────────────────► waits in the list of hypotheses
    │               ├── yes, the run failed its checks ──► the run recorded, the standing kept
    │               ├── yes, it held ────────────────────► enters as a rule with the bounds of its test
    │               ├── yes, it failed once ─────────────► waits, the failure recorded
    │               ├── yes, the tests disagree ─────────► contested: waits, both sides recorded
    │               └── yes, it failed repeatably ───────► refuted: the record stays
    └── about one event
        └── are the rivals, the trace that tells them apart and the refuting outcome named?
            ├── no ───► opinion: stays out
            └── yes ──► hypothesis
                └── is the trace on record, where another person can open it?
                    ├── no ─────────────────────────────► waits in the list of hypotheses
                    ├── yes, only the claim fits it ────► leading: holds for that event
                    ├── yes, a rival fits it as well ───► waits, the trace recorded
                    └── yes, it goes against the claim ─► refuted: the record stays
```

A claim about one event — what caused one failure, what one change did — cannot be tested by doing the work with the technique and without it, so it takes a path of its own. It names its rival explanations and the trace that tells them apart, and a trace on record that tells it from every named rival gives it the standing `leading` for that event; a script that reproduces the event returns it to a test of one run.

A rule needed before its test exists enters as the owner's decision, and its hypothesis waits in the list for its test.

A claim tested in this repository passes through a test of our own. Its hypothesis is committed first, with its measure, its refuting outcome and its threshold. The test runs the same work with the technique and without it, and the result is committed after. The repository's pre-commit hook runs the record check on the content of each commit. The check passes when every rule line the commit adds or changes in a provenance map or a list of hypotheses carries a record, when such a line holding a supported claim or a law whose scope names a model version listed in [`model-versions.md`](plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/model-versions.md) carries `review`, and when a result follows a hypothesis that already stands in the commit history. A record whose term has come is a `DUE` line of the check's output and fails no commit.

The full method, with a bad and a good example for each rule, is [`references/method.md`](plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/method.md). The [`/scientific-method`](plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/SKILL.md) skill carries the procedure, the fields of a record and the checks.

## Plugins

### evidence-based-authoring

Seven skills, each rule of which carries its record on its line in the skill's provenance map. Three form a preparation pipeline — research → explore → write: gather verified sources, map every angle of a topic, then produce agent instructions whose rules say what they rest on. `/appraising-research` adds a critical-appraisal gate over a finished report — grading evidence quality, surfacing hidden assumptions, hunting competing hypotheses and blind spots, and mapping who benefits. `/reading-companion` is an active-reading companion for a book you study yourself — its methodology drawn from learning-science research. `/handing-over-to-readers` shapes what an agent hands to a human — a report, an explanation, a question, a client's question list — into a handover the reader checks against evidence. `/scientific-method` is the repository's own method made a skill: it takes a statement on its way to becoming a rule through the scientific method and leaves a record of what the rule rests on.

| Skill | Description |
|--|--|
| `/researching-topics` | Research any topic on the web with source verification and critical analysis |
| `/discovering-subtopics` | Build a maximum-breadth question map across any topic using 10 complementary methods — structural decomposition, perspective shifting, causal chains, and more |
| `/appraising-research` | Critically appraise a finished research report — evidence grading, hidden assumptions, competing hypotheses, premortem/red-team, and beneficiary/funding analysis — then rank what to re-check, with adversarial debate on the highest-stakes findings |
| `/auditing-ai-context` | Audit the prose an agent reads and the prose an agent writes: CLAUDE.md, SKILL.md, prompts and KB cards; design docs and the comments attached to code; a drafted answer against the house output-style rules. Purges superseded-design traces after a rework; its trace pass lists the sentences a change added that may argue with the text it deleted, has three independent readers judge each, and keeps a sentence only on a ground a script finds in the document |
| `/reading-companion` | Active-reading companion for a book you study yourself — classify it, scaffold a per-book workspace, then explain intent, translate fragments, walk proofs, and capture your own notes; amplifies recall, never substitutes for it |
| `/handing-over-to-readers` | Shape a report, an explanation, a question or a client's question list so the reader understands, checks and decides — a report told as a story written for the ear, with an opening that gives the whole matter, one picture per part and the decision last, closed by a list of `path:line` anchors and checks with exit codes; sent as text and, where a figure or a choice needs it, as a page with voice that lights what is spoken and takes the reader's answer; an explanation of a mechanism for a team as such a page, with each reader's question answered in its first table; filtered questions as decision cards, reversible decisions taken by default — with a plugin hook that blocks an `AskUserQuestion` outside the card form |
| `/scientific-method` | Take a statement on its way to becoming a rule through the scientific method: name its kind (decision, definition, norm, claim), state a claim with its measure, comparison, conditions and refuting outcome, find or run the test that could have refuted it, and write a record with the result, the bounds and the standing; a claim about one event is weighed against its named rival explanations by a trace on record; a claim of our own enters through a test in the repository, its hypothesis committed before its result — one script checks the record and reads that order from history, a read-only reader answers the check questions of the seven rules, and a second script checks the reader's verdict |

## Skill layout

Every skill directory keeps what a run loads or executes apart from what only a maintainer reads.

- **Run set** — `SKILL.md`, `references/`, `scripts/`, `agents/` and `assets/`, plus the plugin's own `agents/`. `SKILL.md` enters the conversation when the skill is invoked; a reference is read when an instruction names it; a script runs when a phase calls it.
- **Maintainer set** — `maintenance/`: the provenance maps of the card corpora and of the rule files, the lists of hypotheses (`pending-evidence.md`), the quotes checked against their sources (`citations.json`), the taxonomies, the authoring scripts, the unit tests and the maintainer guide `maintenance/README.md`; `evals/`: the eval definitions, fixtures and graded runs.

No run-set file names a path under `maintenance/` or `evals/`, apart from one line of `SKILL.md` that points to `maintenance/README.md`; the provenance map behind a finding's source pointer is resolved by `cardlib.provenance_map_path` alone. A run-set file carries a rule, its check and the numbers it rests on, and no reference to a source: what the rule rests on — the source with its locator, the quoted fragment, the evidence class, the eval that tested it, a fetch status and the record of its kind and standing — stands on the rule's line in `maintenance/<name>-provenance.md`, keyed by the rule's id, so an agent's context holds the rule alone. An arXiv id, a DOI or a URL in run-set prose, a citation bracket, an eval expectation tag or a fetch marker fails the check; a URL a run must open is written as code. A plugin install copies the whole plugin directory, so both sets ship. [`check-skill-layout.py`](check-skill-layout.py) enforces the layout (`--table` prints it per skill), and each skill's `README.md` §Layout maps its own files.

Evaluating Personalized Preference Following in LLMs.* [arXiv:2502.09597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09597) — Ten turns after it was stated, Claude 3 Sonnet followed a preference in 13% of answers without help and in 92–99% with the most similar earlier exchanges retrieved into its prompt or a reminder to consider the preference at the moment of use. One explicit preference per conversation, scored by an LLM judge.
147. Cao & Huang (2026). *Clarify User Expertise: Towards Proactive Conversational Agents Tailoring Responses to User Proficiency.* [arXiv:2608.22266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22266) — Expertise inferred from the query alone was right in 16–22% of cases on simulated users, and by a baseline given the same user's earlier dialogue history in 16–20% on people, against 56–77% on people after about 1.2 probing turns. A preprint: simulated users in the main runs, and ten graduate students role-playing assigned levels.
148. Stoisser, Boubnovski Martell, Boldsen, Märtens & Kitchen (2026). *Ambig-DS: A Benchmark for Task-Framing Ambiguity in Data-Science Agents.* [arXiv:2605.09698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09698) — Under a permissive prompt models asked on 33–100% of fully specified tasks; under a conservative one a model asked on none of the ambiguous ones. A simulated user, one question per task and no earlier answers to look up.
149. Su et al. (2026). *Evaluating LLMs for Detecting Architectural Decision Violations.* [arXiv:2602.07609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07609) — Four models judged whether code complies with accepted decision records with 0.83–0.91 accuracy, the status field excluded superseded records, and 28% of the misjudgements came from implicit or missing context the source code did not hold. Records checked against code; no field ablation, and stale records not studied.
150. Zheng, Zheng, Zhu & Ma (2026). *Is That Clear? The Impacts of Clarifying Questions on Human-LLM Interaction.* [arXiv:2611.01087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01087) — 180 participants: wrong answers accepted unchanged in 39% of cases without and 52% after clarifying questions; a violation next to a question's topic reported in 23% against 37%. A preprint with prewritten answers on everyday consumer choices, two questions and one session.
151. Adamczyk & Bailey (2004). *If Not Now, When? The Effects of Interruption at Different Moments Within Task Execution.* [doi:10.1145/985692.985727](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985727) — 16 students: an interruption at a breakpoint between subtasks drew 56% less annoyance than at the worst moment and conveyed 43% more respect, with no effect on task time. The interruption was an unrelated news quiz; interruptions asked together were not tested.
152. Amatriain, Pujol & Oliver (2009). *I Like It… I Like It Not: Evaluating User Ratings Noise in Recommender Systems.* [doi:10.1007/978-3-642-02247-0_24](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-02247-0_24) — 118 people re-rating the same 100 films disagreed with their own earlier answers at RMSE 0.557–0.765, and 36 of them at 0.68–0.83 seven months later. Preferences, not decisions; noise is not separated from a real change.
153. Silva, Paixão & Maia (2018). *Duplicate Question Detection in Stack Overflow: A Reproducibility Study.* [doi:10.1109/SANER.2018.8330262](https://doi.org/10.1109/SANER.2018.8330262) — A retrieval method reported to find 63.8% of duplicate masters among the top 20 found 43.3% when another team rebuilt it on the same period's questions taken from a later dump, and 38.7% and 36.0% on larger sets of posts. Recall alone, for queries that all have a master.
154. Kluger & DeNisi (1996). *The Effects of Feedback Interventions on Performance: A Historical Review, a Meta-Analysis, and a Preliminary Feedback Intervention Theory.* [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) — 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations: feedback raised performance on average (d = .41), while over a third of the interventions lowered it, the more as attention moved toward the self and away from the task, which is the authors' account of the moderators and was not measured directly. A count kept by the learner himself is not among the interventions; read in the abstract on the first page of the scanned article and in Tables 1 and 2.
155. Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013). *Learning Through Case Comparisons: A Meta-Analytic Review.* [doi:10.1080/00461520.2013.775712](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712) — 57 experiments, 336 tests: case comparison over all other learning situations d = 0.50 [0.44, 0.56], and over the same cases studied one by one d = .37 [.31, .44]; more with prompts for similarities and an immediate test. Read in Tables 4 and 6.
156. Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015). *Effects of Feedback in a Computer-Based Learning Environment on Students' Learning Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis.* [doi:10.3102/0034654314564881](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881) — 70 effect sizes from 40 studies: feedback that explains 0.49, the correct answer alone 0.32, a bare right-or-wrong 0.05. Practice items answered at a computer; opened by the abstract alone.
157. Agarwal (2019). *Retrieval Practice & Bloom's Taxonomy: Do Students Need Fact Knowledge Before Higher Order Learning?* [doi:10.1037/edu0000282](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282) — Higher order and mixed quizzes improved higher order test performance, fact quizzes did not; retrieval practice raised delayed results over rereading. Middle school and college students, the fact quizzes given to the college students alone; read in the abstract and Experiments 1–3.
158. Brod (2021). *Predicting as a Learning Strategy.* [doi:10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1) — A theoretical review: a prediction followed at once by the answer teaches through surprise at a wrong one. Simple declarative material and no pooled effect; opened through a summarising fetch of the full text.
159. Schroeder & Kucera (2022). *Refutation Text Facilitates Learning: A Meta-Analysis of Between-Subjects Experiments.* [doi:10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z) — 44 comparisons, 3,869 participants: a text that states a misconception, refutes it and explains g = 0.41 [0.30, 0.51] over a text that only explains. Mostly post-secondary samples reading one short science text; opened through a summarising fetch of the full text.

### Scientific method sources

The texts the [Methodology](#methodology) follows. Each is a formulation of the method, read in the philosophy of science; the line of each rule in [`method-provenance.md`](plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/maintenance/method-provenance.md) carries the passage the rule follows and how the text was seen. The method is a convention the repository adopts, and what would count as a test of it stands in the skill's [list of hypotheses](plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/maintenance/pending-evidence.md). A thinker named with an encyclopedia entry is reported as that entry reports him.

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Scientific Method.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/) — No single universal method (§6.1); science differs from everyday knowledge by being more systematic (§7); the consequences of an error enter the choice between hypotheses (§4).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Science and Pseudo-Science.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/) — Popper's falsifiability and its critics (§4); philosophers agree on particular cases and disagree on the general criterion (§8).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Karl Popper.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/) — A scientific theory prohibits outcomes; only a risky prediction corroborates; a tested theory is corroborated and never verified (§3–5).
- Popper (1959). *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, selections. [course document, University of Washington](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/roe/Knowability_590/Week1/Popper.doc) — Methodological rules are conventions, "the rules of the game of empirical science"; only a reproducible effect refutes; statements enter science only when they can be tested by others; how an idea occurred to someone is irrelevant to its testing.
- Mayo (2018). *Statistical Inference as Severe Testing: How to Get Beyond the Statistics Wars*, §1.1. [Cambridge University Press, excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811076/64647/excerpt/9781107664647_excerpt.pdf) — The severity requirement: "One does not have evidence for a claim if nothing has been done to rule out ways the claim may be false."
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Scientific Objectivity.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-objectivity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-objectivity/) — Objectivity as testing by others (§5.2); values decide how much evidence suffices and are not themselves evidence (§3).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Hume's Moral Philosophy*, §5 "Is and ought". [plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/) — On the dominant reading, no evaluative conclusion follows from purely factual premises.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *The Problem of Induction.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem/) — The step from observed cases to unobserved ones rests on a uniformity that reason does not establish (§1–2).
- Hume (1748). *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding*, 10.4. [davidhume.org/texts/e/10](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/10) — "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."
- Deaton & Cartwright (2018). *Understanding and Misunderstanding Randomized Controlled Trials.* [doi:10.1016/j.socscimed.2017.12.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2017.12.005) — A trial's result is true of the trial sample; extending it to other groups takes its own justification.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Imre Lakatos*, §2.2. [plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/) — A progressive research programme predicts novel facts; a degenerating one patches itself after the event.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Underdetermination of Scientific Theory*, §2.1. [plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/) — Duhem: an experiment tests a group of hypotheses and does not say which of them failed.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Reproducibility of Scientific Results.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-reproducibility](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-reproducibility/) — Replication (§1); hypothesising after the results are known, after Kerr 1998 (§2.3); preregistration separates prediction from postdiction (§4.2).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Operationalism.* [plato.stanford.edu/entries/operationalism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/operationalism/) — A concept tied to the operations that measure it (§2.1); no extension of a concept without operational grounds (§3.2).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Experiment in Physics*, §2.1, and *Theory and Observation in Science*, §3. [plato.stanford.edu/entries/physics-experiment](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/physics-experiment/), [plato.stanford.edu/entries/science-theory-observation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/science-theory-observation/) — How an experimental result is validated, with no strategy guaranteeing it; observation depends on theory and still tests it.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *The Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge*, §2. [plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social/) — In large experiments researchers take one another's results on trust (Hardwig).
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. *Naturalism*, §2.1. [plato.stanford.edu/entries/naturalism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/naturalism/) — Philosophical issues are rarely resolved by new observational data.
- Russell (1912). *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XV. [wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Problems_of_Philosophy/Chapter_15) — A subject leaves philosophy and becomes a science once definite knowledge of it is possible.
- The Royal Society. *History of the Royal Society.* [royalsociety.org/about-us/who-we-are/history](https://royalsociety.org/about-us/who-we-are/history/) — The motto *Nullius in verba*, "take nobody's word for it": statements are verified by an appeal to facts determined by experiment.
- National Academy of Sciences (1998). *Teaching About Evolution and the Nature of Science*, definitions as reproduced by [Weber State University](https://physics.weber.edu/carroll/honors/scidefs.htm) — Fact, hypothesis and law: a hypothesis is "a tentative statement about the natural world leading to deductions that can be tested".
- Mill (1843). *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive*, Book III, ch. XIV, §4. [7th edition (1868), vol. II, Digital Library of Liberty & Power](http://davidmhart.com/liberty/EnglishClassicalLiberals/MilJS/1868-SystemLogic/Mill_SystemLogic2.html) — A hypothesis is a supposition made "either without actual evidence, or on evidence avowedly insufficient"; a genuinely scientific one must "be of such a nature as to be either proved or disproved by comparison with observed facts".
- Feynman (1965). *The Character of Physical Law*, ch. 7, as quoted at [wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman) — "It does not make any difference how smart you are, who made the guess, or what his name is – if it disagrees with experiment it is wrong."
