# The method

## Contents

- Kinds of statement

- The seven rules, each with its check question
- Acceptance

The scientific method is neither a sequence of steps nor a demand for a citation. It is a way of treating statements about how the world works. A claim is stated so that an observation could refute it. It is put to a test that would have found the error, had there been one. It is accepted to the degree, and under the conditions, that the test supports. It stays open to revision: supported never means proved.

A test is open to anyone: another person repeats it, or opens its description, and sees the same. Who proposed a claim and where it came from therefore carry no weight: a paper, practice, a model and one's own guess stand equal. A citation is the address of someone else's test and never the support itself. A test of our own counts the same.

Some statements are claims about the world, and three kinds are something else. A decision says what we want. A definition says what we call a thing. A norm says what an accepted document prescribes. These three are settled by their own grounds, and each is presented as what it is. A rule of a skill is a prescription, and a prescription follows from a goal joined with the claim that the technique serves it, never from facts alone. The goal is the owner's decision; the claim is what the method examines.

The rules of the method are a convention. No single criterion separates science from the rest, so the method takes what the main lines of the philosophy of science agree on and applies it to every statement alike.

## Kinds of statement

| Kind | What it is | What it rests on | Example |
|--|--|--|--|
| decision | a choice: a goal, a priority, a taste, a threshold | the owner's word | "figures on a page are calm" |
| definition | the name of a thing | agreement | "a card is one checkable rule" |
| norm | what an accepted document prescribes: a standard, a specification, a regulation | the document, opened, with its issuer and version | "exit status zero means success" |
| claim | a statement about how the world works | a test | "with this paragraph the agent reads the file before editing more often" |

A norm shows that a practice is accepted; whether the practice works better is a separate claim. A statute and an industry regulation are norms. A logical inference is worth what its premises are worth and forms no kind of its own.

## Standings of a claim

| Standing | What it is | Where it goes |
|--|--|--|
| opinion | no observation is named that would refute it | stays out of the rules |
| hypothesis | the refuting outcome is named and no test exists yet | the list of hypotheses |
| supported | it passed a test that could have refuted it | a rule, with the bounds of its test |
| law | independent tests under varied conditions support it | a rule with wide bounds |
| contested | the tests disagree and no tested condition separates them | the list of hypotheses, both sides recorded |
| refuted | a repeatable test within the same bounds went against it | withdrawn; the record stays |

A fact is the material of a test and no standing: an observation anyone can repeat or open. The result of a run is a fact; a conclusion drawn from it is a claim.

## The seven rules

### 1. Facts apart from decisions

Every statement names its kind: decision, definition, norm or claim. Each kind is presented as itself: a taste as a decision, a norm as accepted practice, and only a tested claim as a finding. Check question: is this about the world, about our choice, about a name, or about what a document prescribes?

- bad: the house taste "figures on a page are calm" written as "calm figures are read faster".
- good: the taste recorded as the owner's decision, and "calm figures are read faster" as a hypothesis.

### 2. Refutability

A claim names what is compared with what, by which measure and under which conditions, and the outcome that would refute it; a claim that names none is an opinion by the table of standings. Check question: which result would make us give it up?

- bad: "short functions are better".
- good: "functions under forty lines draw fewer defect reports per line than longer ones in the same repository; an equal or higher rate refutes it".

### 3. Severity of the test

Only a test that could have come out against the claim supports it. A claim about the benefit of a technique takes a comparison with the same work done without the technique, and enough repetitions to tell the effect from chance. How many repetitions suffice is decided before the test, by the cost of an error. An experiment that intervenes is more severe than an observation of cases as they came. Check question: had the claim been false, would the test have shown it?

- bad: "ten runs with the paragraph all passed" — the runs without it were never made.
- good: "nine of ten runs passed with the paragraph and four of ten without it".

### 4. Reproducibility

Another person can repeat the test. A test of our own lives in the repository and runs by a command. Someone else's test is accepted without repeating it once the source is opened and the test is found in it: the comparison, the measure, the sample and the result are in view. The author's name and the standing of the venue support nothing. Check question: would another person, following the record, see the same?

- bad: "a paper at a major venue shows it", and the paper was never opened.
- good: the paper is open, and its two groups, its measure, its sample and its number are read from the results section.

### 5. Bounds of transfer

A supported claim holds for the conditions of its test: who, what, which version. Beyond those conditions it is a hypothesis again. Applying it more widely is a decision, marked as a transfer. Check question: does the place where the rule is applied match the place where it was tested?

- bad: a result on students reading pairs of sentences stands, with no mark, behind a rule for engineers reading reports.
- good: the same result stands there marked as a transfer, with the difference in readers and texts named.

### 6. No fitting

The prediction, the measure and the threshold are recorded before the test. The record holds every run, the failed ones included. The observation a hypothesis grew from is its origin, and a later observation is its test. A condition added to save a refuted claim is itself a hypothesis, and it predicts something new. Check question: what was recorded before the result was seen?

- bad: after a failed run the claim becomes "it works except on long files" and keeps its standing.
- good: "it fails on long files" enters as a new hypothesis with a refuting outcome of its own.

### 7. Revision

A standing follows the tests and moves in both directions; the strongest standing is still open to a new test. A test that went against the claim is recorded and stays recorded. A claim is refuted by a repeatable result; a single failure is recorded and waits for its repetition. Check question: what changes in the record if a new test goes the other way?

- bad: after one failed run the rule is deleted together with its record.
- good: the failed run is recorded, and a second failure under the same conditions moves the standing to refuted.

## Acceptance

A statement of any origin takes the same path.

```
a new statement
└── which kind is it?
    ├── decision ────► enters under its owner's name
    ├── definition ──► enters as an agreement
    ├── norm ────────► enters as a norm
    └── claim about the world
        └── are the measure, the comparison, the conditions and the refuting outcome named?
            ├── no ───► opinion: stays out
            └── yes ──► hypothesis
                └── is there a test that could have refuted it, ours or someone else's?
                    ├── no ─────────────────────────► waits in the list of hypotheses
                    ├── yes, it held ───────────────► enters as a rule with the bounds of its test
                    ├── yes, it failed once ────────► waits, the failure recorded
                    ├── yes, the tests disagree ────► contested: waits, both sides recorded
                    └── yes, it failed repeatably ──► refuted: the record stays
```

A claim of our own that has no citation takes the lowest branches: a hypothesis, a test of our own, a record in the repository. An opinion enters after it is recast. Either a test is named for it and it becomes a hypothesis, or the owner takes it as a decision and it stops claiming to be a fact. A rule needed before its test exists enters the same way: it is in force as a decision, and its hypothesis waits in the list for its test.

The record of an accepted claim carries the statement with its measure, the refuting outcome, the test, its result, the bounds and the standing; `references/record-format.md` gives the fields. An independent reader answers the seven check questions, and a rerun confirms a test of our own.
