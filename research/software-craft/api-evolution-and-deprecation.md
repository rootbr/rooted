# Research note — API evolution and deprecation

Topic `api-evolution-and-deprecation` of group C. Interfaces, domain `interface`, id prefix `API`. Formulation locators: SEG15 (Software Engineering at Google ch. 15 'Deprecation'); SEG21 (ch. 21 'Dependency Management', where it treats semantic versioning and compatibility promises); Hyrum's Law as SEG ch. 1 states it.

API evolution and deprecation is about changing a published interface without breaking callers its author cannot see. The canon is SEG ch. 1 (Hyrum's Law), ch. 15 (Deprecation) and ch. 21 (Dependency Management). The official documentation of every corpus language agrees with it on the mechanics. Add rather than change or remove (Go blog 'Keeping Your Modules Compatible', Rust RFC 1105, the .NET breaking-change rules, Eclipse's 'Evolving Java-based APIs'). Compiling is not the same as compatible, because return values, side effects, raised exceptions and defaults belong to the contract (PEP 387, the Go blog's encoding/json opt-in). Requiring more or providing less breaks callers, and the direction inverts for implementors (Eclipse, .NET). Additions break callers that implement a type, match it exhaustively or build it with a literal, unless the type was left open (RFC 1105, Rust reference, Go 1 compatibility). Deprecate before removing, which means: the language's machine-readable marker; a notice that names the replacement and records the deprecation version; a run-time warning attributed to the caller; the old element forwarding to the new one; and no new uses. SemVer's FAQ, PEP 387 and 702, NEP 23, java.lang.Deprecated and RFC 1270 state these, and the lint rules of all five languages check them. The industry disagrees on three points. First, whether behaviour the contract leaves unspecified is protected: Go 1, RFC 1105 and PEP 8 say no; Hyrum's Law, the .NET grey-area buckets and the Linux no-regressions rule say yes. Second, whether a deprecated element must eventually be removed: SemVer, NEP 23 and Sonar S1133 say yes; Java's forRemoval=false, PEP 387's soft deprecation, the Go wiki and Hickey say no. Third, whether a major-version bump is the right signal for a breaking change: SemVer and Go modules say yes; the time-based policies of PEP 387 and NEP 23 and Hickey's 'rename instead' disagree. No openable source gives a condition that separates the cases of the first two, so they stay pending. The third separates on whether the project declares semantic versioning. The research is thin and mostly about Java and npm. Venturini et al. (TOSEM 2023) find that 44% of the breaking changes that reached npm clients came in minor or patch releases. Bogart et al. (TOSEM 2021) find that some ecosystems never break while others break and signal it in the version. Raemaekers et al. (relayed snippet) find that Maven methods are commonly deleted without deprecation and that deprecated methods are never deleted. Ochoa et al., Xavier et al., Mostafa et al., Sawant et al. and Brito et al. 2016 remain unopened.

## Formulation layer

I opened every formulation locator from the CC-licensed HTML of Software Engineering at Google: ch. 1 for Hyrum's Law and the Beyoncé Rule, ch. 15 'Deprecation', ch. 21 'Dependency Management' and ch. 20. I also opened the SemVer 2.0.0 spec and FAQ and the engineering documents on GitHub: the Go compatibility docs and blog, PEP 387, the Python warnings docs, java.lang.Deprecated, the Rust API guidelines and reference, the Google and Uber style guides, the Abseil compatibility guidelines, the .NET runtime breaking-change rules, and the Linux kernel's regression and stable-API documents. Rich Hickey's 'Spec-ulation' transcript and the 97 Things essays were also opened. The aposd-vs-clean-code discussion has no position on evolution or deprecation; its only relevant point is that public APIs need interface comments. Fowler (Refactoring, ParallelChange, Public vs Published Interfaces), Bloch (Effective Java, OOPSLA 2006), des Rivières, Tulach and Reddy could not be opened, so they are recorded as paraphrases. WebSearch was exhausted and the denied hosts held the rest. The canon disagrees on three points. (1) What 'breaking' is measured against: SemVer, Go 1 compat and PEP 387 use the declared public API and say unspecified behaviour may change. SEG/Hyrum, the .NET 'grey area' buckets and the Linux 'no regressions' rule count behaviour real callers depend on, even when undocumented. (2) Whether a major version bump is enough: SEG ch. 21, Go's import-compatibility rule and Hickey say an incompatible version should carry a new name so old and new can coexist. (3) Whether deprecation ends in removal: SemVer's FAQ and SEG ch. 15 remove after a stated period. PEP 387 allows a cheap deprecated element to stay indefinitely, and soft deprecation never removes. Hickey and Bloch say never remove. The sources agree on the rest: add rather than change; warn at the caller's use site; the deprecation notice names its replacement; no new uses of deprecated elements; widening what is required or narrowing what is provided is breaking.

## Reception layer

The rules on deprecation mechanics are uncontested, and tools check them mechanically. Every language has a check for using a deprecated element: typescript-eslint no-deprecated, staticcheck SA1019, rustc's deprecated lint, javac -Xlint, Sonar S1874/S5738, Pylint W490x and mypy/pyright. There are also checks for a deprecation notice that explains itself (Error Prone DepAnn, Checkstyle MissingDeprecated, Sonar S1123/S6355, clippy deprecated_semver) and for warning at the caller's site (Ruff B028, the Python warnings docs, NumPy NEP 23). The contested positions are these:
(1) Whether a semver major bump is the right way to signal a breaking change. Hickey's Spec-ulation, SEG ch. 21, Abseil's live-at-head and CalVer dispute it. Bogart et al. (TOSEM 2021) find that ecosystems differ (Eclipse never breaks; npm breaks and signals through the version). PEP 387, NEP 23, Django and TypeScript remove deprecated APIs in non-major releases.
(2) Whether a deprecated element must eventually be removed. Sonar S1133 and NEP 23 say yes. Java's forRemoval=false, PEP 387 soft deprecation, Go 1 compatibility and Hickey say no.
(3) Whether 'a new optional parameter' is additive. The Go blog says no backward-compatible change to a function signature exists. Eclipse and Griffe show that adding, reordering or re-defaulting parameters breaks overriders or changes behaviour silently.
(4) Whom the Hyrum's-Law obligation protects. Go 1, PEP 387 and Abseil limit it to documented behaviour, while Linux's no-regressions rule covers any practical use case. Linux and Abseil also exempt in-tree or tool-migrated callers from a deprecation period.
Contract changes are role-dependent: Eclipse's table shows that strengthening a precondition breaks callers, while weakening it breaks implementors. Freshness items from 2023 to 2025: PEP 387 now prefers a 5-year deprecation (Jan 2025); PEP 702 and mypy/pyright treat deprecation as a type-checker diagnostic, though not on by default; Go added //go:fix inline (2025); and the Rust project goals (2024h2 to 2025h2) aim to make cargo-semver-checks part of cargo publish, citing violations in at least 3% of releases. WebSearch was exhausted, so Hacker News, Lobsters, the named blog critiques (Ashkenas, Schlawack, Cannon) and 2024 to 2026 practitioner surveys could not be opened. The Ochoa et al. replication and the Raemaekers et al. Maven finding are known only through the orchestrator's relay or from memory.

## Evidence layer

I opened the standards and official documentation for every candidate rule from GitHub raw. They are: Semantic Versioning 2.0.0 including its deprecation FAQ; PEP 387, 702 and 8 and the Python warnings docs; the Go 1 compatibility promise, the Go blog post 'Keeping Your Modules Compatible', the Go module-versioning pages, the Go doc-comment 'Deprecated:' convention, the Go wiki page on deprecation, the x/exp/apidiff README and the go:fix inline analyzer; Rust RFC 1105 (API evolution) and RFC 1270 (deprecation), the Rust reference's deprecated and non_exhaustive attributes and the Rust API guidelines' sealed traits; java.lang.Deprecated and the javac man page; the Google TypeScript style guide; and SWEBOK V3 section 4.1. Four local hands-on runs (Go, Rust, Python, Java) confirmed the key behaviours. They are recorded as unfetched because no reader can open them until they sit under evals/fixture/. (TOSEM 2023: 44% of the breaking changes that reached clients came in minor or patch releases), Bogart et al. (TOSEM 2021) and Decan and Mens (SCP 2021). I dropped Brito et al. 2018 ('Why and how Java developers break APIs') because it reports developers' motives and states none of this topic's rules. Cargo's SemVer reference returns 404 on every path I tried, so RFC 1105 stands in for it. The sources disagree on three points. First, whether deprecated code must eventually be removed: semver, PEP 387 and Sonar S1133 say yes; the Go wiki and PEP 387's 'can stay indefinitely' clause say no. Second, whether behaviour outside the documented contract is protected: Hyrum's Law (SEG ch. 1) says callers depend on it anyway, while RFC 1105 and the Go 1 promise reserve the right to change it. Third, whether undocumented names are public: PEP 8 says no, PEP 387 says not automatically private. Separately, the evidence on how reliably version numbers signal breaking changes is mixed: Raemaekers' 'one third of releases' figure (from memory) is challenged by the Ochoa et al. replication (also from memory), and the critique of SemVer itself appears only as a formulation in SEG ch. 21.

## Spine — candidate rules in source order

1. **Changing the return value, raised errors, side effects or defaults of a published element for inputs callers already pass is a breaking change even when every call still compiles** — For arguments existing callers already pass, a published element's return value, side effects, raised exceptions or returned errors, default values, enum constant values and the conditions under which it calls overridable methods belong to its compatibility promise. Changing any of them breaks callers even though no call site stops compiling. The Go wiki's timing (add the notice only once the replacement exists in every supported release) goes into that card's Limits.
- A deprecation warning prevents some new uses but does not by itself move existing callers off the deprecated element — held-not-checkable: A descriptive claim about the effect of warnings, with no form a finder can check on a diff. It feeds the Rationale of no-new-use-of-deprecated-element (warnings and lint checks stop new uses even though they do not move old ones) and is recorded in the research note. Moving existing callers belongs to large-scale-changes-and-migrations.
- A published interface states its compatibility promise, naming what stays compatible and for how long — held-not-checkable: A project-level statement of policy with no form on a code diff. It feeds the Rationale of major-version-for-breaking-change and the Limits of behaviour-change-breaks-callers (the promise decides what the contract is). Release and support policy is process management, outside the corpus.
- An incompatible version of a published interface takes a new name so that the old and new versions can coexist — folded: Folded into two cards. At element level it is add-new-form-beside-old: a new function or type beside the old one is the rename Hickey and the Go blog recommend. At module level, Go's import compatibility rule (a new major version takes a new module path) is the Go Example of major-version-for-breaking-change. Hickey's position that renaming should replace the major bump is recorded there as the 'rename instead of bumping' position.
- A change adds no new use of a deprecated element in non-deprecated code outside the package that defines it — held-cap: beyond max_rules 12; re-run the topic with a higher cap or a later next_id
- A release that makes a breaking change to a published interface raises the major version when the project declares semantic versioning — held-cap: beyond max_rules 12; re-run the topic with a higher cap or a later next_id
