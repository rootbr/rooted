# The gap between the meaning an author intends and the meaning his addressee takes: evidence report

A literature search asked why an addressee takes another meaning from a professional's words than the author put into them, and which ways of narrowing that gap have been tested. The question came from an essay of a management consultancy: its consultants explain to clients why a process does not work, and a client often takes something else from a sentence than its author meant. Twenty-two hypotheses were worded from the essay and committed before a source was opened. Eight units then searched for tests of them, for and against, and for the norms and practices of trades that cannot afford a misunderstanding; eight further units read every finding against its source. No source tests a practice on consultants and their clients: every result reaches that setting by transfer from laboratory participants, patients, students, survey respondents or software practitioners, or stands as a norm.

Main results:

- Authors expect to be understood more often than they are. Speakers predicted that 72 % of their ambiguous sentences were understood against 61 % that were [1]; e-mail senders predicted 78 % against 56 % [2]; Mandarin speakers 70 % against 44 % [3]; outgoing paediatric interns expected 2.6 important items per patient to reach their successor against 1.6 that did [4]. The search found no test against it.
- Imagining the addressee's view does not make the forecast better, and asking him does. Across nine experiments an instruction to take the other's perspective left the count of correct predictions unchanged and lowered accuracy on absolute differences; partners who asked predicted 8.60 of 20 opinions correctly against 3.95 for those who imagined [11]. A second laboratory found a gain from asking in its study of attitudes and in one of the two asking groups of its study of prices, and none in its study of feelings [12].
- Experts predict few of the problems readers have. Writers and subject experts predicted under 15 % of the problems readers of one brochure signalled [16]. Writing students who read readers' think-aloud protocols rose from 3.25 to 5.25 correctly predicted problems, while students taught by other methods, audience-analysis heuristics among them, fell from 3.04 to 2.46 [15]. Educational or clinical experience went with more accurate judgement at d = 0.12 over 75 studies [18].
- A number in brackets beside a vague word of likelihood about doubled the overlap of readers' ranges with the intended one, 32 % to 66 %, while a table of the ranges gave 39 % and a tooltip 40 % [29]; the word with its number did no better than the number alone [30].
- A person who took no part in the conversation understands its words less well. Matchers who talked with the director placed 99 % of figures correctly against 88 % for overhearers who heard every word [57]; where accuracy was at ceiling, a partner who had only listened needed 217 s on his first trial against 70 s for one who had taken part [58].
- The match of a particular pair is real in ratings and untested for understanding. The pair explained over 40 % of the variance in teammates' ratings of each other's listening [49], and over 100 traits and preferences recorded before a meeting predicted none of the pair's part in a rating [50]. Closeness did not make understanding more accurate: friends were not reliably better understood than strangers, and listeners made more egocentric errors with a friend [5].
- Saying the message back is prescribed by aviation rules and army doctrine and is weakly tested. No trial compares it with the question whether all is clear. Emergency patients noticed a gap in their understanding 20 % of the time when one existed [64]. A review of 18 randomised trials of teach-back found no clear pattern on knowledge and a gain in self-reported adherence [62].
- The essay's thesis that no wording is clear to everyone holds for the amount of explanation and fails for plain wording. Instruction with much assistance served learners with little prior knowledge (d = 0.505) and hindered those with much (d = −0.428) [80]. Contract excerpts in plain English were understood better than the same content in legalese by 105 attorneys and by 108 laypeople alike, with no interaction of version and group [82].
- A language model as a stand-in reader has no test with the comparison a rule would need. In neighbouring tests a model's marks of the terms a given reader does not know scored within three points of marking every term [90], and simulated users found 3 points of failure where ten people found 10 [89]. A person's own earlier answers improved a model's forecast of his later answers [98] [97]; his own publications did not [90].

## What was investigated

Each hypothesis states one comparison with its measure and the outcome that would refute it; `research/meaning-gap/pending-evidence.md` holds the full wording. The scope of every claim is a professional wording a finding or a recommendation for a named addressee of another organisation, trade or department.

- **The author's forecast of his own clarity.** H-MG-01: an author expects his message to be taken as meant more often than it is. H-MG-02: an author who knows more of the subject forecasts the addressee's understanding less accurately. H-MG-03: asking the addressee gives a more accurate forecast than imagining his view. H-MG-04: an author who has studied readers' recorded readings predicts more of the problems readers have.
- **One word, several meanings.** H-MG-05: two adults sort the same instances under an abstract word less often than they expect. H-MG-06: people of different trades agree on a shared working term less often than people of one trade. H-MG-07: a number beside a vague word narrows its readings; its record keeps the share of readings within the author's range, and H-MG-23, added after the independent reading, holds the narrowing of their spread. H-MG-08: an instance from the addressee's setting beside an abstract term brings more addressees to the author's meaning. H-MG-09: the addressee's own word for a thing is understood better than the author's.
- **The pair of author and addressee.** H-MG-10: understanding depends on the particular pair beyond the author's and the addressee's averages. H-MG-11: an author and an addressee of the same professional training understand each other more accurately than a pair of different training. H-MG-12: assigning the author by the record of earlier exchanges beats assigning by seniority or knowledge. H-MG-13: a person who took part in the conversation understands its message better than one who receives the same words.
- **Checking what the addressee took.** H-MG-14: saying the message back leaves more addressees able to state it than asking whether it is clear. H-MG-15: a reader from the addressee's side marks more later misreadings than a colleague of the author. H-MG-16: a core of two or three sentences confirmed first saves rounds of correction.
- **A negative finding, and one text for several readers.** H-MG-17: a finding worded as an observation of a process with its measured consequence is accepted by more addressees than a verdict. H-MG-18: a critical sentence is taken as an attack by a larger share of listeners in an organisation where speaking of problems is less safe. H-MG-19: no one version of a text serves readers of little and of much knowledge best. H-MG-20: a revision to common words, short sentences and the main point first raises comprehension.
- **A language model as the stand-in reader.** H-MG-21: a model reading as the addressee marks more of the later misreadings than the author's own re-reading. H-MG-22: a model given the addressee's own texts forecasts his reading better than one given a label of his role.

## Method

**Order.** The hypotheses were committed to the repository before the search (`research/meaning-gap/pending-evidence.md`), so a source found later is a test or stands beside its claim. Some claims were worded after a published result recalled from memory; such a source tests nothing of that claim, and the record of the claim, in `research/meaning-gap/pending-evidence.md` or `research/meaning-gap/solution-provenance.md`, names it as the one the claim was worded from.

**Units.** Six units searched one theme each. A seventh opened the prescribing documents of trades in which a misunderstanding is costly, and an eighth returned to the leads on words that the second had not reached. Each unit had a brief with the hypotheses, a list of leads marked as unverified recall, and the instruction to look for what goes against a claim as hard as for what supports it. Nothing entered from memory: a unit opened the source itself, and what it could not open it listed apart.

**Findings.** A finding names its source with the URL it was read at, one to three verbatim quotes, the comparison, the measure, the sample, the result, its direction for the hypothesis and how its conditions differ from the claim's. The reading says how the source was read:

- `first-hand`: the full text was open, with the comparison, the measure, the sample and the result in view;
- `abstract`: a peer-reviewed source read by its abstract alone;
- `relay`: a source read about in another text, or a preprint read by its abstract; a relay carries nothing. One stands under H-MG-08, a journal article known through its authors' own conference summary; the others are listed under [What could not be opened](#what-could-not-be-opened).

**The second reading.** Eight further units took the findings theme by theme, opened every source again and gave each finding a verdict: it stands, it is weaker than written, it is false, or its source could not be opened. Of the 132 findings, 69 stand as written and 63 are weaker; none was false, and no source stayed unopened. A weaker finding stands here as corrected: a figure moved to the comparison it belongs to, a sample described as its source describes it, or a direction lowered to a neighbouring question where the source's comparison is another one than the claim's. A script fetched each URL again with a plain GET and searched the page for each quote: of 215 quotes at an address that returns HTML, text, JSON or XML it found 192 word for word; 14 stand at PubMed pages that answer a plain GET with a cookie notice, and the second reading found each of them in the abstract at another address; 5 addresses refused the fetch, and 4 records did not match. A further 76 quotes come from PDF files, which the script does not read; the second reading checked each of them in its file.

**The reading of the records.** The claims and norms drawn from the findings stand as 34 records in `research/meaning-gap/`, 23 claims and 11 norms. Each was read by a reader that had seen nothing of the work, opened the sources itself and answered the seven check questions of the method. A record that failed a question was changed as its reader said and went to a fresh reader, and the 34 records took 60 readings. The readings moved this report as well, since both are built from the same findings. Two tests of the first claim turned out to be sources its measure was taken from. The claim on a number beside a vague word was narrowed to the share of readings within the intended range. The essay's thesis on versions of a text stands as contested. The descriptions of about twenty sources were corrected. Partway through, a separate pass went over the 39 findings read by abstract in the records not yet read and rewrote 38 of them, so that each says no more than its abstract.

**Kinds of source.** An experiment, a meta-analysis, a systematic review or a field measurement counts as a test. A standard, a regulation, a doctrine publication or official guidance counts as a norm: it prescribes and measures nothing. A report of what a trade does counts as neither.

**Counts.** The counts give the findings as they stand after the second reading. A source cited under two hypotheses counts once for each; the list of sources holds 122 lines.

## The author's forecast of his own clarity

### H-MG-01. An author expects to be understood more often than he is

Four studies set an author's forecast against what his addressee took or reported, and in each the forecast was higher: by 11 points for ambiguous sentences said aloud, by 22 for the tone of an e-mail, by 26 for ambiguous phrases in Mandarin, and by one item per patient in clinical handoffs. The handoff study is the only one with professionals, and there sender and receiver share a trade. Overhearers who knew the intended meaning showed no such bias [1], which places the bias in the author. A fifth study shows the forecast above the result in every cell of a figure without testing it, and finds the over-forecast larger with a friend or a spouse than with a stranger.

In the claim's direction:

- Keysar & Henly 2002 [1] (first-hand): 40 speaker-listener pairs saying 16 ambiguous sentences compared the speaker's forecast of the listener's understanding with the listener's choice, speakers forecast 72% understood against 61% actual (t(39) = 4.36, p < .001) while yoked overhearers who knew the intended meaning showed no bias (30% against 28% over- and underestimation, t(36) = 0.016, p = .7), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: laboratory participants of unstated occupation, experimenter-made ambiguous sentences read aloud, a two-alternative choice, speaker and listener of one population.
- Kruger et al. 2005 [2] (first-hand): student senders of short statements compared their forecast of the receiver's correct classification of tone with the receiver's classification, e-mail senders forecast 78.0% against 56.0% actual (t(28) = 3.40, p = .002, d = .61) and over three media 88.8% against 70.4% (F(1, 148) = 118.26, p < .001), with no reliable over-forecast over voice in Study 2 (t < 1) and no effect of friendship (Fs < 1), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: students, the tone of a statement rather than a finding, a choice among two or four labels.
- Lau et al. 2022 [3] (first-hand): 120 Mandarin-speaking speakers conveying ambiguous phrases compared their judgement of the listener's understanding with the listener's choice, speakers judged 70% understood against 44% actual (t(119) = 12.30, p < .001, d = 1.12) and 50% against 35% for listeners who knew no Chinese (t(119) = 5.28, p < .001, d = .48), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: lay participants, experimenter-made phrases, a four-alternative choice.
- Chang et al. 2010 [4] (first-hand): pediatric interns handing over 72 patients compared the items the outgoing intern expected the incoming intern to report with the items reported, outgoing interns expected 2.6 important items per patient against 1.6 mentioned (P < .01, test not named) and the item they ranked most important was not perceived as such for 60% of patients, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: sender and receiver of one trade and team, items recalled rather than messages taken as meant.

A neighbouring question:

- Savitsky et al. 2011 [5] (first-hand): student friends and married couples conveying ambiguous phrases compared the speaker's forecast of a listener's understanding with the listener's choice, for a friend or spouse against strangers, speakers over-forecast more for a friend or spouse than for strangers (accuracy by relationship interaction F(1, 14) = 27.45, p < .0001 and F(1, 11) = 9.73, p < .01) while spouses understood no better than strangers (t(11) = 1.08, ns); Fig. 4 shows the predicted count above the actual count in every cell but the paper reports no test of predicted against actual, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: students and couples, experimenter-made phrases with four meanings, the tested comparison is the closeness of the pair.

Untested: the forecast of a professional for an addressee of another trade; a finding or a recommendation as the message, where the measure is what the addressee restates or does.

### H-MG-02. More knowledge of the subject, a worse forecast

The sources disagree, and none measures an author's forecast against a named addressee's result by the author's level of knowledge. Forecasters who knew a fact were biased toward it when they predicted what uninformed others would say, and feedback did not remove the bias. Teachers in training with more mathematics held a wrong order of difficulty more often, and people who had just studied a set of facts estimated less accurately how many novices knew them, where they had to answer each question themselves before estimating. Against that, laypersons were more biased than computer experts in estimating how widely terms are known. The pooled replication of the adult false-belief effect is small, d = 0.20 against 0.469 in the original.

In the claim's direction:

- Camerer et al. 1989 [6] (first-hand): business students forecasting a less-informed group's mean earnings prediction compared forecasters who knew the actual earnings with forecasters who did not, informed individuals with incentives (n = 19) or with feedback (n = 13) showed a positive bias index w toward the actual earnings with no difference between the two conditions, while uninformed forecasters' judgments were randomly distributed around the uninformed group's judgments (the paper reports no test of either against w = 0), and market trading reduced the bias by about half (significant at 5 percent for three of eight companies) with equilibrium prices between the no-bias and full-bias predictions, both rejected, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: students, numeric forecasts of others' estimates, one known fact rather than subject expertise.
- Nathan & Petrosino 2003 [7] (abstract): 48 preservice secondary teachers judging students' algebra problem difficulty compared teachers with more and less advanced mathematics education, those with more were more likely to hold a difficulty order that contradicts students' actual performance (no statistic in the abstract), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: teachers in training, problem difficulty rather than understanding of a message.
- Tullis & Feder 2023 [8] (abstract): participants who studied trivia questions and estimated the percentage of novices who would know each answer across several rounds of study and estimation, studying the facts impaired calibration and resolution of the estimates when participants had to answer the question before estimating what novices would know, and reducing reliance on one's own knowledge did not restore accuracy (no figures in the abstract), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: laboratory participants, trivia answers, anonymous novices.

Against the claim:

- Bromme et al. 2001 [9] (abstract): computer experts and laypersons estimating how widely Internet and general-knowledge concepts are known among students compared the two groups' estimates with norm values, laypersons were more biased than experts (no figures in the abstract), against the claim, under other conditions: single terms, a student population rather than a named addressee.

A neighbouring question:

- Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014 [10] (first-hand): 3074 adults in seven experiments judging where a naive story character would search compared those who knew which container the object had been moved to with those who knew only that it had been moved, knowledge shifted the judgement with a pooled d = 0.20 against d = .469 originally and the plausibility effect was d = 0.24 against d = .645 (no interval reported for the pooled values), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: undergraduates and crowdworkers, a false-belief story with no real addressee whose result scores the judgement, one known fact.

Untested: the claim's own comparison. The most direct lead, a study of experts predicting novices' task times, could not be opened.

### H-MG-03. Asking against imagining

One series of twenty-five experiments carries the claim, and it is the source the claim was worded from. Its last experiment compares the three arms the claim names. A second laboratory compared asking with not asking, without an arm that imagines, and found a gain in one of three studies and in the optional group of a second. Two further experiments show that doing the reader's task, or seeing what he saw, improved the writer's text or the predictor's accuracy.

In the claim's direction:

- Eyal et al. 2018 [11] (first-hand): 104 couples predicting the partner's ratings of 20 opinion statements compared asking the partner, imagining the partner's perspective and a free strategy, asking about all items gave 8.60 correct against 4.90 in control (absolute error t(99) = -4.89, p < .001, d = 1.85) while imagining gave 3.95 correct, not reliably fewer than control (t(99) = -1.28, p = .20), with a larger absolute error than control (t(99) = 2.20, p = .031), and across Experiments 16-24 imagining against control had a pooled d = -.01 (95% CI -.23 to .20) on correct predictions and a significant loss of accuracy on absolute differences (d = 0.16, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.30), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: couples, opinion items, asking covered the items predicted.

Both ways:

- Damen et al. 2021 [12] (first-hand): in three experiments on asking a target before predicting him, asking raised accuracy against a control that could not ask among friends predicting attitudes (t(156) = 4.81, p < .001, d = 0.90) and in the optional group for price estimates (p = .004), but not for instructed askers of prices (p = .110); in the feelings study asking was open to all 46 participants, and the 12 who chose to ask were no more accurate than the 34 who did not (p = .302), a mixed result, under other conditions: students and friends, feelings, prices and attitudes rather than understanding of a message.

A neighbouring question:

- Traxler & Gernsbacher 1993 [13] (abstract): writers describing geometric figures compared performing a task that their readers would subsequently perform with a control task before revising, those who performed the readers' task revised successfully and the others did not (no figures in the abstract), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: doing the reader's task rather than asking him, text quality rather than forecast accuracy.
- Zhou et al. 2017 [14] (abstract): predictors of a stranger's reactions compared reading his facial expressions with viewing the pictures he viewed, viewing the same situation was substantially more accurate and inferring was overconfident (no figures in the abstract), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: experiencing the other's situation rather than asking him, reactions to pictures.

Untested: an author who asks an addressee about his view of a matter and then forecasts how he reads a text about it.

### H-MG-04. Readers' recorded readings calibrate the author

The essay names wide exposure to clients as what helps. The sources separate exposure from feedback. One classroom experiment, the source the claim was worded from, found that writers who read readers' think-aloud protocols learned to predict readers' problems while writers taught to analyse an audience did not. Feedback on readers' success improved writers' later descriptions. Experts who had no such readings predicted under 15 % of the problems readers signalled, and across 75 studies experience raised clinicians' accuracy of judgement a little.

In the claim's direction:

- Schriver 1994 [15] (first-hand, not peer-reviewed): 117 writing students in ten classes compared five classes who read lay readers' think-aloud protocols after predicting their problems with five classes taught by a combination of methods that included audience-analysis heuristics, peer-response groups and role-playing, accurately predicted reader problems per writer per test rose from 3.25 to 5.25 against 3.04 to 2.46 (gain F(1, 8) = 26.037, p <= .001) with a larger gain in discriminating problems from non-problems (F(1, 8) = 8.752, p <= .018), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: students, freshman readers of magazine science texts, classes as units of analysis.

A neighbouring question:

- Lentz & De Jong 1997 [16] (abstract): technical writers and subject or audience experts predicting the problems readers signalled in a government alcohol brochure were compared with a reader-focused evaluation, the experts predicted on average less than 15% of the reader problems and agreed little with one another, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: no group that had studied readers' readings, a public brochure.
- Traxler & Gernsbacher 1992 [17] (abstract): writers describing geometric figures compared receiving feedback on their readers' selection success with no feedback, writers with feedback improved their descriptions more and later wrote better new descriptions (no figures in the abstract), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: text quality rather than prediction of readers' problems, a success rate rather than recorded readings.
- Spengler et al. 2009 [18] (abstract): 75 studies of 4,607 clinicians compared more with less educational or clinical experience, experience, educational or clinical, was positively associated with judgement accuracy at a small pooled d = 0.12, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: clinical judgements rather than predictions of readers' problems, experience rather than study of readers' readings.

Untested: professionals in place of students; whether years of practice with clients, without a record of what each client took, change the forecast at all.

## One word, several meanings

### H-MG-05. Two people agree on a word less often than they expect

One study measured both the agreement and its forecast, and the claim was worded from it: people predicted that about two thirds of others shared their concept of a word, while two random people shared one with a probability between 13 % and 70 %, depending on the word. Its words were names of animals and of politicians, probed by judgements of similarity. Meanings of abstract words differed more between people than meanings of concrete ones, and an item that leaves more room for construal produced a larger false consensus.

In the claim's direction:

- Marti et al. 2023 [19] (first-hand): 1,799 U.S. crowdworkers judging 20 words by similarity triads, predicted agreement against measured agreement, about 2/3 of others predicted to agree while two random people shared a concept with probability around 14-70% for animals and 13-33% for politicians, with predicted and actual agreement rank-correlated (Spearman rho = 0.45, p < 0.001), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: concrete nouns and politicians' names probed by similarity triads, not abstract working terms probed by definitions.

A neighbouring question:

- Wang & Bi 2021 [20] (abstract): 20 adults judging 90 words in behavioural and fMRI measures, inter-subject consistency by word abstractness, individual differences in word meaning increase with abstractness (the abstract names no test statistic), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: spread of meaning measured without any forecast of agreement.
- Gilovich 1990 [21] (abstract): four studies of the false consensus effect, larger effects on items that permit the most latitude for subjective construal (Study 1) and on a general than on a specific version of the same question (Study 2), while subjects interpreted the response alternatives in ways that reflected the choices they made, and subjects led to construe the alternatives alike tended to make the same choices (Studies 3 and 4) (the abstract as read gives no figures or statistic), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: estimates of others' choices rather than a match on a word's meaning.

Untested: abstract working terms such as "efficiency" or "product", probed by definitions or instances, with the speakers' forecast of agreement.

### H-MG-06. Across trades

No source measures how often two people agree on a working term across trades against within one. The nearest tests compare professionals with laypeople against a key, and they show gaps: between doctors, staff and patients on common health terms, and between surgeons and laypeople on words of probability. Judges and laypeople, by contrast, judged an ordinary word much alike, the judges somewhat more inclusive, and each group split on the hard cases. Two field studies of firms describe the problem without a measure, and one counts its cost: 41 rework tasks in three small software organisations traced to a lack of shared understanding of non-functional requirements, 78 % of them to an avoidable one.

In the claim's direction:

- Hadlow & Pitts 1991 [22] (abstract): 40 doctors, 60 health support staff and 120 patients surveyed by multiple choice questionnaire on their understanding of common medical and psychological terms, significant differences in levels of understanding between these groups, the level of medical education predicting the level of correct understanding, and the widest gap in doctor-patient understanding shown for common psychological terms (the abstract gives no figures beyond the sample and names no test), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: correct understanding on a multiple choice questionnaire rather than a pair match, professional against lay rather than two trades.
- Harris et al. 2022 [23] (abstract): in Study 2, ENT surgeons' and laypeople's (potential patients') interpretations of the ten most frequent verbal probability expressions listed in Study 1, surgeons against laypeople, considerable variability in interpretations in both groups with more in lay participants, whose interpretations were also systematically higher than those of surgeons (the abstract gives no figures and names no test), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: probability words, professional against lay, a comparison of the two groups' interpretations rather than pair matches.

Both ways:

- Tobia 2020 [24] (first-hand, not peer-reviewed): 96 U.S. judges, 232 law students and 201 crowdworkers judging whether 25 entities are vehicles, judges and students against laypeople, no Method by Group interaction (chi-square = 2.24, p = .69) and similar yes-rates with judges more inclusive (OR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.06-2.25, p = .025) and each group split about 50-50 on a canoe, both ways, under other conditions: an ordinary word judged by legal experts and laypeople, group yes-rates rather than pair matches.

A neighbouring question:

- Gotlieb et al. 2022 [25] (abstract): 215 adults who did not work or train to work in the medical field, surveyed by short answer and multiple choice questions on phrases that may have different meanings in medicine than in colloquial English, accurate understanding of the medical terminology ranging from 207 (96%) who knew that negative cancer screening results meant they did not have cancer to 44 (21%) who understood that a clinician calling their radiography impressive was generally bad news, and in each of the paired phrases comparing jargon with nonjargon the nonjargon phrase understood significantly better (P < .001, the abstract names no test), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: lay respondents alone with no within-trade comparison.
- DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus 2010 [26] (abstract): a meta-analysis of 231 correlations from 65 independent studies of team cognition and its relations to teamwork processes, motivational states and performance outcomes, team cognition with strong positive relationships to team behavioral process, motivational states and team performance, and explaining significant incremental variance in team performance after the effects of behavioral and motivational dynamics were controlled (the abstract gives no pooled coefficients), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: team cognition and team outcomes, not the match on a term across trades.
- Werner et al. 2020 [27] (abstract): a case study of three small organizations scaling up continuous software engineering, an in-depth analysis identifying 41 NFR-related software tasks as rework due to a lack of shared understanding of non-functional requirements, of which 78% were due to an avoidable (accidental) lack of shared understanding (the abstract names no statistical test), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: the cost of misunderstanding in software organizations rather than a match on a term across against within trades.

Untested: the claim's own comparison, on business or engineering terms.

### H-MG-07. A number beside a vague word

Two experiments with the claim's comparison and measure support it, and one result bounds it. The number has to stand beside the word: in a table or behind a tooltip it barely helped. The word adds nothing to the number: the combined format did no better than the number alone. What both measured is the share of readings within the intended range. On the spread of the readings they do not agree in kind: one reports less variability between readers without figures by arm, and the other found no effect of format on the width of each reader's own interval. A study in 24 countries, the source the claim was worded from, found the same direction. One study of side-effect descriptors on a medicine leaflet did not confirm the combined descriptors a regulator recommends; its abstract does not report the contrast with words alone.

In the claim's direction:

- Budescu et al. 2014 [28] (abstract): laypeople in 25 samples in 24 countries and 17 languages interpreting the verbal descriptions of uncertainty the IPCC uses, the verbal descriptions against an alternative presentation format supplementing the verbal terms with numerical ranges, the alternative format increasing the correspondence between the public's interpretations and the IPCC guidelines, with the terms better differentiated and interpretations in various languages more similar (the abstract gives no figures for these effects and names no test), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: laypeople interpreting climate statements against the IPCC guidelines.
- Wintle et al. 2019 [29] (first-hand): members of the public reading four ICD 203 phrases (very unlikely, unlikely, likely, very likely) in eight statements taken from US intelligence reports, 236 with the phrase alone against 232 with its numeric range in brackets beside it (and further arms with a table and a tooltip, 924 participants in all), mean overlap with the intended range 32% (95% CI 29-35) against 66% (62-70), with table 39% and tooltip 40%, and best estimates in range 59% (56-62) against 82% (79-86), with table 64% and tooltip 65%, a Bayesian hierarchical model on percentage overlap giving a 95% credible interval that excludes zero for the brackets arm alone; the paper says that variability in interpretation was substantially reduced with brackets and gives no standard deviations by arm, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: probability words of an intelligence standard read by the public in written statements on a screen.

## The pair of author and addressee

### H-MG-10. The pair's share

The essay reports that no consultant is best for every client and that stable matches exist between particular people. Designs that separate the pair from the two people find a large share for the pair in ratings: over 40 % of the variance in how well teammates felt listened to, and 31 % to 36 % in attraction after a short meeting. No such design measured how accurately a message was understood. The accuracy with which a perceiver read a target's feelings depended on both together, an expressive target and an empathic perceiver.

In the claim's direction:

- Kluger et al. 2021 [49] (abstract): in two studies teammates rated each other on listening and intimacy, totaling 324 and 526 dyadic ratings, and in both studies social relations modeling suggested that the dyad level explained over 40 percent of the variance in both listening and intimacy, the abstract naming no test, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: a rated quality of listening among teammates rather than measured understanding of a message across organisations.
- Joel et al. 2017 [50] (first-hand): 163 and 187 undergraduate speed daters in a round-robin of 4-minute dates, relationship variance in rated desire was 31.0-35.98% of the total, the largest component, with error separated (27.3-31.8%), and could not be predicted from more than 100 pre-date traits and preferences, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: a rated attraction after a short date rather than understanding of a message.

A neighbouring question:

- Zaki et al. 2008 [51] (first-hand): 33 perceivers judging 40 video clips of targets' emotional narratives, accuracy depended on the interaction of perceiver empathy and target expressivity (b = 0.07, p < .02) while perceiver empathy alone did not predict it (r = .04, p = .22), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: judged affect from video and an interaction of two traits rather than the pair's share of variance in understanding a message.

### H-MG-11. Shared background

The sources do not support the essay's guess that a match comes from a likeness in how two people think and speak. Friends were not reliably better understood than strangers, and listeners made more egocentric errors with a friend. In a word game friends were more accurate than strangers face to face and no more accurate by e-mail, and a measure of shared knowledge predicted neither accuracy nor efficiency. Pairs who shared more names confused each other more on the names only one of them knew, with no difference overall. A shared native language between physician and patient went with at least one better outcome in 25 of 33 studies, on outcomes that mostly are not measured understanding.

Against the claim:

- Savitsky et al. 2011 [5] (first-hand): laboratory pairs of friends, spouses and strangers, addressees following a friend made more egocentric errors than those following a stranger, and in the full text speakers were not reliably more accurate with a friend (t(14) = 1.81, p = .09) or a spouse (paired t(11) = 1.08, ns) than with strangers while they overestimated their success more with them, against the claim, under other conditions: closeness of the relationship rather than shared training or trade, ambiguous sentences and an object task rather than a professional message.

Both ways:

- Wu & Keysar 2007 [53] (first-hand): laboratory pairs in a referential game with novel figures, directors who shared more learned names with their addressee used more names only they knew and confused their addressees more often over those objects, while the abstract states that sharing more knowledge can be beneficial to communication overall, the abstract naming no test, both ways, under other conditions: overlap of learned names in a game rather than shared training or trade, and confusion over privileged names rather than understanding of a message.
- Pollmann & Krahmer 2018 [54] (abstract): friends and strangers playing the word-description game Taboo via e-mail and face-to-face, when descriptions were sent via e-mail there was no difference in accuracy (number of correct answers) nor in efficiency (number of words per correct answer) between friends and strangers, when descriptions were given face-to-face friends were more accurate than strangers but not more efficient, and shared knowledge did not predict accuracy or efficiency, the abstract giving no count of participants and naming no test, both ways, under other conditions: friendship rather than shared training or trade, and objects described without certain taboo words in a game.

A neighbouring question:

- Diamond et al. 2019 [55] (abstract): a systematic review of 33 studies comparing language-concordant care for limited English proficient (LEP) patients with other interventions, including professional and ad hoc interpreters, 25 of 33 studies demonstrated that at least one of the outcomes assessed was better for patients receiving language-concordant care, 5 of 33 demonstrated no difference in outcomes and 3 of 33 worse outcomes, the abstract giving counts of studies and no pooled effect or test, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: a shared language rather than shared training or trade, and any of several outcomes rather than measured understanding.

Untested: shared training or trade, as against friendship or a shared language, with the author's knowledge of the subject held equal.

### H-MG-12. Assigning the author by the record

One randomised trial tests assignment by a record in a neighbouring trade, and the claim was worded from it: patients assigned to a therapist by his record with earlier patients improved more than patients assigned as usual. For 58.6 % of the matched patients the match meant a therapist with no record of failure in any of their problem domains, and for 9.1 % one with a record of success in their three worst. Over 100 traits and preferences recorded before a meeting predicted nothing of the pair's part in a rating, which bears on what a match can be read from: a record of exchanges, not a description of the two people.

A neighbouring question:

- Joel et al. 2017 [50] (first-hand): 163 and 187 undergraduate speed daters, more than 100 pre-date traits and preferences of both members predicted none of the pair-specific variance in rated desire while predicting 4-27% of the individual components, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: matching by profiles before meeting rather than by a record of exchanges, and attraction rather than understanding.
- Constantino et al. 2021 [56] (first-hand): 218 outpatients and 48 therapists in a double-blind randomised trial, patients randomly assigned to good-fitting therapists by five levels of match computed from each therapist's record with 15 or more earlier cases, from effective on the patient's three most elevated problem domains (level 1, 9.1% of matched patients) down to not effective on any elevated domain but not ineffective on any domain (level 5, 58.6%), an effective therapist being one whose patients on average reliably exceeded their expected outcome, against assignment as usual, general impairment fell more in the matched group (P = .02, patient-level d = 0.75), and a match-level analysis gives d = 1.25, with no P value on the article page, for a match on the top three domains, a neighbouring question, under other conditions: clinical improvement over 16 weeks rather than understanding of a message at the first exchange, and a match that for most patients meant a therapist without a record of failure rather than one with a record of success.

### H-MG-13. A participant against an outsider

Outsiders made fewer placement errors after a recorded dialogue than after a recorded monologue, 1.57 against 2.27. For a deck read by an executive who was in none of the working sessions the results point one way: he reads it as an outsider.

Both ways:

- Wilkes-Gibbs & Clark 1992 [58] (first-hand): 32 student triads arranging tangram figures, a second matcher who had been a side participant needed 70 s on his first trial against 128 s for an omniscient bystander who had watched and heard everything by video (F(1,14) = 9.76, p < .01), 196 s for a naive partner (omniscient bystander against naive partner F(1,14) = 6.70, p < .05) and 217 s for a bystander in the room who had heard every word without seeing the figures, while the 2.3% error rate did not vary significantly by condition, both ways, under other conditions: accuracy at ceiling in an interactive laboratory task, so participation showed in effort rather than accuracy.

- Fox Tree 1999 [59] (first-hand): 160 University of California students as overhearers, each hearing one taped spontaneous dialogue and one taped spontaneous monologue in which a director described the order of tangram figures, made on average 1.57 placement errors after a dialogue and 2.27 after a monologue (t1(159) = 2.82, p < .01 in the subject analysis; t2(7) = 2.36, p = .05 in the item analysis), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: outsiders compared by the form of talk they heard rather than participants against outsiders.

## Checking what the addressee took

### H-MG-14. Saying it back

Every trial of teach-back compares it with the usual instructions, so the claim's comparison, saying back against the question whether it is clear, is untested. What the question misses is measured: patients noticed their own gap one time in five. The trials themselves disagree: the newest review finds no clear pattern on knowledge and gains on self-reported adherence and self-efficacy; two emergency-department studies find gains in comprehension. In simulated crises a receiver who read an item back was 8.27 times as likely to know it later as one who gave no verbal response, and one who merely acknowledged it 3.16 times. A handoff programme whose last step is the receiver's synthesis went with fewer medical errors, as one part of a bundle.

In the claim's direction:

- Griffey et al. 2015 [60] (abstract): emergency patients with limited health literacy randomised to teach-back or standard discharge instructions had higher comprehension of medication (P < 0.02), self-care (P < 0.03) and follow-up instructions (P < 0.0001) by multivariable ordinal logistic regression, with no change in perceived comprehension or satisfaction, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: patients, standard discharge rather than an 'is it clear?' question as comparator.
- Mahajan et al. 2020 [60] (abstract): 483 emergency patients discharged with teach-back or standard discharge in a prospective cohort scored higher on all recall and comprehension domains immediately after discharge, and the share leaving with a comprehension deficit fell from 49% to 11.9% (no test statistic for this contrast in the abstract as read), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: patients, non-randomised allocation, standard discharge as comparator.

Both ways:

- Engel et al. 2009 [64] (abstract): of 140 emergency patients whose recall was compared with the chart, 78% showed a comprehension deficit in at least one domain and they perceived difficulty only 20% of the time when deficient (no test named in the abstract as read), a neighbouring question, under other conditions: patients, self-perceived against measured comprehension rather than two ways of closing an exchange.

Untested: the claim's comparison; an addressee who is the author's client, where asking him to say it back may itself be taken as a slight.

### H-MG-16. The confirmed core first

## A negative finding, and one text for several readers

### H-MG-17. The wording of a negative finding

No source compares a finding worded as an observation of a process with the same finding worded as a verdict on people. The neighbours agree on a direction. Over a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, more so where feedback drew attention to the self. Software practitioners rated a specific and considerate review comment as more valid than a vague and inconsiderate one. Criticism with a note of high standards and assurance was acted on more often by school students. ### H-MG-19. No version for every reader

The claim restates the essay's thesis, and the sources split it in two, so its record stands as contested. The amount of help reverses with knowledge: instruction with much assistance served learners with little prior knowledge and hindered those with much, and an incoherent science text allowed school readers with sufficient background knowledge a deeper understanding than a coherent one might. Plain wording does not reverse: attorneys and laypeople both understood contract excerpts better in plain English, with no interaction of version and group, and a later study of cohesion found the reversal only among less skilled readers who knew much.

In the claim's direction:

- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 [80] (abstract): a meta-analysis of 176 effect sizes from 60 experimental studies and a total of 5924 participants found that low prior knowledge learners learn better from high-assistance instruction (d = 0.505) and high prior knowledge learners learn better from low-assistance instruction (d = -0.428), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: instructional assistance for learners rather than a professional's text for an addressee, learning gains rather than comprehension of one document.
- McNamara et al. 1996 [81] (abstract): two experiments on the role of text coherence in junior high school students' comprehension of science texts found that text coherence improved readers' comprehension, while giving readers with sufficient background knowledge an incoherent text that forced them to infer unstated relations engaged them in compensatory processing, allowing deeper text understanding than might occur with a coherent text, with no figures or test in the description, in the claim's direction, under other conditions: school students, science texts in versions that differ in coherence, an advantage of the incoherent text the description gives as deeper understanding.

Against the claim:

- Martínez et al. 2023 [82] (first-hand): 105 US attorneys and 108 laypeople reading the same contract excerpts in legalese or plain English, both groups comprehended (beta = 0.354, P < 0.001) and recalled (beta = 0.360, P = 0.003) the plain version better with no register by group interaction (comprehension P = 0.638), against the claim, under other conditions: legal contracts, readers grouped by legal training.

Both ways:

- O'Reilly & McNamara 2007 [83] (abstract): college students (n = 143) who read either a high- or a low-cohesion text, the benefit of the low-cohesion text was restricted to less skilled, high-knowledge readers while skilled comprehenders with high knowledge benefited from the high-cohesion text, and the interaction of cohesion and knowledge was restricted to text-based questions, with no figures or test in the description, both ways, under other conditions: college students, versions that differ in cohesion, a reversal confined to one subgroup and to text-based questions.

A neighbouring question:

- Ozuru et al. 2009 [84] (abstract): college students with low and high levels of biology knowledge, the two groups similar in reading skill, read two biology texts, one of them high in cohesion, and answered questions at text-based, local-bridging and global-bridging levels, reading the high-cohesion text improved text-based comprehension, overall comprehension was positively correlated with prior knowledge and skilled participants gained more from the high-cohesion text, the abstract giving no comprehension score by version within either knowledge group and stating no interaction of cohesion with prior knowledge, with no figures or test, a neighbouring question whose direction for the claim stays open, under other conditions: college students, versions that differ in cohesion, a gain the abstract states for text-based comprehension.

Untested: one business document in two versions read by an expert and a lay addressee.

### H-MG-20. A plain revision

Plain wording raised comprehension of contracts for lawyers and laypeople, and a review of plain-language summaries names one study with the same result. The tests cover words and sentences; none isolates putting the main point first.

In the claim's direction:

- Martínez et al. 2023 [82] (first-hand): 105 US attorneys and 108 laypeople reading contract excerpts in legalese or in plain English comprehended the plain version better (beta = 0.354, SE = 0.088, P < 0.001) and recalled it better (beta = 0.360, P = 0.003), in the claim's direction, under other conditions: legal contracts, a register revision without main-point-first ordering.

- Stoll et al. 2022 [86] (first-hand): a systematic review of 33 empirical articles on plain language summaries reports one study in which videos and plain language summaries beat graphical and scientific abstracts on comprehension, and no difference only where framing formats or plain formats were compared with each other or ease of understanding was the measure, with no pooled test, in the claim's direction on one study, under other conditions: research summaries read by often highly educated or selected samples, most comparisons among plain formats.

Untested: the three parts of the revision apart; a consultant's finding as the text.

## A language model as the stand-in reader

### H-MG-21. A model against the author's re-reading

No study sets a model against an author re-reading his own draft, scored by what real addressees misread. - software reliability engineering [117] (first-hand; bears on H-MG-17): a written postmortem after an incident names its contributing causes and avoids indicting any individual or team describes the blameless postmortem as a documented practice and assumes everyone acted in good faith with the information they had; it reports no measure of acceptance or of later action.
- user-experience research [118] (first-hand; bears on H-MG-21): Synthetic (AI-generated) users are used for desk research, hypotheses and piloting an interview guide, never as a replacement for research with real users, for validating concepts or for final decisions. Reports a qualitative comparison: the authors generated synthetic participants for three studies they had already run with real users; it gives no numerical measure and advises supplementing, not substituting, real-user research..
- medicine (surgical residency, patient handoff) [119] (first-hand; bears on H-MG-14): at a handoff the receiver closes the I-PASS sequence by summarising what he heard, asking questions and restating the key actions the programme prescribes the I-PASS mnemonic, whose last element is synthesis by the receiver, and a verbal face-to-face report accompanied by a written one; it reports no measure.
- software operations (site reliability engineering) [120] (first-hand; bears on H-MG-17): a written postmortem after an incident names the contributing causes of the incident and does not indict any individual or team, and is reviewed by senior engineers before it is shared describes the blameless postmortem as the provider's documented practice and argues that blame keeps people from bringing issues to light for fear of punishment; it reports no measured outcome.
- manufacturing (engineers, technicians and assemblers on a production floor) [121] (abstract; bears on H-MG-06): When communication problems arise between occupational communities, members offer solutions that invoke the differences in their work contexts and so co-create common ground about the product and the production process. A study of a production floor that links misunderstandings between engineers, technicians and assemblers to their work contexts; the communities' knowledge-sharing difficulties are rooted in differences in their language, the locus of their practice and their conceptualisation of the product; if members provide solutions which invoke the differences in the work contexts and create common ground, they can transform the understandings of others; the abstract does not name the method and reports no measure of agreement or of its change.
- product innovation in large firms (technical, manufacturing, sales and planning departments) [122] (abstract; bears on H-MG-06): Successful innovators deal explicitly with the interpretive barriers between departmental thought worlds and with organisational product routines in order to link technological and market knowledge. Finds two interpretive schemes that inhibit technology-market knowledge, departmental thought worlds and organisational product routines, describes the differences among the thought worlds and how successful innovators overcome both barriers; the abstract reports no measure.

What these documents share is the direction of the check. The receiver says the message back, or a member of the audience is tested, and the sender corrects; none of them rests on the sender's own judgement of his clarity.

## Where the sources go against the essay or a candidate remedy

- **A match by likeness.** The essay explains a match by two people thinking and speaking alike. Closeness and shared knowledge did not make understanding more accurate [5] [54], closeness raised the author's over-forecast [5], and descriptions of two people predicted nothing of the pair's part in a rating [50].
- **No wording clear to everyone.** Plain wording helped experts and laypeople alike [82]. What does depend on the reader is the amount of explanation [80].
- **Exposure.** Experience alone moved the accuracy of judgement little [18]; readers' actual readings moved writers' predictions [15].
- **A colleague's fresh eyes.** Experts and writers predicted under 15 % of readers' problems [16]; a colleague shares the author's words.
- **Imagining the client.** The instruction to take the other's perspective did not raise accuracy and lowered it on one measure [11].
- **Teach-back as a settled practice.** Eighteen randomised trials give no clear pattern on knowledge [62].
- **A model as the reader.** Its marks of unfamiliar terms scored close to marking every term [90], and it missed most of what people met [89].
- **Mirroring the client's style.** Among 64 negotiating pairs, those with higher style matching were more likely to reach an impasse [48].
- **Early confirmation saves rework.** Late fixes were not consistently costlier in one large data set of software projects [71].
