# Forms of delivery: text, page with voice

One story and one set of pictures serve both forms. The story is written once, for the ear (`references/explaining.md` § The story); each part that has something to show takes one picture, designed once (§ The pictures there). Each form draws the same pictures in its own way.

| Form | What the reader gets | Delivered by default when |
|--|--|--|
| text | the story under part headings, each picture as pseudographics, and the list "Where to look", in the chat | always: an ordinary report, a status, an answer the reader returns to |
| page with voice | the story on a page that speaks each part from a button at its heading; each picture drawn as a figure; the sentence being spoken and the element of the figure it names are lit; a switch where options differ; the decision answered on the page, a field for his words after every part and a comment on any piece of the text; each proof opens in place; on an explanation, a map of the page at its side | the report has a mechanism that a figure shows, or a decision among options; the deliverable is an explanation for several readers (`references/explainer.md`) |

## Who chooses

- The default follows the table: the text always goes out, and the page is added when its row applies.
- The reader's word overrides the default, for one deliverable or as a standing preference: "make a page here", "only the page", "text only". A standing preference is recorded with the reader's other decisions (`SKILL.md` § 7) and read before the next report.
- When the page cannot be built here (no synthesiser, no page publisher), the text says so and goes out alone.

## The page with voice

- **One text.** The page's prose is the story as it is spoken, sentence for sentence. A spoken sentence lands on exactly one place of the page: its own sentence, or the block of a picture that shows what it says, as an option's block shows the sentences about that option.
- **The page lights, the reader moves.** While the voice runs, the sentence being spoken and the element of the figure it names are lit a little brighter. The page scrolls nothing and switches nothing by itself, and it starts the voice only to return what the reader's own comment interrupted: the reader scrolls, switches and opens. The voice is started part by part: a button at the heading of each part speaks that part and stops at its end, and the button of the next part is then marked as the way on. Multimedia lessons cut into segments that the learner paces raised transfer (d = 0.45 in one meta-analysis) with no significant gain in retention; in one study of a narrated animation 11 of 18 children never used its free stop and play buttons, and 10 of 18 had to be told to press Play after the first stop.
- **Calm figures.** A figure is drawn in the page's own palette: neutral lines, one accent, green and red only where they carry a verdict, one colour for what is lit. Each figure stands apart in its own frame. The opening has no figure.
- **A switch says what it changes.** Where options behave differently on one input, the figure shows that input and a switch names each behaviour in the reader's words ("the client repeats the payment", "the client does not repeat the payment"), never an option's number alone. An option that is not built is marked on the figure as how it would behave.
- **The decision is answered on the page.** Each option is a button the reader picks. A field under the options takes his words, typed or dictated. One control copies the answer for the chat: the picked option and his words. The page keeps the answer where the session can read it. The decisions stand at the end by default; one that rests on a single part may stand under that part.
- **Every part takes the reader's words.** After each part of the story a field takes his words on that part, typed or dictated, so he comments while he reads. The control that copies the decision's answer carries these words with it, one line a part, and the page keeps them with the answer. A part that holds a decision takes none of its own: the decision's field serves it.
- **The pause is a button and the Space key.** The button at the heading of the part being spoken pauses and resumes the voice, and so does the Space key once the reader has started the voice; until then Space scrolls the page as on any page. A click on the text leaves the voice as it is: the text stays free for selecting and for following a link. The controls of the voice stay at the headings of the parts. The voice also pauses when he puts the cursor into a field for his words.
- **A piece of the text takes a comment.** He selects a piece. It stays selected, and two small buttons appear beside it without covering it: one copies the piece, the other opens a window under it for his comment, the cursor in its field. For a selected piece the page writes to the clipboard at that button alone: by the specification of the desktop clipboard a selection without a command to copy never sets it. The guidelines of two platforms place the commands for a selection beside it in this way. The voice pauses while he comments; a voice the comment interrupted goes on from the start of the interrupted sentence, and one he had paused himself stays paused. The piece stays marked, and a click on it opens its comment again. The copied answer carries each comment as one line that names the piece and its part, after the decisions and the parts' words, and the page keeps the comments with the answer.
- **A map of the page.** On an explanation a panel at the left lists every part and, under each part, its figures, tables, pseudocode and questions, each with its kind and a short name written for the map; a click takes the reader to the item, and the panel marks the item he is at. In a wide window the panel stands open beside the text; in a narrow one it opens over the text from a button at the top left. The reader closes and opens it, the page remembers which, and the text he was reading stays where it was on the screen when the panel moves it. On desktop websites, navigation kept behind a button was used in 27 % of cases against about half for navigation left in view, and people were at least 39 % slower with it; the interface guidelines of one platform say not to hide a sidebar by default. An overview of a hypertext helped readers new to its subject recall it and made no difference to the recall of readers who knew the subject (an interaction with prior knowledge of Cohen's f = 0.32 in one study of 54 readers).
- **A question hides its answer.** On an explanation a part may close with a question: the answer opens at the reader's click, he marks for himself whether he had it, and the page lists the parts worth returning to and shows no count (`references/explainer.md` § A question after a part).
- **Complete without the sound.** Everything is readable at rest; nothing waits for the player.
- **Proof in place.** Each line of "Where to look" opens the lines around its anchor, the proving line marked. On an explanation a name of the code opens those lines where it stands in the text (`references/explainer.md` § Where to look).
- **Built from one body.** `references/page.md` gives the steps: the body with the spoken text in place, the story the builder writes from it, the voice with the time of every sentence (`scripts/voice_cues.py`), the build that refuses a page breaking these rules (`scripts/build_page.py`), the check of the built page in a browser (`scripts/check_page.py`), the publication and the reading of the answer.
