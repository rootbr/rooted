# Research note — Large-scale changes and migrations

Topic `large-scale-changes-and-migrations` of group F. Changing code, domain `change`, id prefix `CHG`. Formulation locators: SEG22 (Software Engineering at Google ch. 22 'Large-Scale Changes': what a large-scale change is, why atomic changes are hard, the infrastructure and the process); SW3.8 (SWEBOK Guide V4.0 ch. 4 'Software Construction' §3.8).

Large-scale changes and migrations covers a change that is logically one but cannot land as one reviewable change. The canon is SEG ch. 22, read with Cox's 'Codebase Refactoring' (2016), which SEG cites, and SEG ch. 15. It agrees on four points: (1) the edit is generated by tools; (2) it is split into shards that each land, build and pass alone; (3) old and new coexist through an alias or a forwarding definition while callers move; (4) new uses of the old path are blocked until it is gone. The official documentation of the five languages writes down the same practice: Go's type-alias design, the alias-names and go fix blogs and the inline analyzer; the Rust edition guide; PEPs 236 and 562; TypeScript aliases; the Rust reference's re-exports; Error Prone InlineMe. Each language also has an analyzer rule that can ban an old module or API (ESLint no-restricted-imports with bulk suppressions, Ruff TID251, clippy disallowed_methods, depguard, Checkstyle IllegalImport). The industry disputes three points. First, whether a codebase-wide change must be split at all. Potvin and Levenberg (relayed) count atomic changes as a monorepo advantage, and eng-practices accepts one large change from a fully trusted tool. SEG, Cox and the Go alias-names blog say that past some scale an atomic change becomes impractical. No openable source gives a condition a finder can test on a diff, so that rule is pending. Second, whether a name- and syntax-aware tool is required. facebook/codemod, Comby ('Sometimes, yes') and SEG accept text substitution when every match is reviewed or every match denotes the target, for example after an alias has made the names one type. That acceptance is the rule's separating condition. Third, whether the old path must be removed. Go's compatibility promise forbids removing a published name, and the Go blogs state the separating condition themselves: delete once callers have moved, unless backward compatibility or a stable published API requires keeping it. The research is thin. Piranha (ICSE-SEIP 2020, fetched in full) measures AST-generated cleanup of stale flags at Uber. It states what retiring a flag removes and that every flag needs an owner and an expiry date. LASE (ICSE 2013) shows that developers miss locations of a systematic edit made by hand. ClangMR, Potvin and Levenberg, Ivers et al., Dig and Johnson, and Kim et al. are relayed snippets only. No study compares atomic with sharded changes, or regex with AST rewrites. Nine rules are carded. Six candidates are folded into them, three are pending and two are held. The orchestrator's local hands-on runs (a text versus binding-aware rename, an alias versus a distinct type in five languages, and an AST round-trip that drops comments) support three cards. The runs live only under /tmp/craft-research/chg/lsc/handson and must be committed under the skill's evals/fixture/ before any card cites them. Ownership overlaps that the spine flags: the shipped API-22, API-26 and API-31 (add beside, deprecate before removing, forward to the replacement) cover published elements, while this topic's coexistence and deletion cards cover callers inside the codebase. The technical-debt topic should fold its rolled-out-flag item into retired-flag-removes-its-dead-branch-and-tests.

Fowler's Refactoring, Sato's ParallelChange, Hodgson's Feature Toggles and Feathers' WELC are also from memory only. ClangMR and Potvin/Levenberg rest on the orchestrator's relayed snippets. The canon agrees on four points: the work is generated by tools; it is split into shards that each land, build and pass alone; old and new coexist through an alias or forwarding definition; and later uses of the old path are blocked. It qualifies or disputes four others. (1) It does not reject plain text substitution outright: SEG22 accepts sed for smaller changes and finished scoped_ptr with a textual substitution once the alias made the two names identical. (2) Atomic change is the 'conventional wisdom' in Cox, and Potvin lists it as a monorepo advantage, while SEG22 says the largest possible atomic change shrinks as a codebase grows. (3) The 97 Things essay 'Don't Be Afraid to Break Things' tolerates temporary breakage, against eng-practices 'Don't Break the Build' and Branch by Abstraction's rule that no commit breaks the build. (4) Removing the old path holds only when no compatibility promise forces it to stay (Go 1 compatibility blocks stage 3; SEG22 lets 'done' range from full removal to leaving old uses to disappear). Whitespace in quotes taken from HTML is collapsed to single spaces and footnote digits are dropped.

## Reception layer

The industry mostly agrees on gradual migration. Russ Cox's 2016 "gradual code repair" (introduce an interchangeable new API, convert callers across as many commits as needed, then remove the old one) matches SEG ch. 22, the 2026 Go blog posts on //go:fix inline, Error Prone @InlineMe, the Rust edition guide and the Python porting HOWTO: the old and new paths coexist, with the old expressed in terms of the new. Each of the five target languages has a mechanical check that blocks new uses of the old path: typescript-eslint no-deprecated, ESLint no-restricted-imports and bulk suppressions, PEP 702, Ruff TID251 and Pylint deprecated-*, clippy disallowed_methods and the rustc deprecated lint, staticcheck SA1019, and Sonar S1874/S1133 plus Error Prone InlineMeInliner. Three candidate rules are contested. (1) One atomic change versus a split: Potvin & Levenberg list atomic changes as a monorepo advantage, eng-practices accepts one large CL from a fully trusted tool, and Cox shows that a moved type with no alias forces an atomic repair. (2) A syntax-aware tool versus text substitution: Facebook's codemod/fastmod and SEG itself accept regex with review for smaller changes, comby answers "Sometimes, yes", OpenRewrite says syntax alone is not enough without type attribution, and Bowler/Rerast show that the tools themselves go stale. (3) "Finish by removing the old path": Go's compatibility promise forbids removing published names, SEG admits that definitions of done vary, Hammant allows keeping the abstraction, and Kubernetes waits three releases before removing a gate; the flag-removal sources themselves (Piranha, trunkbaseddevelopment.com, Kubernetes) agree that a flag is temporary and needs an owner and a removal date. WebSearch was exhausted and HN, Lobsters and martinfowler.com are denied, so no forum threads, no Fowler/Sato or Hodgson text and no 2024–2026 reports on LLM-assisted migration could be opened. ## Evidence layer

Fetched and reviewed: the Piranha paper (author PDF in uber/piranha), the NWIT bib abstracts for LASE, PyEvolve, SOAR and Golubev et al., and official tool and language docs (gopls, gofmt, the Go inline analyzer, the Go type-alias design doc, the Rust edition guide, PEPs 236 and 562, TS allowJs, LibCST, jscodeshift, OpenRewrite, Error Prone Refaster and InlineMe, and the Abseil philosophy page). Also fetched: lint rules that ban an old API across all five languages, eng-practices small-cls and SWEBOK V3, plus my own runs in Go, TypeScript, Python, Rust and Java. In those runs, text substitution of a free function's name silently changed program output and renamed unrelated same-named symbols. A binding-aware rename did neither. An old name kept as an alias interoperated in both directions in Go, Rust, TS and Python, but not in Java, where a subclass shim fails to compile in one direction. The evidence is strongest for coexistence (alias or forwarding, then remove) and for finishing the migration: Piranha measures stale-flag debt and its cleanup at Uber (1381 flags, 65% of diffs landed with no changes, over 85% compile and pass). The claim "syntax-aware tool rather than text substitution" is backed by tool documentation, hands-on runs and studies of how error-prone manual systematic edits are, but no study compares tools against regex directly. It is also contested: SEG22, Comby and Facebook's codemod and fastmod accept regex for small or interactively reviewed changes. Sharding is backed by eng-practices, SWEBOK V3 incremental integration and the Rust per-package migration. eng-practices allows large changes generated by a trusted tool, and Potvin and Levenberg (relayed) count atomic changes as a monorepo advantage. No study of reverts or atomic-versus-sharded changes was found. ClangMR, Dig and Johnson, Kim et al. 2011, Ivers et al. and Potvin and Levenberg are relayed only (WebSearch exhausted, no GitHub copy found). Sawant et al. and SWEBOK V4 §3.8 are unfetched and cannot anchor a card. The Russ Cox refactor article and SEG22 are formulation only.

## Spine — candidate rules in source order

1. **A change that spans more files, packages or owners than can be built, tested, reviewed and merged as one unit is split into independently landing shards rather than forced into one atomic commit** — A change whose edits are logically one but span more files, packages or owners than can practically be built, tested, reviewed and merged as one unit is split into shards that land one at a time rather than prepared and submitted as one atomic commit. One side holds that the largest practical atomic change shrinks as a codebase and its contributors grow; the other counts atomic cross-codebase changes as an advantage of a single repository and accepts one large change generated by a fully trusted tool.

3. **A change that renames, moves, re-signs or removes an element without updating every use of it keeps the old form working beside the new one until no use of the old form remains** — When a change renames, moves, re-signs or removes an element and leaves some of its uses to later changes, the old form stays declared and working beside the new one, as an alias, a re-export or a forwarding definition. The system then builds and runs after each change, and the remaining uses can move in separate changes. The old form is removed only once no use of it remains, counting uses in code the rewriting tool did not process: code built only under another configuration or platform, macro bodies and documentation examples. A change that updates the declaration and every use together meets the rule, and an element whose callers lie outside the codebase falls under deprecation policy instead.
