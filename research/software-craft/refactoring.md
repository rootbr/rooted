# Research note — Refactoring

Topic `refactoring` of group F. Changing code, domain `change`, id prefix `CHG`. Formulation locators: CC24 (Code Complete 2nd ed. ch. 24 'Refactoring': kinds of software evolution, reasons to refactor, specific refactorings, refactoring safely, refactoring strategies); PP 'Refactoring' (The Pragmatic Programmer, 20th anniversary ed., ch. 7 'While You Are Coding', topic 'Refactoring'); the 97 Things essay 'Before You Refactor' (97-things/97-things-every-programmer-should-know en/thing_06/README.md).

The canon agrees on one core. CC2 ch. 24, PP Topic 40 and 97 Things 'Before You Refactor' all say this, as do Fowler, Clean Code ch. 14, Beck, SWEBOK V3 and Google's eng-practices as further formulations. Refactoring is a behaviour-preserving restructuring, done under tests, in small steps, apart from adding features, and mostly as part of the work at hand. The canon disagrees in four places. (1) Rewriting: Spolsky and Attapattu say do not rewrite; CC2, Fowler, Homer and matklad say rewrite when refactoring is the wrong tool. (2) Temporary breakage: Lewis tolerates it; Fowler, Clean Code and gopls do not. (3) Whether every change must improve the design: the Boy Scout Rule, APOSD and CC2's Cardinal Rule say yes; Attapattu asks 'if it isn't broken, why fix it?'. (4) Preparatory versus free-standing refactoring. The evidence supports five cards a finder can check on a diff. First, behaviour preservation. Alves et al. (TSE 2018) found that testing alone located 13 percent of anomalies seeded into manual refactorings. Refactoring engines, lint fixes and LLMs are all documented changing behaviour (Soares TSE 2013, Ruff fix safety, Go inline docs, arXiv:2411.04444). Second, tests in place before a pure refactoring (google/eng-practices small-cls.md; SWEBOK V3 ch. 5). Third, a pure refactoring leaves test expectations unchanged (SWEBOK V3 ch. 4 §2.2.5; Kashiwa et al. ICSME 2021, who show that only interface-changing refactorings routinely break tests). Fourth, a rename also covers the uses resolved at run time: the gopls docs admit that a rename can cause dynamic errors through reflection, encoding/json and text/template, rust-analyzer gives up on macros, and Ge et al. ICSE 2012 describe chasing error messages as error-prone. Fifth, extra care with hierarchy-changing refactorings. Bavota et al. (SCAM 2012) found Pull Up Method and other hierarchy refactorings induce faults very frequently. Di Penta et al. (ESEC/FSE 2020) confirmed the association on 103 systems while only partly attributing the bugs to the refactorings. PEP 698 reports production outages from renamed overridden methods. Four rules are held because a diff cannot show them: one transformation at a time with tests between steps (no study, and the steps leave no trace), tool-performed over hand edits (a diff does not record who or what made the edit), refactoring to prepare the change at hand (contested by Kim et al. FSE 2012's dedicated Windows effort, and intent leaves no trace), and the always-improve rule. Two stay pending. Rewrite versus refactor has no study; It Will Never Work in Theory lists it as an open question. Clean-up-now (eng-practices pushback) conflicts with Google's style-guide TODO for short-term code, and no source separates the two. Almost all research is on Java, and SWEBOK V3 items must be re-anchored to V4.0. Cross-language reach rests on official documentation for Python, TypeScript, Go and Rust.

## Formulation layer

The canon agrees on one core position for refactoring: it is a transformation that preserves behaviour. You do it with tests already passing, in small steps taken one at a time, retesting between steps. You keep it apart from adding functionality, and you do it mostly as preparation for the change at hand rather than as a dedicated project. CC2 ch. 24, PP Topic 40, Fowler, Clean Code ch. 14, Beck, the eng-practices small-CL guide, SWE at Google and gopls all state this. The 97 Things essays, the aposd-vs-clean-code discussion, eng-practices, gopls, SWEBOK V3, SWE at Google and the Go refactoring article were fetched verbatim. CC2, PP, Fowler 2nd ed., Clean Code, Tidy First?, APOSD and Spolsky were not openable as books, so they are quoted as relayed from third-party GitHub copies or notes. WELC and Fowler's 'Automated Refactorings' section are unfetched paraphrases, and WebSearch was not used. The canon disagrees in three places. First, rewriting: Spolsky and 97 Things say do not rewrite, while CC2 and Fowler say rewrite when refactoring is the wrong tool. Second, whether the code may be broken mid-refactoring: 97 Things 'Don't Be Afraid to Break Things' says yes, while Fowler, Clean Code and gopls say keep it working or compiling. Third, when to refactor: Ousterhout says TDD gives no guidance on when or what to refactor, while Martin says the TDD literature discourages delaying it. The Boy Scout and APOSD 'always improve' rule also sits uneasily with 97 Things 'if something isn't broken, why fix it?'. No canon text names hierarchy-changing refactorings as fault-prone. The closest are CC2's 'adjust your approach to the riskiness of the refactoring' and Fowler's Pull Up Method mechanics, so the hierarchy claim has to rest on the evidence layer (Bavota et al. SCAM 2012; Di Penta et al. ESEC/FSE 2020).

## Reception layer

The reception mostly agrees with the canon that refactoring preserves behaviour, goes in small steps and runs under a safety net. It disputes three of the topic's positions. (1) Whether tests are the safety net. Chris James, Fowler (relayed) and SWE at Google say tests are required. Provable-refactorings (Belshee et al.) relies on no tests at all. Feathers (relayed) allows tool refactoring without tests if you know what the tool checks. PEP 698 notes that tests are less likely to catch a broken override after a rename. (2) Whether refactoring should only prepare the change at hand. Beck (relayed), Arlo's notation, Silva et al. (relayed) and PEP 8 support preparatory refactoring. Martin's Boy Scout Rule, Lewis's hygiene list, Homer's "type it in over again" and matklad's weekend rewrites (2026) go further. Attapattu, Spolsky (relayed) and Abramov push back. (3) How cleanly refactoring tools perform. Tool documentation admits the gaps itself: gopls rename can introduce runtime errors through reflection, gopls extract is "considerably less rigorous", rust-analyzer gives up on macros, and Arlo's notation says provable safety is hard in dynamic languages. Two research items show tools and LLMs both produce unsafe refactorings: TOSEM 2025 found refactoring-engine bugs, and arXiv:2411.04444 (2024) found unsafe LLM refactorings. Tools check hierarchy-changing refactorings mechanically in every target language: Java through Error Prone and PMD MissingOverride, TypeScript through noImplicitOverride, Python through PEP 698 with mypy and pyright (both off by default), Go through compile-time interface assertions, and Rust through E0407. Bavota's hierarchy-fault finding is relayed and partly qualified by the Di Penta 2020 replication. I could not open Hacker News, Lobsters, Fowler's bliki, Ron Jeffries' site or any 2024–2026 practitioner survey; WebSearch was exhausted and those hosts are denied. Third-party GitHub copies of Beck, Fowler, Feathers, Spolsky and Tidy First are marked relayed.

## Evidence layer

The strongest evidence covers three rules. First, refactorings that change a class hierarchy carry extra fault risk: Bavota et al. (SCAM 2012) found this in 3 systems and Di Penta et al. (FSE 2020) replicated it on 103, though their manual review 'partially demystifies' the link. Cedrim et al. (FSE 2017) and Sellitto et al. (SANER 2022) add costs to design and readability. Second, tool-performed refactoring beats hand edits for complex changes (Ge et al. ICSE 2012, Vakilian et al. ECOOP 2013, gopls docs), but the tools themselves have bugs and developers trust them unchecked (Soares et al. TSE 2013, Daniel et al. FSE 2007, Vakilian et al. ICSE 2012). Third, in practice refactoring mostly serves the change being made (Silva et al. FSE 2016, Murphy-Hill et al. ICSE 2009, Google eng-practices). 'Refactor only under passing tests' rests on documentation (Google eng-practices, SWEBOK V3 ch. 5), and research qualifies it: renames and signature changes break tests (Kashiwa et al. ICSME 2021), and testing alone missed most seeded errors in hand-made refactorings (Alves et al. TSE 2018). Rachatasumrit & Kim (ICSM 2012) could not be opened. No study was found that tests 'one transformation at a time, tests between steps' or compares big-bang rewrites with incremental refactoring; both rest on formulation only. Studies disagree on whether refactoring pays off: Kim et al. FSE 2012 and Moser et al. 2008 report benefits, while Al Dallal & Abdin TSE 2018, Cedrim 2017, Weißgerber & Diehl MSR 2006, Kim et al. ICSE 2011 and Kim et al.'s own TSE 2014 extension report mixed or negative effects. WebSearch was unavailable, so paper abstracts were opened from GitHub-hosted copies (author pages, author-group bibliographies, metadata datasets, neverworkintheory sources). Almost all of the evidence comes from Java projects.

## Spine — candidate rules in source order

1. **Every change to existing code leaves the internal quality of the code it touches at least a little better than before** — Each modification is treated as an opportunity to improve the design of the code it touches, so that no change degrades internal quality and most changes improve it a little (CC2's Cardinal Rule of Software Evolution, the Boy Scout Rule, APOSD's 'stay strategic'). The research is split. A designated team's multi-year refactoring of Windows 7 modules was followed by fewer inter-module dependencies and post-release defects. A review of 76 studies finds it false that refactoring always improves all quality aspects, and in a 23-project study 33.3% of refactorings introduced new smells.

13. **A behaviour-preserving transformation is made with a refactoring tool where one supports it rather than by hand, and the tool's output is still tested and reviewed** — Where a refactoring tool supports a transformation (rename, extract, inline, move), the developer uses it rather than editing by hand. Complex refactorings are composed from small, predictable tool steps, and LLM-suggested refactorings are made safe by re-applying them through a tested engine. Tools differ in rigour and have bugs: gopls's Extract is less rigorous than its Rename and Inline, the Eclipse, NetBeans and IntelliJ engines have shipped behaviour-changing bugs, and developers rarely preview automated refactorings.
