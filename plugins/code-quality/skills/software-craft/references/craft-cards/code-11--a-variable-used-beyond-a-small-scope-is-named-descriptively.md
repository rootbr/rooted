# A variable, constant or field used beyond a small scope is named descriptively, never with a single letter, an unfamiliar abbreviation or a short generic word

## Thesis
A name's descriptiveness is proportional to its scope of visibility and to the distance between its declaration and its uses. A single letter or a short popular word that could name many things (`g`, `open`, `kNum`) stays inside a small scope: one or two small operations, about 1 to 7 lines as a rough baseline rather than a strict rule, and 10 lines or fewer at the outside. A global, module-level or exported variable or constant, a field, and a local used farther from its declaration than that carry a descriptive name, whole words or an abbreviation widely known outside the project, never a contraction made by dropping letters from a word (`cnt`, `cstmr`) unless that shortened form is itself widely known with few alternate interpretations (`db` for the one database in use), that makes the value's purpose clear to a reader who meets it away from the code that explains it. A scope holding many variables, or variables for similar values or concepts, may call for longer names than its size alone suggests.

## Rationale
A broadly visible name is read in another routine, another file or another module, where the name is all the reader has; the larger the scope, the greater the chance of confusion and of an unintended clash with another name. A global can be used across multiple files, so a short global name costs at any distance, while a local's cost grows with the lines between its declaration and its last use. The rule of thumb sizes a name in proportion to its scope and in inverse proportion to the number of times it is used within that scope. The rough baseline behind the numbers: a small scope is one or two small operations, say 1 to 7 lines; a medium scope a few small or one large operation, 8 to 15 lines; a large scope 15 to 25 lines; a very large scope more than a page, over 25 lines, where a file-level variable may need several words. A name clear in a small scope, `c` for a counter, can be insufficient in a larger one and then needs clarification further along. Dropping letters to save typing (`cstmr`, `cnt`) leaves the reader to reconstruct the word; the cost is highest on exported names, which readers outside the project meet. Descriptive is not long: a name that repeats what the immediate context already states, the enclosing routine or the type, distracts. The property restored is that each name read at a distance identifies its value without a trip back to its declaration.

## Example
```java
bad:  private int cnt;
      private static final int NUM = 5;
good: private int activeSessionCount;
      private static final int MAX_LOGIN_ATTEMPTS = 5;
```

## Limits
Conventional short names hold within their small scope: a loop counter or index (`i`, `j`, `k`), a coordinate (`x`, `y`), a caught exception (`e`), a file handle in a scoped block (`f`) and a discard (`_`); a conventional name spread across multiple nested scopes is likely too vague again. In mathematically heavy code, a short name matching the established notation of a reference paper or algorithm holds when a comment cites that source or documents the notation, though a descriptive name is preferred for an exported name, which readers are much more likely to meet out of context. A one- or two-letter method receiver holds however long its method, since it appears on almost every line of the type's methods, and a single-capital-letter type parameter (`T`) holds across the routine, type or module that declares it wherever the language's documented convention admits one; some conventions ask a descriptive name of a constrained or externally visible type variable. An abbreviation widely known outside the project with few alternate interpretations (`id`, `url`, `dns`, `fqdn`, `db` for the one database in use) stays clear in a large scope, and so does a vocabulary name reused so widely that it is always in context and documented at length. The rule asks for no length in a small scope: `loop_counter` for a local counter, or a name restating the routine it sits in, is the opposite cost. A small scope admits a single letter or a short popular word, not a contraction made by dropping letters from a word; whether such a contraction is acceptable inside a small scope is outside this rule. A short-name allowlist or line-count threshold in the project's documented linter configuration rejects the finding for the names and distances it covers. The name of a routine or a type, the casing convention and a name that contradicts what its code does are outside this rule.

## Validator
Grep the hunk for added declarations whose name is one to three characters, a word with letters dropped, or a short popular word (`data`, `val`, `tmp`, `num`, `open`): at file or module level, as a field or class constant, as an exported variable or constant, and as a local anywhere in a routine. Open the file and settle, for each candidate, its visibility (global, module-level, exported, field, local); for a local, count the lines from its declaration to its last use and the other variables in that scope, and note whether similar concepts share it. Drop a conventional short name within its small scope, an unexported notation name whose source or notation a comment documents, a method receiver, a type parameter the language's convention admits, a widely known abbreviation, and a name the project's documented allowlist covers. Validator question: **Is a name that is a global, module-level or exported variable or constant, a field, or a local used more than about 7 to 10 lines from its declaration (fewer when the scope holds many or similar variables) a single letter, a letter-dropped abbreviation or a short popular word whose meaning the reader cannot recover without returning to its declaration?** Yes → flag.

## Finding output
When the validator answers yes, the finder emits one finding (`rule_id: CODE-11`, severity minor, `file`, `symbol`, `code` = the added declaration carrying the short name, verbatim from the diff, `fix` = the declaration renamed to a descriptive name and one of its distant uses renamed with it, in the file's language, `rationale` = the name's visibility or the line distance from its declaration to its last use, and what a reader at that use cannot tell from the name).
