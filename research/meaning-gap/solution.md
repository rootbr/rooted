# Narrowing the gap between what an author means and what his addressee takes: a method

A professional explains a finding to a client, and the client takes another meaning from the words than their author put into them. The method below treats this as a measurement problem. The author's own sense that a text is clear is no evidence of how it will be taken: authors predict that they are understood more often than they are, and the one thing that shows what an addressee took is the addressee saying it. So the method moves the check from the author to the addressee at every step: his words go into the text before it is written, he confirms its core before it is built, a person from his side reads what goes to those who were not in the room, and every exchange ends with him saying the message back.

Ten practices follow. Each is a proposal: it comes into force as the decision of whoever adopts it, with the goal named under it. The claim that a practice works is a statement of its own, recorded with the test it rests on in `solution-provenance.md` or waiting for one in `pending-evidence.md`. Two of the twenty-three claims have a published test with their own comparison and measure, and both reach this setting as a transfer from other people and other texts. Of the other twenty-one, twenty are hypotheses and one is contested: its published tests disagree. The search found no test of any practice on consultants and their clients (`research/2026-11-22_meaning-gap-evidence.md`).

## The terms

- **A key sentence** is a sentence whose misreading changes what the addressee decides or does.
- **The core** of a message is its two or three key sentences: what was found, what it costs, what is proposed.
- **A say-back** is the addressee's statement, in his own words, of what a message says and what he will do with it.
- **A stand-in reader** is a person of the addressee's trade or organisation who reads a draft before the addressee does and says what each key sentence means to him.
- **The first-try match** is the share of key sentences an addressee or a stand-in reader says back as the author meant them, before any correction.

## The loop

```
1 his words  -->  2 the core, confirmed  -->  3 the text, made concrete and plain
     ^                                                    |
     |                                                    v
6 the record  <--  5 the say-back  <--  4 a stand-in reader, for those who were not there
```

## The practices

### 1. Collect the addressee's words before writing

From his documents and from notes of what he said, list his word for each thing the finding names, with one instance from his own business of what the word covers. For a term the finding rests on, such as "efficiency" or "product", ask him for an instance before the term is used. Where his word and the author's differ, his word goes into the text.

- Goal: the text names each thing with a word the addressee already holds.
- Rests on: H-MG-05, H-MG-06 and H-MG-09, hypotheses. People predicted that about two thirds of others shared their concept of a word, while two people shared one with a probability of 13 % to 70 %. A listener picked the picture named before in 78.0 % of trials against 54.3 % with no precedent. N-MG-08 prescribes how a defined term is chosen and placed.
- Bound: the tested effect is for an expression settled earlier in the same exchange. Copying the addressee's manner of speaking at large is another thing: among 64 negotiating pairs, those with higher style matching were more likely to reach an impasse.

### 2. Ask, do not imagine

Before wording the finding, ask the addressee, or one person on his side, how he sees the matter and what he will do with the result. The answer replaces the author's guess at his view.

- Goal: the author's forecast of how the text will be taken rests on the addressee's words.
- Rests on: H-MG-01, supported as a transfer by one preregistered experiment: speakers judged 70 % of their ambiguous phrases understood against 44 % that were. Two earlier experiments, from which the claim took its measure, found 72 % against 61 % and 78 % against 56 %. H-MG-03, a hypothesis: partners who asked predicted 8.60 of 20 opinions correctly against 3.95 for those who imagined, and across nine experiments the instruction to take the other's perspective did not raise accuracy and lowered it on one measure.

### 3. Confirm the core before building the deliverable

Write the core and give it to the addressee before the deck or the report exists. He says it back or corrects it. The deliverable is written after his confirmation and opens with those sentences.

- Goal: a wrong reading of the core is found when it costs three sentences.
- Rests on: H-MG-16, a hypothesis with no test found. N-MG-02 has a subordinate say his commander's intent back right after an order, N-MG-07 asks for the main message up front, and N-MG-10 has auditors return a written summary of officials' oral comments to them for verification.
- Against: in 171 software projects a fix made in a later phase did not take consistently more effort than an early one, so the saving is not to be assumed.

### 4. Make it concrete

Put a number in brackets beside each vague word of quantity, frequency or likelihood: "most orders (7 of 10)", "often late (one delivery in four)". Put one instance from the addressee's own setting beside each abstract term.

- Goal: a vague or abstract word is read within the range the author meant.
- Rests on: H-MG-07, supported as a transfer: a range in brackets beside a word of likelihood raised the overlap of readers' ranges with the intended one from 32 % to 66 %. H-MG-08, a hypothesis: readers of a requirement answered 27 of 45 questions correctly with tables of concrete cases beside the text against 8 of 45 without. N-MG-06 fixes a table of seven words of likelihood with their ranges.
- Bound: the number stands beside the word. In a separate table it gave 39 %, behind a tooltip 40 %. The word adds nothing to the number: the two together did no better than the number alone. Both tests showed the number in written text; for a finding given in speech the practice is untested.

### 5. Plain wording for every reader, depth by reader

Common words and short sentences for every addressee, an expert included. The amount of explanation follows what this addressee knows. A document for several addressees keeps one core and sets the explanation in parts a knowing reader can skip.

- Goal: no addressee is slowed by the wording, and none is left without the explanation he needs.
- Rests on: H-MG-20, a hypothesis, and H-MG-19, contested. Attorneys and laypeople both understood contract excerpts better in plain English, with no interaction of version and group. Instruction with much assistance served learners with little prior knowledge (d = 0.505) and hindered those with much (d = −0.428).
- Bound: plain wording has a ceiling. Where contracts were redrafted in three stages, simplified words and sentence structure raised comprehension, and its absolute level stayed very low.

### 6. A negative finding in four parts

State what should be, what is with its number, what in the process causes the difference, and what it costs. The sentence has the process as its subject and passes no verdict on people: "an order waits nine of its twelve days for approval" in place of "your approval does not work".

- Goal: the addressee can act on the finding and need not defend himself against it.
- Rests on: H-MG-17 and H-MG-18, hypotheses with no test of the comparison. N-MG-09 and N-MG-11 prescribe a finding as criteria, condition, cause and effect. Over a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, more so where feedback drew attention to the self, and software practitioners rated a specific, considerate review comment as more valid than a vague, inconsiderate one.
- Bound: the auditing rules give the form so that the audited body can plan a correction. They say nothing about how the finding is received, and a house in which any criticism is taken as an attack is checked by practice 7, not assumed away by the form.

### 7. A stand-in reader for those who were not there

A text that goes to someone who took no part in the working conversations is read first by one or more people of his trade or organisation. Each says in his own words what every key sentence means. The author writes the words down, does not correct him, and fixes the text wherever the say-back differs from what was meant. A colleague's review stays as an earlier filter and is not counted as this check.

- Goal: the misreadings of an absent reader are found before he reads.
- Bound: N-MG-03 itself says that the full test is probably not worth the time for a document that goes to a few people. For one absent reader the practice asks for one stand-in reader, and the six to nine interviews belong to a text that goes to many.
- Rests on: H-MG-15 and H-MG-13, hypotheses. Writers and subject experts predicted under 15 % of the problems readers of a brochure signalled. Listeners who could answer back placed 99 % of figures correctly against 88 % for overhearers of the same words. N-MG-03 prescribes six to nine such interviews for a short document, N-MG-04 requires pretesting with respondents and allows expert review where constraints make that impossible, and N-MG-05 gives an illustrative method with twenty members of the target group, professionals excluded, that passes at 16 of 20 on each question.

### 8. Close on the say-back

End an exchange with a request to say the message back: "Tell me in your words what you take from this and what you will do first." The author listens for the difference and corrects it at once. Before a senior addressee the request is put as a check of the author: "I want to be sure I said it clearly."

- Goal: a difference between what was meant and what was taken is found while both are still in the room.
- Rests on: H-MG-14, a hypothesis: no trial compares the say-back with the question whether all is clear. Patients noticed a gap in their own understanding 20 % of the time when one existed. N-MG-01 has an air crew read a clearance back and the controller correct any discrepancy at once; N-MG-02 has a subordinate say his commander's intent back.
- Against: eighteen randomised trials of teach-back against usual instructions show no clear pattern on knowledge.

### 9. Keep a record of each exchange

After each exchange write down who wrote, who read, the key sentences, which of them were said back as meant at the first try, which words were misread or resented, and the addressee's own words for the things named. The record serves three uses. The author sees what readers took from his texts. The firm sees from counts which author is understood by which addressee and can assign by them. The addressee's words are at hand for the next text.

- Goal: the firm's knowledge of what works with whom rests on recorded say-backs.
- Rests on: H-MG-04, H-MG-10 and H-MG-12, hypotheses. Writers who read readers' think-aloud protocols rose from 3.25 to 5.25 correctly predicted problems, while writers taught by other methods, audience-analysis heuristics among them, fell from 3.04 to 2.46; educational or clinical experience went with more accurate judgement at d = 0.12. The pair explained over 40 % of the variance in teammates' ratings of each other's listening. Patients assigned to a therapist by his recorded outcomes with patients like them improved more than patients assigned as usual (d = 0.75); for 58.6 % of them the match meant a therapist with no record of failure in their problem domains, and for 9.1 % one with a record of success in their three worst, so the first use of a record is to avoid a known mismatch.
- Bound: one exchange does not show a match. The part of a pair is told from chance only where the pair has two or more recorded exchanges. A match is read from the record and not from a likeness of the two people. Over 100 traits recorded before a meeting predicted none of the pair's part in a rating. A pair that knows each other well still closes on the say-back: with a friend, speakers were not reliably better understood than with a stranger and overrated their success more (H-MG-11).

### 10. A language model as a first filter only

A model may read a draft before the stand-in reader and list sentences it expects to be misread. Its list counts as no check and replaces neither practice 7 nor practice 8. A model that is used gets the addressee's recorded say-backs and questions from practice 9, not a label of his role.

- Goal: the author's time before the stand-in reading is spent on likely places, and no decision rests on a model's reading.
- Rests on: H-MG-21 and H-MG-22, hypotheses with no test of their comparisons. A model's marks of the terms a given reader does not know scored within three points of marking every term; simulated users found 3 points of failure where ten people found 10. A person's two-hour interview and earlier survey answers raised a model's forecast of his later answers to 72.8 %, from 60.4 % with his demographics; a paragraph he wrote about himself did not, and his own publications gave nothing over the name of his field.

## What the method does not take from the essay

- **A match by likeness.** The essay explains a match by two people thinking and speaking alike. The sources found closeness and shared knowledge without a gain in accuracy, so the method assigns by a record of say-backs.
- **No wording clear to everyone.** Plain wording helped experts and laypeople alike. What depends on the reader is the amount of explanation and the terms.
- **Exposure as such.** Experience alone moved accuracy little. What moved writers' forecasts was seeing what readers took, which the record of practice 9 supplies.
- **A colleague's fresh eyes as the check.** A colleague shares the author's words; the check is a reader from the addressee's side.

## How a firm tests it on itself

The first-try match is counted from the record of practice 9, and so is the count of rounds that correct what a deliverable says. A firm that records twenty exchanges before it adopts a practice and twenty after has the comparison each hypothesis names. The threshold is fixed before the second twenty: for example, the first-try match with a confirmed core is at least fifteen points above the match without one, and a smaller difference refutes the claim for this firm.

| Practice | Hypothesis | Compared with | Refuted when |
|--|--|--|--|
| 1 his words | H-MG-09 | the same text in the author's words | he identifies no more of the things named |
| 2 ask | H-MG-03 | a forecast after imagining his view | the forecast after asking is no more accurate |
| 3 the core first | H-MG-16 | the full deliverable first | the correcting rounds are not fewer |
| 4 an instance | H-MG-08 | the bare abstract term | the first-try match is not higher |
| 6 four parts | H-MG-17 | the same finding as a verdict | no more addressees accept the finding |
| 7 a stand-in reader | H-MG-15 | a colleague's review | he marks no more of the later misreadings |
| 8 the say-back | H-MG-14 | closing on "is it clear?" | no more addressees state the message correctly afterwards |
| 9 assignment by the record | H-MG-12 | the firm's usual assignment | the first-try match is not higher |
| 10 a model as filter | H-MG-21 | the author's own re-reading | it marks no more misread sentences, or more false ones |

## For an agent that writes for its operator

The same loop describes an agent that writes reports for the person who runs it, and this repository's skill `handing-over-to-readers` holds it for that setting by its owner's decision. Before a long deliverable the agent reads what the records hold of its reader and asks only what they leave open (practices 1 and 2). The opening of the deliverable goes to him first, with the request to say in his own words what he takes from it (3 and 8). A vague word carries its number, and a finding that something does not work stands in four parts (4 and 6). The cold reader, a model, gets a question for each thing this reader lacks and stays a first filter under a person's reading (7 and 10). Each long deliverable leaves a record of its exchange, and a command prints the reader's own counts (9). Plain words with depth by reader (5) is a clause of the skill's earlier research. Each clause is the owner's decision with a hypothesis that waits in the skill's own list for a test, except the number beside a vague word, which rests on H-MG-07 by a further transfer; the four parts of a finding follow two norms of auditing. `research/2026-11-22_meaning-gap-evidence.md` § Rules and data maps the clauses to their sources.
