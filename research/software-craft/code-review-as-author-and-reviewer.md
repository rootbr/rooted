# Research note — Code review as author and reviewer

Topic `code-review-as-author-and-reviewer` of group I. Reading and documenting, domain `docs`, id prefix `DOC`. Formulation locators: CC21 (Code Complete 2nd ed. ch. 21 'Collaborative Construction': pair programming, formal inspections, walk-throughs, code reading); SEG9 (Software Engineering at Google ch. 9 'Code Review'); SEG19 (ch. 19 'Critique: Google's Code Review Tool') where it treats the author's and the reviewer's steps; the 97 Things essay 'Code Reviews' (thing_14).

The canon treats code review as a written exchange with duties on both sides. The canon here is Code Complete ch. 21, Software Engineering at Google ch. 9 and ch. 19, and the 97 Things essay 'Code Reviews'. The author describes the change, reads it before sending it, and answers every comment, preferably in the code. The reviewer reads the whole change in context, labels and explains each comment, and approves once the change improves the code rather than when it is perfect. Official project documentation states these duties in openable form: Google eng-practices, git, the Linux kernel, Chromium, Go, Rust Forge, OpenJDK, GitLab, GitHub, Gerrit and Phabricator. The research that could be opened ties a missing rationale in the change description to reviewer confusion, a delayed merge decision and lower review quality. That research is Ebert et al. SANER 2019 and EMSE 2021, with MacLeod 2018, Tao 2012 and Bacchelli & Bird 2013 relayed through them or through the orchestrator. The industry disagrees on several points: the purpose of review (finding defects against spreading understanding), whether an answer given only in the review thread is enough, whether cleanup may be deferred to a follow-up change, how self-contained a description must be when it links an issue tracker, the grammatical form of the first line, who wins a tie between author and reviewer, and whether feedback comes in one round. A finder holds the hunks and the commit history, so it can see only the change description and the files the author left in the change. The topic therefore yields six cards. Four check the description: its first-line summary, its problem and reason, its account of verification and its known shortcomings. One checks that the description matches the final diff, and one that the change adds no file unrelated to its purpose. Review policy is outside the corpus. Reviewer conduct and the thread exchange are held as not checkable on a diff; the research note records them, and there they map onto the skill's own reading procedure and finding format. Three rules fold into existing cards: tests in the change (TST-14), quantified optimizations (PRF-11) and a change that builds before review (CHG-17).

## Formulation layer

Opened from this environment: Software Engineering at Google ch. 9 'Code Review' and ch. 19 'Critique' (the CC BY-NC-ND HTML in abseil/abseil.github.io); the 97 Things essay 'Code Reviews' (thing_14); the aposd-vs-clean-code discussion; Google's eng-practices reviewer and author guides; Chromium's author and reviewer guides; git's SubmittingPatches; and the Linux kernel process documents. Code Complete ch. 21 and A Philosophy of Software Design ch. 18 could not be opened from here, so they appear as marked paraphrases. Of the papers, only Bacchelli & Bird is carried, through the orchestrator's relayed snippet. Sadowski et al. 2018 stays unfetched (no GitHub copy found; neverworkintheory's bibliography does not list it), and McIntosh et al. is left to the evidence layer. The sources agree on these points:
- The author reads the change, as the reviewer will see it, before sending it.
- The description says what changes and why, and gives context, links, testing instructions and numbers for optimizations.
- The reviewer reads the design, the behaviour, the tests, every line and the surrounding code.
- Comments are about the code, say why, and are labelled by severity.
- Approval comes once the change improves code health, not once it is perfect.
- When a reviewer asks a question, the answer goes into the code (clarify it, then comment it) rather than only into the thread.
The sources disagree on five points:
- The purpose of review: 97 Things says learn the code rather than hunt errors; SEG9, eng-practices and CC21 count defect-finding as part of the review.
- Whether a reply only in the review tool is enough: the SEG19 workflow and its archaeology passage allow it; eng-practices, Linux and Morton do not.
- Whether reviewers propose fixes: CC21 says detection, not correction; eng-practices and SEG19 allow suggested edits.
- Timing of feedback: SEG9 says give it in one round, not piecemeal; eng-practices' navigate guide says send design comments immediately.
- Cleanup: eng-practices says clean up now; Chromium allows deferring to a follow-up change with a bug.
Review policy (who approves, OWNERS and readability, number of reviewers, turnaround times, weekly meetings) is recorded once and stays outside the corpus. Change size is handed to small-steps-and-minimal-diffs.

## Reception layer

The industry broadly agrees on four author/reviewer rules. These sources say so: Google eng-practices, the contributor guides of git, Linux, LLVM, Go, the Rust compiler, OpenJDK, CPython, Node.js, Kubernetes, Chromium and GitLab, the GitHub and Gerrit docs, and named practitioners (Michael Lynch, thoughtbot, Palantir, Michaela Greiler). (1) A change description says what changes and why. (2) The author reviews their own diff before asking. (3) An author answers a reviewer's confusion in the code, a code comment or the changelog rather than only in the thread. (4) A review comment states whether it blocks. These positions are contested. Who wins a tie: Google, SEG9 and thoughtbot defer to the author on design, Lynch defers to the reviewer, and LLVM expects suggestions to be taken unless the reviewers agree otherwise. Where the description lives and whether it must stand alone: the kernel, git and the Rust compiler say yes, while OpenJDK (JBS) and the Microsoft playbook disagree. Whether the description must restate the 'what' or follow format rules: Phabricator disagrees with Google, Go, git, Kubernetes and GitLab. How revisions are presented: git rewrites history while GitHub-based guides push fixup commits, so this depends on the tool. The approval bar: Lynch's 'C or B-' against Google's pushback.md. Whether review exists to find defects: Czerwonka and 97 Things against SEG9. Whether to read every line now that AI produces large diffs: Greiler 2026. Whether a generated PR description is acceptable: GitHub against LLVM and Kubernetes. Tool rules found: checkpatch COMMIT_MESSAGE and GIT_COMMIT_ID, gitlint B5/B6, commitlint body-empty (not enabled in config-conventional), Danger's PR-body check, GitLab's Danger body rule (30 lines across 3 files), and Phabricator's Test Plan, required by default. WebSearch was exhausted, so these could not be opened: HN and Lobsters threads, the Conventional Comments site (hosted on GitLab), Code Complete ch. 21, and blogs off GitHub. Papers were reached only through Greiler's research list, neverworkintheory, the ds4se chapter, a bibtex page and the orchestrator's relayed snippet.

## Evidence layer

Most of the support for the author-side rules can be opened. A change description that states what and why, is self-contained, backs its claims and is updated before submit is stated by google/eng-practices, git SubmittingPatches, the Linux kernel's submitting-patches.rst and the Go contribution guide. Self-review before requesting review is stated by git, GitHub Docs and Chromium. Answering a reviewer's confusion in the code rather than only in the thread is stated by Google's author and reviewer guides and by Linux. The reviewer-side rules are documented by eng-practices, Gerrit, GitLab, OpenStack and SWEBOK V3: read the design and main part first, read every line and the surrounding context, label comment severity, give the reason, and approve when the change improves code health. The only research I opened is Ebert et al. (SANER 2019, and the EMSE 2021 preprint), from the first author's GitHub Pages site. These papers measure the description rules: missing rationale is the most frequent reason for confusion, and confusion leads to blind approval and to discussion moving off-line. MacLeod 2018, Tao 2012, Ram 2018, Uwano 2006 and Kononenko 2015 are known here only through Ebert's account. Bacchelli and Bird 2013 and McIntosh 2014 are known only through the orchestrator's snippets, and Porter 1997 only through the awesome-code-review annotation. Sadowski 2018, Bosu 2015 and Rao 2022 stay unfetched: their titles are confirmed but no openable copy was found (tpn/pdfs, papers-we-love, sback.github.io and vhellendoorn.github.io were tried). Fregnan 2022 and Czerwonka 2015 were left out because no openable source confirmed them. The canon disagrees in three places. Go wants a first line that is not a complete sentence and completes 'This change modifies Go to ___', while Google wants an imperative complete sentence. SWEBOK's description of formal inspection allows sampling instead of reading every line. Google's own pushback guide limits 'approve when not perfect': new complexity must be cleaned up before submit, because a promised later cleanup rarely happens.

2. **A reviewer reads a change in full on their own, the description first, then the main part for its design, then every human-written line together with its tests and the surrounding code** — The reviewer reads in this order:
- First, check that the change makes sense and has a good description.
- Then read the most important part for its overall design.
- Then read the rest in a sensible order, looking at every human-written line assigned to them and at the tests.

When the few lines the review tool shows are not enough to judge the change, the reviewer opens the whole file. Data files, generated code and large data structures may be scanned. Inspection practice reads individually, guided by a checklist of the defect kinds that have caused problems before. In one small eye-tracking study, reviewers who first scanned the whole code found defects faster.
