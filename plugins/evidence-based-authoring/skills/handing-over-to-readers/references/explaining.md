# Explaining: the reader's questions, the story, where to look, the cold read

A deliverable explains when its reader, after one reading, can retell what the task was, what happened and what is needed from him.

## The reader's questions

1. The questions come from the reader before they come from the author's guess. Technical writers and subject or audience experts asked to predict where readers of one brochure would stumble predicted under 15 % of the problems its readers reported.
2. Sources, in this order: the request (every question his words name); the record of a meeting where the readers discussed this work; the reader record (`references/intake.md`); the records the project keeps of him (a review rehearsal journal, review comments on this work, what he asked or complained about after earlier reports); for ground none of them covers, the defaults: what was the task, how did it end, what happened and why, what is unverified, what is his to decide. A question from a meeting is read in the record itself, with the remarks around it: they show what the reader believes now, which is the second field of his registry line. What the meeting decided is the last thing said on the matter. A digest of the meeting points to the place in the record and is never the source: in one case a work item taken from a digest had been postponed at the end of the same meeting, and a question there rested on a model of the mechanism that only the remarks around it showed. An explanation of a mechanism (`references/explainer.md`) takes the newcomer's questions always, whatever the other sources cover: which signals the mechanism has, how each is measured, what each gives, what it computes from them and by which steps. In one case a registry held the questions of one meeting alone, and four reviewers of the page built from it asked the newcomer's: which signals there are, whose queue is meant, over which period a share is taken, of which steps the decision consists.
3. Skip what he holds, tell what he has not seen. What an engineer holds without help: his language, the standard library, the patterns of his trade. What he does not hold after being away: the state of this task, the things this work touched, a term coined during the work. An explainer who overestimated the recipient's knowledge, and one who underestimated it, both taught him less than one who held a valid estimate (6.2 and 6.4 against 8.5 on a test of 18); the two errors were not tested against each other.
4. A question whose answer cannot be said in one plain sentence marks a gap in the author's own understanding; it is closed by reading, running or computing before any text is written.

## The terms

The words for the things a deliverable names are collected before its first sentence, as the reader's questions are.

1. One line for each thing the text will name — an actor, a signal, a quantity, a state, a unit: the term as it is written, its case included; what it names, in one clause; its name in the code where it has one; the words the text must not use for it.
2. The term is the readers' own. Sources, in this order: the words the reader record keeps for this reader (`references/intake.md` § After the deliverable), the records of the meetings and reviews where the readers spoke of the thing, their documents, the code as its authors say it aloud, the reader's trade. A thing that none of them names, or that two of them name differently, is a question to the operator in the card form (`SKILL.md` § 5), the candidate words as its options.
3. The barred words are the ones an author reaches for: his own synonym, the textbook word where the team has its own, a translation of a term the readers say in English; and the words the reader record marks as rejected or misread by this reader.
4. Units and the notation of numbers are lines of the same list. A prefix stands on the unit, after a space (`100 kHz`); a multiplier written on a count (`100k requests a second`) is a convention of the team and takes a line when the team writes so.
5. A script holds the text to the list: `scripts/build_page.py --terms` refuses a page whose text holds a barred word (`references/page.md` § The terms as data).

A standard of controlled technical English prescribes both halves of the list: the noun the company or the field has approved, and no second noun for an item already named; the style guides of two software vendors prescribe one term for one concept. The nearest tests on readers are of names in code: professional developers found defects 19 % faster with whole words as identifiers than with letters or abbreviations. That a list made before the first sentence leaves fewer variants in the text is not measured.

## The story

The story is written for the ear: it is the text the author would read aloud to a colleague who was away, and its sentences are what the voice speaks when the report goes out with sound (`references/media.md`); the bullets on the parts and on the decision name the two places where the text shows a heading or a picture for what the voice says, and the bullet on a condition names the block the voice passes. In one case read by one reader, the narration he found clear ran 8 words a sentence on average and 16 at most; the written story of the same case, which he found tangled, ran 17 on average, with a sentence of 40.

- **The opening gives the whole matter.** The first paragraph says, in three to five sentences, what the reader needs to place everything after it: the task in his words with its terms, how it ended, the problem with its cause in one sentence, and what is needed from him, the options named. The parts after it unfold what the opening named. A sentence whose antecedent is already given is understood faster (835 against 1016 ms to the press of a button on understanding; students reading pairs of sentences), and a report that opens on its result leaves the reader asking what it is about.
- **The opening of a long deliverable goes to the reader first.** A deliverable is long when it explains a mechanism, carries a decision among options or runs past the length bound of the form check (`references/intake.md`). Its opening is confirmed before anything is built on it, the parts of a report as much as the first part and the page of an explanation:
  1. The opening goes to the reader alone with one request: to say in his own words what he takes from it and what he wants unfolded.
  2. Where his words differ from what was meant, the opening is rewritten and the line of the registry corrected; what he asks to see becomes a question of the registry. A bare yes is taken as it is.
  3. The deliverable opens with the confirmed sentences, and its exchange is recorded with how the opening was confirmed (`records.py exchange add --opening ...`, `references/intake.md` § After the deliverable).

  The request asks for his own words because a reader who misread seldom knows it: emergency patients whose recall of their instructions fell short of the chart perceived a difficulty in one case of five. The step is left out when the reader has said to deliver without it, for one deliverable or as a standing preference (`SKILL.md` § 7). An army prescribes the step for its orders: a subordinate briefs his commander's intent back to him right after the order. Two norms stand near it: an intelligence standard puts the main message of a product up front and asks that the customer receive the message the analyst meant, and auditors return a written summary of officials' oral comments to them for verification. The step is there to find a reading other than the one meant while it costs a few sentences. That it costs less than a correction after the whole deliverable is untested: no test found compares an opening confirmed first with a deliverable handed over whole, and in 171 software projects a defect fixed in a later phase did not take consistently more effort than one fixed early.
- **A sentence the ear can hold.** One claim per sentence, no clause nested inside another, under about twenty words. A second claim starts a new sentence: "The test waited for one attempt. It saw four." `scripts/check_report.py` warns past twenty words.
- **Parts announced.** A short phrase opens each part and says what it is about: "How it works.", "Now the red build.", "There are two ways." A small change takes four parts: the opening, how the thing works, what went wrong, what is his to decide. In the text and on the page each part after the opening stands under a heading of one to four words in the reader's words; the voice says the phrase.
- **Then what happened, in the order of cause and effect.** Each sentence opens on something the earlier ones gave and ends on the new thing. The relation between neighbours is a word: because, so, but, otherwise. A list of facts leaves that relation to the reader.
- **Introduce a thing before using it.** A thing new to the reader is first said as what it is ("the operations team has an alert on errors; it finds the error lines by a pattern") and only then named or used. A computed quantity is first said as what it is for and what happens without it, and only then given as its formula. One new thing per sentence, one name per thing for the whole text. A term that returns a part or more after its definition, or that was defined only in a caption or a table, is said again in a clause at its return: "the version, the count of uploads of this picture". A sentence points at a numbered line or node only after the reader has seen the numbers. In reading-time experiments an antecedent mentioned early in a passage was reinstated more slowly than a late one, and an elaborated one faster than a bare one; a definition said again at the return is itself untested. The part of an explanation that gives the mechanism whole (`references/explainer.md` § The first part) is the one level where a thing is used before it is unfolded: it is named there by its role, in words that explain themselves ("the estimate of how much the path still takes"), and the name links to the part that unfolds it.
- **A condition and a calculation stand as a formula beside their sentence.** The sentence says what the condition is for; the formula stands with it in a block the voice does not read, a fence in the text and a figure on the page. Words alone leave the operation open: in one case "no shorter than 100 ms and two round trips" was read in two ways by the reviewers of one page, where the code takes the larger of the two.

  ```
  period = max(100 ms, 2 × round-trip time)
  ```

  A number that is a share or an average says four things: of what it is a share, over which period it is taken, when it is updated and when it is read. The condition the mechanism reacts to stands apart from the way the code notices it: "the path is full" is the condition, "the delay grew in three reports in a row" is how it is noticed.

  The terms of a compound condition are joined by a written "and" or "or" at every term, one term a line when there are more than two; where both connectives meet, the indent shows which binds first. A comma between the terms leaves the connective to the reader.

  ```
  purge the entry when   the picture was uploaded again
                     and no reader holds the old key
  ```

  A handbook on ambiguity in requirements calls a sentence that mixes "and" with "or" ambiguous and settles the order by layout. An earlier experiment, known here only through a later report's summary, found instructions harder to follow where commas carried the "or" than where the "or" was repeated.
- **The name is the reader's.** A thing is called by the term its line of the list gives (§ The terms), a name of the code as the word its authors say ("the retry policy") and the identifier itself staying out of the sentences; a coined word stays only where no source has one. A term of the trade is written the way an engineer of that trade says it aloud to a colleague in the language of the text. An abbreviation or a word he says in English stays in Latin letters in a Russian or a German text, gets its expansion and one sentence of meaning at its first mention; a native substitute or a literal translation of it is a barred word (§ The terms). Terms of one series are written alike: where one stays in English, so do the others. A thing with a living native word keeps it ("queue", "window"). In text for the voice the term stands as it is written, and the synthesiser's pronunciation dictionary sets how it sounds. In one case a text called the round-trip time by a coined native word 52 times against two mentions of RTT, while its readers said RTT; the same readers replaced two more coined words with their own.
- **A word has one meaning and one owner.** A word the text uses for a thing names that thing alone. Where the mechanism has several things of one kind, the word carries its owner every time: "the sender's queue", "the parser's queue". Quantities of one family are named by what each is made of, starting from the one that is measured: "the round-trip time of one packet", then "the smallest of them over the last ten seconds", so that each name is read from the measured one. The value of a signal is defined by the event in the code that sets it: the text says what has just happened when the value is set, and takes the name from that event. In one case read by four reviewers, three names of one family were defined in a table through one another, "the queue" of the main condition meant several buffers at once, and one value of a signal was named after the place the data is read from, while the code sets it when data is left over after a frame is assembled.
- **Plain words, no code.** A thing is named by its role: "requests that only read", "the payment", "the payments team's test"; a term of the reader's trade is one of his plain words. No path, identifier, file name, command or exit code; a number only when the story turns on it, one to a sentence; no label and no bold, and a list only where the next bullet gives one. A formula and pseudocode stand in a block beside the sentence, as the bullet on a condition and a calculation says.
- **A vague word carries its number.** A word of likelihood (likely, probably, almost certainly) stands with its share or its range in the same sentence. A word of quantity or frequency (most, several, often) stands with its count: "Most of the tests pass, 212 of 230." A share written as two counts is one number of its sentence. Where the author has no number, the sentence says what he saw in place of the word: "Three of the four runs I made passed." `scripts/check_report.py` warns at such a word with no number in its sentence. With the numeric range in brackets beside a phrase of likelihood, 82 % of readers' best estimates fell inside the range the phrase stands for, against 59 % with the phrase alone; the readers were members of the public, the phrases the four of an intelligence standard. Words of quantity and frequency were not in that test.
- **A counted enumeration stands as a list.** Three or more things of one kind, announced by a sentence that gives their count ("The cache keeps three things for a picture."), stand one to a line. Each item opens with the name of its thing and says the same kinds of thing about it in the same order as the others; the voice reads the items as sentences, in their order. Things whose relation is the point — a cause and its effect, a rule and its exception — stay in a paragraph, where the connecting words carry the relation. In a series of studies of functional text a bulleted list raised recall of its items in three, less so for items of different kinds, and lowered recall of the text around the list in two.
- **The gist of a part may stand in one marked block.** The few sentences a reader has to keep from a part — the model a mechanism builds, its rule in words — stand together in a block set apart by a rule at its side and a tinted ground, one block a part at most. The sign of a warning stays with a risk. Signals raised memory for the content they cue and often left the rest unaffected, and across multimedia lessons signalling went with better learning (d = 0.38). Inappropriate highlighting impaired comprehension and the accuracy of readers' own judgement of it, and proficient readers processed and kept the central information whatever was highlighted.
- **A wrong model is named before it is corrected.** Where the registry says the reader's present answer does not hold, the part goes in three steps: his model in his words; how the mechanism differs from it; what the thing he asked about really gives or costs. A yes or a no to a question asked from the wrong model stands after the first step, because a bare answer is read inside the model the question came from. Texts that state a common misconception, refute it and then explain taught more than texts that only explain (g = 0.41 over 44 comparisons, most of them students reading one short science text).
- **A picture for each part that has something to show**: a mechanism with its values, a failure, a choice (§ The pictures). The opening takes none: its sentences carry the state. The sentences of a part stay whole without its picture, so the voice skips it. A concrete case that fades into the general statement transferred better than the general statement alone, which a concrete case by itself did not.
- **An exchange and a rule in words get one worked case.** After the sentences that say how two sides exchange messages, or how a rule computes a value, one case with numbers follows, a block a step (§ The pictures). A derivation goes in steps of one operation, each labelled with what the step is for, in the order of the blocks of its figure; a single line of chained equalities hides where each term comes from. A sentence that names an outcome also gives the step that produces it: "the reader gets the old thumbnail, because the entry under the old key has not expired". Worked examples helped learners of mathematics (g = 0.48), and the help turned into a cost as knowledge of the taught subject grew; a reader meets a mechanism new to him as a novice in it, whatever his trade. A label on each group of steps of an example helped participants form its subgoals, as their solving of novel problems showed across four experiments; in the first, 60 % and 44 % of the two groups with a label mentioned the subgoal, against 16 % of the group without one. Readers who knew little of a text's domain gained from a coherent text, and readers who knew the domain gained from a minimally coherent one.
- **Why this way** is answered with what happens without it: the input or the state under which the alternative fails, with the number. In an explanation the alternative is the simpler scheme the reader would build himself, named in his words, and the answer is one case: its starting conditions and its numbers, the step at which the simpler scheme fails, and what this mechanism does at that step. The simpler scheme is built whole, as its reader would build it from nothing, and the case runs on that scheme. A factor of the mechanism gets a small model of its own, run with it and without it (§ The pictures); a measured difference comes with its scenario, its command and the spread of its runs (`references/evidence.md`). Each input the mechanism reads gets such a case, a run that differs by that input alone. Where taking the input away breaks nothing in the chosen case because another input covers for it, the text says so and takes the case in which the mechanism does fail. People ask why one thing happened instead of another, as a review of the research on explanation found; that such a case makes a reader understand the mechanism better is not measured.
- **What was not done or not verified** is a sentence, and only when it changes what the reader does or trusts: "I did not check that."
- **A value the story states is one the author can point at.** It stands on a line under "Where to look", or the story says in the first person that it was computed or not verified. Before writing the consequence of a change, trace it through every file behind the things the story names: a story reads smoothly whether or not its numbers are right.
- **The reader's decision closes the story.** The voice counts the options aloud: "The first: … Then … The second: … Then …", one sentence for what the option is and one for what it gives and costs. The text and the page show the options as the choice picture (§ The pictures) in place of those sentences. The author's choice follows with its reason; one question stands last. A decision that rests on one part alone may stand under that part, its options counted there; the end of the story is the default.
- **Length.** As many sentences as the reader needs to retell the matter and no more. Before a sentence goes in, name the question it answers; a sentence that answers none leaves. A small change takes twenty to thirty short sentences, about two hundred words, a minute and a half aloud.

## Where to look

The proof follows the story as a short list, so the story stays readable and every claim stays checkable.

- At most five lines in a report; an explanation for several readers opens its code at the claim and keeps in the list what has no place in the text (`references/explainer.md` § Where to look). Each names in plain words what is there, then gives the `path:line` anchor, and the fragment of the line when the line proves a claim; the fragment is the shortest piece that identifies the line.
- The checks that ran stand here with their counts and exit codes, taken from their output. A claim about behaviour takes a check; a claim about code takes an anchor (`references/evidence.md`).
- The order is the order of the story.

## The cold read

Before a report with a decision goes out, a reader with no context answers the reader's questions from the text alone (`SKILL.md` § 6).

- **Questions without answers.** The cold reader gets the questions of the registry and never the author's answers. Its answer counts only with a sentence of the report behind it, so an answer from the model's own knowledge is set aside.
- **What it shows.** A question left without a full answer points at something the report may not say. On four reports with planted omissions the read marked the missing decision in four of four and the missing cause in four of four, with each of three reader models.
- **What it does not show.** It noticed at most one of four removed sentences that introduced a thing: the sentences around the gap let it rebuild the thing. Introducing a thing before its use stays the author's own check (§ The story). Whether the text is easy to read is outside it too.
- **A question for each thing this reader lacks.** Beside the questions of the registry the cold reader gets one question for each thing the text names that the reader record puts under what this reader lacks, and for each term coined during the work: "What is a purge request, as the text says it?" The question is answered only by a sentence of the text that says what the thing is. The author turns the reader record into these questions, and the cold reader gets the questions and the text alone. That such a question finds a missing introduction more often than the read without it is not measured.
- **A mark is weighed, and a clean read proves nothing.** Two of the three models marked one intact report on the question about what is unverified: the report named it, and the reader asked whether there was more. On 60 real reports written before this form, read against all six default questions, the read left a question open in 58; of the 30 things their reader then asked back about, it named 8 and touched 11 more, among five to six marks a report. Compare each mark with the text: add what the text does not say, and leave a mark the text already answers.
- **A stated unknown is an answer.** "I could not tell whether the value came with this change" answers the question about it.
- **The first part of an explanation is read alone, before the rest.** The cold reader gets the opening and the first part, the newcomer's questions, and the request to retell the mechanism by its steps; each step counts only with a sentence of the text behind it. The steps are compared with the lines of the pseudocode: a line no step retells, two lines retold as one, or steps in another order mark where the first part says other than its author meant. A person of the team reads the same text, and his word outranks the cold read (the bullet on what it does not show).

## The pictures

Each part the story's picture bullet names (§ The story) takes one picture; a worked case and the case of an input take a picture each. The picture is designed once and drawn in both forms of delivery (`references/media.md`): in the text as pseudographics, on the page as a figure whose elements light with the voice.

| The part says | The picture shows | In the text |
|--|--|--|
| it was one way and is now another | two rows, before and after, the differing part aligned | a plain fence |
| a sequence in time | the steps as boxes on one line, to scale, the gaps labelled | a plain fence drawn with box glyphs |
| who sends what to whom, and how often | the actors joined by an arrow, one row per step, the wrong rows marked | a `diff` fence: the actors on an unmarked line, `+` the step that is right, `-` the steps that are wrong |
| a choice among options | one block per option: what it gives, what it costs | a `diff` fence: the option on an unmarked line, `+` a gain, `-` a cost |
| two sides exchange messages, step by step | one block a step, the numbers of one case in it: what is sent, what its receiver computes, what each side knows after the step | a table, a row a step |
| a recorded run passes through stages | a chart with the stages numbered and a table under it: the number, the time, what the participant sees, which rule fired, the value; a row is lit on its sentence | a table, the stage number in its first column |
| two causes give a similar picture | a table: the causes in rows, the observable signs in columns | a table |
| where a decision of the design comes from | a table: what the mechanism has, its source, what was changed and why, where it stands in our material; under it what was not taken and why; a link the author added himself is marked as his | a table |
| the rule of a mechanism as a whole | pseudocode of a few steps, one line a step, a thing named as the bullet on introducing a thing says | a fenced block |
| a measured quantity is made of parts, and several names are cut from it | one bar to scale, split into the parts; under it one span for each name, over the parts that name takes in | a plain fence drawn with box glyphs |
| what one factor of the mechanism changes | a small model with that factor alone: the same starting conditions and numbers run without it and with it, side by side, the step where the two part marked | a table, or a `diff` fence: `-` the run without the factor, `+` the run with it |

- A picture states one thing and marks the row or the element the story turns on.
- A value in the picture of a recorded run is read from the run: a stage is declared once, and its row and its mark on the chart take the same record. In one case stage values typed twice, into the table and into the chart's labels, were reconciled by eye.
- A picture adds what the sentences cannot carry: the instance with its values, the scale, the comparison side by side. One that restates the sentences in boxes is deleted.
- A picture stands where the text first uses what it shows, and the sentence that introduces a picture or a group of its blocks stands above it. Text placed with the picture it belongs to taught better than the two set apart (g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons); explanatory notes worked into a text, which helped novices, turned against experts in one experiment.
- A label stands on the thing it names, and an arrow says on itself what travels along it. A label of a mechanism's figure that no sentence uses leaves the figure; a caption says the one thing the figure is for and leaves the rest to the labels. Readers of line charts took a caption's message as their own only when the chart made the same feature stand out.
- One numbering serves a figure, the pseudocode and the sentences: the node, the line and the reference to them carry the same number, and a thing is referred to by its number or its name, never by where it stands on the page.
- A path through a mechanism is a still line with its arrowheads; nothing in a figure moves by itself. Animation showed no gain over a still picture for abstract representations (g = 0.006 over 26 comparisons) and no reliable gain where written text went with it.
- In the text a picture stays within 100 columns and draws its lines from one glyph source; in a `diff` fence the `+` and `-` carry the verdict that the colour repeats.
