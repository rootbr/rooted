# Research note — Reading and understanding code

Topic `reading-and-understanding-code` of group I. Reading and documenting, domain `docs`, id prefix `DOC`. Formulation locators: no canon chapter; researched from the evidence layer. The topic is usually formulated from Felienne Hermans, The Programmer's Brain (2021); Diomidis Spinellis, Code Reading: The Open Source Perspective (2003); WELC ch. 16 'I Don't Understand the Code Well Enough to Change It' (notes and sketching, scratch refactoring); and the 97 Things essays 'Read Code' (thing_70) and 'Only the Code Tells the Truth' (thing_62).

The canon takes three positions on reading before writing, and they agree. Developers spend most of their working time reading code. Before a change they read with a goal: they locate the code by search, read its callers, tests and history, and find out why it is there (Spinellis, WELC ch. 11 and 16, Chesterton's fence in Software Engineering at Google ch. 3, google/eng-practices). A guess about behaviour is confirmed by running the code, a test or an assertion, not inferred from a name or a comment (Spinellis maxims 21 and 24, Pragmatic Programmer Tips 34 and 38, Hermans ch. 7, 9 and 11). Practitioners disagree on four points. The first is the size of the reading share: the 10:1 ratio is uncited, and the one field study (Xia et al. 2018) measured about 58%. The second is trust in names and comments: Martin trusts names, Ousterhout trusts comments, and each disputes the other's half; Sonar's rule that method calls are free takes Martin's side. The third is whether complexity metrics capture understandability (Muñoz Barón et al. say in part; Scalabrino et al. and clippy say no). The fourth is how far atoms of confusion transfer beyond C (only 7 of 14 showed the effect in Java). The evidence supports four diff-checkable cards. (1) A standard routine whose documented contract differs from its name or from its namesake in another language is used as documented: Python, Go, Java, MDN and Rust docs, fetched, plus a hands-on run in all five languages. (2) A behaviour change leaves no in-repository caller depending on the old behaviour: LaToza and Myers 2010, Ko et al. 2006, SWEBOK impact analysis, LSP. (3) An added call depends on no property that the callee's name or comment suggests but its body contradicts. This card is contested; the separating condition is whether the name or comment agrees with the body (Siegmund et al. 2017 on one side; Ko et al. 2006, Fakhoury et al. 2018 and Wen et al. 2019 on the other). (4) Removing a guard or workaround whose reason is recorded shows that the reason no longer applies: eng-practices CL descriptions, Ko et al. 2007, the git blame, pickaxe and -L docs, with Aranda and Venolia 2009 as the limit. The rest is held because it leaves no trace in a diff: the reading share, scratch refactoring, sketching, navigation from a focal point, running to confirm, the confusing-construct hazard, reading tests first, and the complexity-metric dispute. No openable study shows that reading tests first, sketching or checking history before a change improves outcomes. Neighbouring shipped cards fix the boundaries. API-17 covers reliance on unspecified behaviour and API-20/CHG-25 published callers. CODE-02, CODE-71 and CODE-63 are the writer-side forms of honest names and current comments. API-03 covers assumptions checked by assertions, and CODE-53 operator grouping. Trigger caveat for the removal card: triggers see added lines only, so a hunk that only deletes lines does not trigger it.

## Formulation layer

The canon covers three positions, and the sources agree on all three. First, developers spend most of their time reading code (Clean Code says 10:1, Ousterhout's own figure is 50-80%, Hermans says about 60%, SWEBOK V3 says about half of maintenance effort, and PEP 8 states it without a number). Second, before changing code you read it with a goal: you locate it by search, take stock of its tests and history, and work out why it is there (Spinellis, the Chesterton's fence passage in SWE at Google ch. 3, the google/eng-practices page on CL descriptions, and git blame and log -S/-L). Third, a guess about behaviour gets checked by running the code, a test or an assertion, not inferred from a name or a comment (Spinellis maxims 21 and 24, Pragmatic Programmer Tips 34 and 38, Hermans ch. 9 on mischunking, and Clean Code on side effects). The canon disagrees on how far a reader can trust names and comments. Ousterhout says a trusted name can hide side effects and refusing to trust comments is costly. Martin says trusted names let the reader skip the callee and every comment is potential misinformation. Sonar S3776's 'method calls are free' takes Martin's side. The 97 Things essays, the aposd-vs-clean-code discussion, SWE at Google, eng-practices, the Go style guide, PEP 8, SWEBOK V3, the git docs, Sonar and Checkstyle were all fetched. The Programmer's Brain, Code Reading, Clean Code and The Pragmatic Programmer could only be reached through third-party notes and transcriptions on GitHub, so those are marked relayed. WELC ch. 16 and ch. 11 stay unfetched paraphrases; one third-party outline confirms only ch. 16's section titles. The research papers (Xia et al., Busjahn et al., Siegmund/Peitek, atoms of confusion, Arnaoudova's linguistic antipatterns, Sadowski et al. 2015) are left to the evidence layer. They are named in notes only where a canon source relies on them.

## Reception layer

The industry agrees that reading takes a large share of a developer's work: PEP 8, Robert C. Martin, Software Engineering at Google and SonarSource S3776 all say so. The size of that share is disputed. The common "10 to 1" figure has no study behind it (per a 2026 claims ledger), while the field study by Xia et al. 2018 (opened from the author's GitHub Pages) measured up to ~58%. The sharpest dispute is over what reading can trust. Sommerlad's "Only the Code Tells the Truth" and Martin trust the code and its names over comments. Ousterhout says interface comments are needed and that trusting a name hides side effects. Google's eng-practices says source code shows what the software does but not why it exists, which points the reader to version history; the git docs bound what blame and pickaxe can show, with their own caveats. On confusing constructs, research gives mixed support. Atoms-of-confusion studies come only from C/C++ and a Java replication with novice students, and their own authors later questioned the hand-evaluation instrument. Tool rules (Error Prone OperatorPrecedence, clippy precedence, ESLint no-cond-assign and the deprecated no-mixed-operators) record the precedence and assignment-in-condition hazards they check. Understandability metrics are openly contested: the clippy maintainers, Scalabrino et al. 2021, Lavazza et al. and a 2026 Ultracite post dispute the S3776 threshold of 15 and its "method calls are free" rule. ## Evidence layer

The research I could open here supports five reading rules. Comprehension takes most of a change's time: Xia et al. measured about 58% of developers' time in the field, and SWEBOK says about half of maintenance effort. Developers search for the code to change, then follow its incoming and outgoing dependencies; in Ko et al. 88% of searches led nowhere and names misled. In LaToza and Myers half of the bugs developers inserted were associated with reachability questions about callers and paths. The reason code was written a certain way was the information most often left unfound, and developers dug for it in change history and bug reports (Ko et al. 2007, Google's code-search data, and the git blame, -S/-G and -L documentation). Known confusing constructs measurably raise misreading in C, about half of them in Java, and some in JavaScript (atoms of confusion). A local run and official documentation for all five languages show functions with the same name behaving differently: round, sort, replace and trim. Most of this is descriptive field or lab work, not tests of whether a reading practice prevents defects. I found no openable study that reading tests, sketching, or checking history before a change improves outcomes; those rules rest on documentation, Google's practice and descriptive studies. Papers came only through GitHub-hosted channels: ACM SIGSOFT OpenTOC abstracts, the neverworkintheory bibliography, IEEE and Microsoft Academic metadata mirrors, Amy Ko's site repository with full PDFs, and an author's LaTeX source. WebSearch was not used because its budget is spent. Sources disagree on four points. Whether metrics such as cognitive complexity capture understandability: Muñoz Barón et al. and Sonar say yes, Scalabrino et al. and clippy say no. How far atoms of confusion carry beyond C: only 7 of 14 atoms showed the effect in Java, and the think-aloud study questions the measuring instrument. Whether experience drives reading order: Busjahn et al. say yes, Peachock et al. (as reported by Peitek et al.) do not. And names help comprehension as beacons (Siegmund et al.) yet mislead in practice (Ko et al., Fakhoury et al.).

12. **A change to what an existing routine returns, raises, mutates or requires for inputs its callers already pass leaves no caller in the repository that still depends on the old behaviour** — A change may alter what an existing routine does for inputs its callers already pass: its return values, the errors it raises or returns, the state or arguments it mutates, the preconditions it requires, or the defaults it applies. Every caller is found through the language server's references and incoming-calls queries, or through a text search where calls are resolved at run time (reflection, dynamic dispatch, string-named lookups, generated code), and each one is either unaffected or updated in the same change. In one lab study, half of the bugs developers inserted were associated with reachability questions about feasible paths through the program. Successful developers followed the incoming and outgoing dependencies of the code they changed, and impact analysis identifies everything a change affects before it is made.
