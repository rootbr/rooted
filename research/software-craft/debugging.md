# Research note — Debugging

Topic `debugging` of group G. Diagnostics and performance, domain `performance`, id prefix `PRF`. Formulation locators: CC23 (Code Complete 2nd ed. ch. 23 'Debugging': finding a defect by the scientific method, fixing a defect, psychological considerations, debugging tools); PoP5 (The Practice of Programming ch. 5 'Debugging': good clues and easy bugs, no clues and hard bugs, non-reproducible bugs, debugging tools); PP 'Debugging' (The Pragmatic Programmer, 20th anniversary ed., ch. 3 'The Basic Tools', topic 'Debugging').

The three canon chapters agree on the process. Code Complete ch. 23, The Practice of Programming ch. 5 and The Pragmatic Programmer Topic 20, restated openly by Zeller's Debugging Book and echoed by Spinellis and Agans, say to stabilize and reproduce the failure first and to narrow it by hypotheses and experiments rather than random edits. When the cause is not evident, shrink the input or bisect the history. Fix the cause rather than the symptom, look for the same mistake elsewhere, and keep the reproducing test. The industry disagrees in two places. The first is debugger versus print statements: Kernighan & Pike, Read and the Rust Book favour prints, Zeller and McConnell favour the debugger, and Beller et al. find printf debugging still common without ranking the two. The second is whether diagnostic aids are removed or kept behind a switch: PoP §5.3 and CC §8.6 keep switchable checks, while clippy, Ruff, ESLint, PMD, Error Prone, forbidigo and the Rust std docs treat ad-hoc prints as remnants, exempting a command-line program's output and, for dbg!, tests. The evidence supports six diff-checkable cards. (1) A fix corrects the fault rather than bypassing the failure: Parnin & Orso's preliminary Observation 1, and Yin et al.'s 14.8–24.4% incorrect post-release fixes. (2) The same mistake is corrected wherever it repeats: Park et al. found 22–33% of resolved bugs needed more than one fix attempt, and Nguyen et al. found recurring fixes in code peers. (3) The regression test's failing input is reduced: Hildebrandt & Zeller (95 actions to 3), the Go fuzzing docs and fast-check. (4–6) Hard-coded breakpoints, diagnostic prints and test focus markers are removed before the change lands, backed by tool rules in all five languages, the Rust std dbg! docs, PEP 553, the jest no-focused-tests rule and Ginkgo's docs. The process rules themselves leave no artefact in a diff: hypothesis-driven diagnosis, bisecting history, reading the failure, suspecting your own code first, recording the diagnosis and writing bug reports. They are held for Rationales or the research note. The regression test itself is already stated by the shipped TST-13 and TST-14, so it is folded there. OpenJDK's noreg exception is passed on as a TST-14 Limits update. Concurrency failures (Heisenbugs, timing-dependent bisection, Yin et al.'s 39% incorrect concurrency fixes) and tracing across services are reserved specialties outside the corpus.

## Formulation layer

I found what the three named chapters say through openable third-party copies. Code Complete ch. 23 comes from two transcriptions of McConnell's 'Debugging Reminders' checklist (cc2e.com/2368, p. 559) plus a reader's section notes. Practice of Programming ch. 5 comes from a full English transcription (debugging.tex, with Chinese glosses added by the transcriber) and its Appendix 'Collected Rules'. Pragmatic Programmer 2nd-ed. Topic 20 comes from reader summaries that agree on Tips 29–34, and a 1st-ed. summary (Tips 24–27) shows that 'Failing Test Before Fixing Code' and 'Read the Damn Error Message' were added in 2019. None of the books was opened directly, so every book quote is a transcription or a reader's note and is flagged as one in its notes; WebSearch was not used. The wider canon agrees on most points: reproduce first, test hypotheses rather than make random edits, shrink input or bisect history, fix the cause not the symptom, look for the same defect elsewhere, and add the missing test. This is stated by Zeller's Debugging Book, Spinellis's Effective Debugging, Agans' nine rules, Software Engineering at Google (ch. 9, 12, 23), git-bisect and Rust's dbg! documentation. The canon disagrees in three places: (1) debugger versus print statements — K&P prefer prints that 'stay with the program' while Zeller calls printf debugging 'horribly inefficient'; (2) whether debugging aids should be removed or kept behind a switch — PoP's check() and CC §8.6 keep them, while clippy, the Rust std doc and Spinellis Item 20 say to clean them up; (3) Ousterhout against Martin on whether TDD removes the need for debugging.

## Reception layer

Static-analysis tools agree that hard-coded breakpoints should not ship. ESLint no-debugger is in eslint:recommended at error, Biome noDebugger is recommended, Pylint W1515 is on by default, and Ruff T100 joined Ruff's default set in 0.16.0 (2026-07-23). Rules that flag print or console output are opt-in or come with stated limits: ESLint no-console is not recommended and is not meant for Node.js, Ruff T201 excludes CLI output and marks its fix unsafe, clippy dbg_macro and print_stdout are allow-by-default restriction lints, and Error Prone SystemOut is disabled by default. A card should therefore remove breakpoints unconditionally but remove only the prints added for diagnosis, and no tool can tell those prints apart from intended output. The other practices have openable support with no dispute found: reproduce first (Bettenburg survey via NWIT, Linux bisect docs), bisect or shrink (git, Go cmd/bisect, Go fuzzing, Hypothesis, Couder's git paper with Molnar's testimony), keep the reproducing test (rustc-dev-guide, Go fuzzing docs, SWE at Google ch. 9) and search for variants of the fix (the Debugging Book, CodeQL). The real dispute is debugger versus print statements. Zeller's Debugging Book, which adapts McConnell's 'devil's guide', calls printf debugging inefficient and a maintenance nightmare; Robert Read, the Rust Book and the std dbg! docs defend it, as do Kernighan and Torvalds (from memory, unfetched), while Carmack takes the debugger side (unfetched). Beller et al. find printf debugging still common, so a card should name neither tool outside an Example. Smaller limits: Allan Kelly says one-fix-at-a-time breaks down when two defects cause one failure, Parnin & Orso dispute ranked fault-localization lists, and bisection and delta debugging both need a deterministic, reliable reproducer. Not openable in this run: WebSearch budget exhausted and HN, Lobsters, LWN, jvns.ca and blogs denied, so no 2024-2026 thread or survey freshness check was possible and the practitioner quotes from memory are marked unfetched.

## Evidence layer

I found openable evidence for every candidate rule of the debugging topic. Reproduce first rests on bug-report studies (Bettenburg 2008; Soltani 2020; Rahman 2022) and an expert-interview study (Arab et al., TSE 2026, author copy on GitHub). Capture the failure as a test and keep it as a regression test rests on the Go fuzzing docs and the OpenJDK Developers' Guide. Shrink the failing input rests on the full text of Hildebrandt & Zeller's ISSTA 2000 ddmin paper (found in a GitHub-hosted corpus), with Go, cargo-fuzz and fast-check docs. Bisect the history rests on git-bisect.adoc and the abstract of Zeller's ESEC/FSE 1999 paper. Fix the cause rests on Parnin & Orso's full text, Observation 1 (participants bypassed the failure instead of correcting the fault), and on Yin et al. (14.8–24.4% of post-release fixes are incorrect). Look for the same defect elsewhere rests on Park et al. (22–33% of bugs need more than one fix attempt) and Nguyen et al. (recurring fixes cluster in code peers). Remove temporary debugging aids rests on tool rules in all five languages (ESLint, Ruff, Pylint, clippy, forbidigo, PMD, Error Prone) and on the Rust std dbg! documentation. The hypothesis rule rests on the relayed Alaboudi & LaToza 2020 snippet: no GitHub copy of its full text was found, and a citing paper reports 25 participants where the snippet says 20. It also has fetched corroboration from Arab et al., the authors' lab page and Böhme et al.'s abstract. Where the sources disagree or set conditions: Rust std allows dbg! in version control for short periods and in tests, while clippy says it should never be committed; ESLint no-console and Ruff T201 both exempt user-facing console output. Simplification and bisection are reported to work only once a defect is reproducible and not timing-sensitive. OpenJDK lets a change skip the regression test only under a label that says why (noreg-hard and others). Automated fault ranking helped only experienced developers on simpler code. Concurrency defects (39% of their fixes incorrect in Yin et al.; Heisenbugs in Arab et al.) belong to the reserved concurrency specialty and are named here only as out of scope.

## Spine — candidate rules in source order

1. **The cause of a failure is narrowed by explicit hypotheses, each tested by an experiment whose outcome can refute it, rather than by edits tried until the failure goes away** — Before a fix, the developer forms explicit hypotheses about the cause from the observed failure and tests each with an experiment that can support or refute it, such as a rerun with different input or an examination of program state, instead of changing code at random to see whether the failure disappears. In a controlled experiment, developers typically held two hypotheses per defect. A correct hypothesis early strongly predicted success, and incorrect hypotheses led developers to investigate irrelevant information. Offering potential hypotheses made developers six times more likely to succeed, while offering fault locations did not help. These figures come from a relayed abstract, and the sample size is 20 in that abstract but 25 in a citing paper.

3. **When a failure reproduces deterministically, the test that reproduces it in the fix feeds the code the failing input reduced to what still triggers the failure, not the whole input captured when the failure was found** — A large failing input, such as a captured file, payload, log or recorded sequence of user actions, is reduced before it becomes the fix's regression test. The reduction uses repeated halving or an automated reducer and stops at the smallest input that still produces the same failure. A minimal test case allows a short problem description and insight into the problem, and it subsumes several current and future bug reports. In one case study, automated reduction cut a browser crash from 95 user actions to 3 and 896 lines of HTML to the single line that caused the failure, using 139 automated test runs. Reduction presumes a test that is deterministic and quick enough to repeat many times. The failure condition must also be stated precisely enough that the reduction does not drift to a different failure.

6. **A bug fix corrects the fault that produces the failure rather than stopping the failure where it surfaces or special-casing the input that exposed it** — The change that fixes a bug alters the code whose fault produces the wrong state. Its diagnosis explains both how that fault causes the failure and why the code there is wrong. Some changes only stop the failure from appearing while the fault stays: they adjust the code where the wrong state surfaces, add a case for the one input that exposed it, or suppress the error raised there. Such a change patches the failure, not the fault. In a study with 34 graduate students debugging Java programs, quite a few participants stopped a failure by modifying the rotation calculation code instead of correcting an invalid configuration; the authors call this a preliminary observation. The topic note also yields the regression test's structure to unit-testing-and-test-quality. The OpenJDK noreg-hard, noreg-perf and noreg-other labels add an exception that TST-14's Limits do not carry: a fix may land without a regression test when the bug records why one is infeasible. That exception goes to the unit-testing maintainers as a Limits update for TST-14. The reproduce-first research (Bettenburg 2008, Soltani 2020, Rahman 2022, Arab 2026) feeds the Rationale of regression-input-reduced and of TST-13.
- Stepping through a debugger and printing temporary output are both accepted ways to observe a failing program, and neither is prescribed over the other — held-not-checkable: The choice of observation tool leaves nothing in a landed diff to judge. No openable source separates when one tool beats the other, and Beller et al. describe practice without ranking. A debugger and a print appear only in Examples. Removing whichever aid was added is carded in no-hard-coded-breakpoint and temporary-diagnostic-output-removed. Recorded in the research note.
- Diagnosis of a failure starts from its own report, the error message and the failing line of the stack trace, before any code is changed — held-not-checkable: Reading the report leaves no trace in a diff, so the rule is recorded in the research note. What a failure report should contain is owned by logging-and-diagnostic-output and error-and-exception-handling.
- A failure seen during development is tracked down when it appears rather than ignored, since it may not recur until it is far harder to fix — held-not-checkable: An ignored failure leaves nothing in the change, so the rule has no diff form. Its one checkable residue, a failing test skipped or disabled instead of investigated, is judged by the shipped TST-38 (a skipped, disabled or quarantined test states its reason or tracking reference). Recorded in the research note.
- The tests tried during a long search are recorded with their results, and the fix of a bug is committed together with its diagnosis — held-not-checkable: The record lives in the commit message, the issue or the developer's log, and none of these is in the hunks a finder holds. Commit-message content belongs to version-control practice (the committing-changes skill). Recorded in the research note.
- A bug report states how to reproduce the failure precisely and how often, what should have happened, what actually happened and the versions involved, reduced to a minimal self-contained case — held-not-checkable: A bug report is not code and does not appear in a diff, so the rule is recorded in the research note.
