# What the rules of researching-topics rest on — evidence found to 2026-11-22

The skill `researching-topics` tells an agent how to research a question on the web, and its `SKILL.md` cited no source for any of its rules. Each of its 26 rules was written out as a line of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/researching-topics/maintenance/skill-provenance.md`, searched for tests of the claim behind it and for documents that prescribe the practice, and closed with a record that says what the rule rests on. Eight rules rest on a norm, two rest on a claim with the standing supported, and sixteen rest on a decision of the repository owner whose claim waits as a hypothesis in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/researching-topics/maintenance/pending-evidence.md`; the sixteen hypotheses name 61 tests. A skeptic re-read against their sources the 23 proposals the search raised: 11 stand as stated, 12 are weaker than stated, and none fell. By the skeptic's reading 3 proposals bear against a rule, 9 bear on the same question under other conditions, and 11 are tested findings that no rule covers. None of the tests found ran inside the scope of the rules, an agent that answers a user from web sources.

The main results:

- G-06, the rule that a premise of the user which the evidence contradicts is contradicted with sources, is supported on three external tests and applied to the skill as a transfer: a dialogue that countered a participant's own conspiracy belief with evidence lowered it by 14.6 points on a 0–100 scale against 1.4 after an unrelated dialogue (dppc = 0.88, 95% CI [0.69, 1.07], p < .001) [11, Study 2].
- G-07, the rule that a specialised topic is searched in the user's phrasing and in the field's canonical terms, is supported on three external tests and applied as a transfer: all 14 information needs that only one register found were found by the agency register (exact two-sided sign test, p ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁴) [35, §6.1–6.4].
- Eight rules rest on norms issued by ACRL, Cochrane, Google Search Central, the English Wikipedia community, the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the UNC Writing Center, the PRISMA 2020 group and Anthropic.
- The sixteen decisions stand on the owner's word, and every one of the 61 tests their hypotheses name ran outside the skill's scope.
- By the skeptic's reading three tests go against a rule as the skill states it: the cue of no named experts in G-01 [34], the counterarguments shown with their weight in Phase 5 [22, 36], and, weakly, the avoidance of tutorial sites in Phase 4 [46].
- The owner is asked one decision: whether G-01 keeps "no named experts quoted" among its signs of a generated article.

The quoted words and figures of the lines are kept with their sources in `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/researching-topics/maintenance/citations.json`. Each entry of [Sources](#sources) says how it was read.

## What was investigated and why

The provenance map keys the rules of `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/researching-topics/SKILL.md` by its persona, its seven phases, its output and its ten gotchas. None of these rules cited a source.

The repository's method, `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/method.md`, treats a rule of a skill as a prescription with two premises. The goal is the owner's decision. The claim that the technique serves the goal is what a test has to bear. A rule whose ground is a document that prescribes the practice is a norm.

The question asked of each rule was: which test, or which document, does it rest on? The rules were written out as 26 lines: 16 for the persona, the phases and the output, keyed by the section of `SKILL.md`, and the 10 gotchas, keyed G-01 to G-10.

The run does not answer whether any rule serves its goal for the skill's own agent, because no test ran with that agent. It does not judge the goals, which are the owner's decisions. `SKILL.md` is unchanged. The proposals it raised stay in this note, apart from the one question put to the owner.

## Method

Each line was grounded by one researcher. Nine researchers worked on one to nine lines each. For every line the researcher stated the claim behind the rule, searched for tests of that claim and for documents that prescribe the practice, opened what was reported on, and wrote the line's text, its record and, for a decision, its hypothesis. The records follow `plugins/evidence-based-authoring/skills/scientific-method/references/record-format.md`.

A test is a comparison of the technique with the same work done without it, by a named measure, on a named sample, with its result. Someone else's test counts when its source was opened and the comparison, the measure, the sample and the result were found in it; the record marks such a test `first-hand`. A peer-reviewed source read by its abstract is marked `abstract`. A source only read about is a `relay`, a lead for a later search.

A norm is a document of an accepted issuer that prescribes the practice, opened, with its issuer, its version and the locator of the prescription. A norm shows that a practice is accepted. Whether the practice works better is a separate claim.

A rule whose claim no test within the rule's scope supports is recorded as the owner's decision, with its goal. Its claim enters the list of hypotheses with its measure, its comparison, its scope and the outcome that would refute it.

An independent reader, who saw none of the work, read the two supported records and answered the check questions of the method's seven rules: facts apart from decisions, refutability, severity of the test, reproducibility, bounds of transfer, no fitting, and revision. Both records hold all seven. No independent reading of the 24 other records is reported.

The file `citations.json` beside the provenance map holds 682 quoted words and figures with their sources. The quote check `check_quotes.py`, run over the texts fetched on 2026-11-22, finds every one of them in its source.

The researchers raised 23 proposals: tests that go against a rule as the skill states it, and tested findings that no rule covers. A skeptic opened the source of each proposal and re-read the proposal against it. The skeptic judged 11 proposals to stand as stated and 12 to be weaker than stated, and none fell. The skeptic also re-read each proposal's bearing: 3 bear against a rule, 9 bear on the same question under other conditions, and 11 are tested findings that no rule covers. This note states each proposal as the skeptic read it and with the skeptic's bearing.

Where the skeptic's reading of a source differed from what a line said, the line was corrected. Eight lines were corrected: `SKILL.md § Phase 4 consumer`, `SKILL.md § Phase 4 technical`, `SKILL.md § Phase 5 cross-reference`, G-01, G-02, G-05, G-06 and G-08. For G-06 the correction touched the line's text and not its record.

The limits of the method:

- One researcher searched each line, and no second search covered the same line.
- Each researcher worked within a step budget. Two of the first three researchers, given nine, ten and seven lines, reached their step limit before they wrote a result, and their lines were divided again into batches of one to four.
- Most tests stand in arXiv papers, many of them of 2026, and the records call several of them preprints, among them the study of Costello, Pennycook and Rand on which G-06 chiefly rests [11].
- The researchers, the independent reader and the skeptic are models of one family.
- Nine sources that looked relevant stayed unopened.
- None of the tests ran inside the scope of the rules: an agent that answers a user from web sources.

G-06 is a claim with the standing supported, on three tests, within their bounds, and applied to the skill's scope as a transfer. The claim is that an answer which contradicts, with its evidence, a premise of the user that the evidence contradicts leaves the reader's rated belief in the premise lower than the same kind of answer that leaves the premise uncontradicted. The record takes the reader's own rating of the premise as the very quantity claimed (validity: direct), and the claim would be refuted if belief after a contradicting answer were not lower than after an uncontradicting one. In Study 2 of Costello, Pennycook and Rand [11], 903 US adults who rated a conspiracy belief of their own at or above the midpoint held a GPT-5.2 dialogue that countered it with evidence. Their belief fell by 14.6 points against 1.4 after a dialogue on an unrelated topic (dppc = 0.88, 95% CI [0.69, 1.07], p < .001). The dialogue left 23.1% (75 of 324) below the midpoint against 1.5% (3 of 197) after the control (Fisher's exact p < .001). A single tailored message did nearly as well against that control (dppc = 0.69, 95% CI [0.53, 0.87], p < .001). In Study 1, with 1,290 adults, the same treatment told to persuade without factual evidence lowered belief by 3.81 points, below the evidence-based treatment by a posterior mean of −7.17 (95% CrI [−11.39, −2.83], BF10 = 11.43). In Lucy et al. [26, §5], 300 readers rated the accuracy of a false statement after an explicit refutation and after affirmation alone: −0.57 [−0.90, −0.21] against +0.17 [−0.07, +0.39] in English and −0.51 [−0.90, −0.12] against +0.63 [+0.37, +0.90] in German, as 95% intervals per condition with no test of the difference named. In Chinese the interval of refutation, −0.45 [−0.95, +0.04], holds zero. In the meta-analysis of Chan and Albarracín [8, Results], 205 effect sizes from 74 reports with N = 60,861, corrections of science-relevant misinformation gave d = 0.19, P = 0.131, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.43, in the claim's direction and not told from chance, and detailed corrections did better than succinct ones, b(60) = 0.64, P = 0.020. The bounds are US adults recruited online, with GPT-4 and GPT-5.2, in a 2026 preprint; English-, German- and Chinese-speaking Prolific workers reading answers of seven models of 2026; and experiments with students and online adults pooled in 2023. The skill's scope differs: the readers of the tests rated a belief they held or a statement put to them, after answers that GPT, Gemini, Qwen and other models wrote for the study, while the scope is a premise of the user's own research question answered by a Claude model from web sources in Claude Code. The Costello treatment was told to persuade, and the refuting answers of Lucy et al. cite sources only where their models did so. The line also names a bound from Wang, Shwartz and Gonen [48]: the rule holds for premises the evidence contradicts, apart from premises the evidence leaves unverified (see Tests under other conditions).

G-07 is a claim with the standing supported, on three tests, within their bounds, and applied to the skill's scope as a transfer. The claim is that, for a specialised topic, a query in the field's canonical terms beside the query in the user's phrasing finds the relevant source among the top five or ten results for more information needs than the user's phrasing alone. It would be refuted if, among the needs one phrasing finds and the other misses, those found only by the canonical query were no more frequent than those found only by the user's phrasing, by a paired test at the 0.05 level. In Sapru [35, §6.1–6.4 Tables 1 and 2], 25 information needs over 51 passages of US federal benefit-eligibility rules were each phrased in agency register and in plain register. BM25 found a gold passage in its top five for all 25 agency-register queries and for 11 of the 25 plain ones, and all 14 needs that only one register found went to the agency register (exact two-sided sign test, p ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁴). Plain-to-formal terms added to the plain query raised BM25 Recall@5 from 44% to 80%, with no test named for that step. In Shanian et al. [38, Table 3], the original question was set against GPT-4.1 rewordings with other vocabulary, the results merged, under dense retrieval with reranking: on EnterpriseRAG-Bench HIT@10 was 42.91 against 39.22 (paired bootstrap, p < 0.01) and Recall@10 37.21 against 34.33 (p < 0.05), and on AmbigNQ HIT@10 was 95.47 against 93.67 (p < 0.001). On HotpotQA, a general multi-hop set outside the claim's scope, Recall@10 was 86.14 against 87.70 (p < 0.001), against the claim. In Barron et al. [5, Tables I and III], generated vocabulary added to SPLADE-v3 queries raised nDCG@10 in all twelve method and collection comparisons on NFCorpus, TREC-COVID and SciDocs, eleven of them significant after Holm correction of two-sided paired sign-randomization tests, for a neighbouring technique scored by a ranking measure. The bounds are BM25, TF-IDF and a term-graph retriever over the benefit-rule passages, bge dense retrieval with cross-encoder reranking over three seeds, and SPLADE-v3 over three BEIR scientific collections, all in 2026. The skill's scope differs: the tests search closed corpora with fixed gold passages and queries written by the authors or by GPT-4.1, while the scope is web search engines whose ranking is not only lexical, queries written by a Claude agent, and topics whose relevant sources are not fixed in advance.

## The norms

Eight lines rest on a norm. A norm line carries no hypothesis, so whether each practice serves the skill's goal is a claim these records do not state.

- `SKILL.md § Phase 1` [2]. The ACRL Framework for Information Literacy for Higher Education, adopted 2016-01-11 with revisions of 2026-09-02, lists among a learner's knowledge practices in the frame Searching as Strategic Exploration to "determine the initial scope of the task and types of sources that could address their information needs" and to "match information needs to appropriate search tools and information producers". Its frame Authority Is Constructed and Contextual states that resources "are evaluated based on the information need and the context in which the information will be used".
- `SKILL.md § Phase 3 phrasing` [9]. The Cochrane Handbook v6.5.1, Chapter 4 §4.4.4 and MECIR Box 4.4.c expectation C33, prescribes: "Searches for Cochrane reviews should use an appropriate combination of these two approaches, i.e. text words and controlled vocabulary". The line also names the test of Sapru [35, Table 1], on which G-07 is supported, and the test of Shanian et al. [38, Table 3].
- `SKILL.md § Phase 4 consumer` [16, 15]. Google Search Central's Write high quality reviews, last updated 2025-12-10, § best practices, asks a review to give evidence of the writer's own experience, first-hand support for a best pick, and what a product does beyond what the manufacturer says. Its Spam policies for Google web search, last updated 2026-08-28, § Thin affiliation and § Scaled content abuse, hold affiliate pages copied from the merchant and pages mass-produced to manipulate rankings to be spam that may rank lower or not appear.
- `SKILL.md § Phase 4 factual` [52]. Wikipedia:Reliable sources at oldid 1385652516 holds academic and peer-reviewed publications, scholarly monographs and textbooks to be usually the most reliable sources, allows reputable encyclopedias to be cited, holds sites of largely user-generated content such as Internet forums generally unacceptable, and holds content produced by LLMs generally unreliable (§ Scholarship; § Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources; § User-generated content, WP:UGC; § Sources produced by machine learning, WP:RSLLM).
- `SKILL.md § Phase 6 direct` [43, 44]. The EIA Writing Style Guide of December 2020, Chapter 1 item 2 Put the Bottom Line Up Front, prescribes to "Start with your main message so your readers can quickly get the most important information" and to order content from the highest level to supporting details. The UNC Writing Center's Literature Reviews, § Find a focus, states that a review "is usually organized around ideas, not the sources themselves as an annotated bibliography would be organized".
- `SKILL.md § Phase 6 discovery` [31, 10]. The PRISMA 2020 statement reports results "For each synthesis", with "for each the summary estimate and its precision" (items 20a and 20b), and closes the discussion on the "implications of the results for practice, policy, and future research" (item 23d). The Cochrane Handbook v6.5.1 Chapter 15, §15.1 and §15.6.1 to §15.6.3, closes a review on Authors' conclusions in two standard subsections, the second of which "should comment on the need for further research".
- `SKILL.md § Output` [4, 53]. Anthropic's Web search tool page, § Citations, states that "Citations are always enabled for web search", each citation carrying the URL and title of its source, and that "When displaying API outputs directly to end users, citations must be included to the original source". Wikipedia:Verifiability at revision 1386008258, lead section (WP:V), states that quotations and challenged material "must include an inline citation to a reliable source that directly supports" it. For the Markdown file on request or after a question map the line records that no document or test was found and names it a house convention.
- G-03 [51]. Wikipedia:Neutral point of view at revision 1386821137 of 2026-11-20, section Explanation (WP:SERIOUSLYCONTESTED), prescribes: "If different reliable sources make conflicting assertions about a matter, treat these assertions as opinions rather than facts". Its lead paragraph concerns representing all significant views.

## Tests against a rule

`SKILL.md § Phase 4 technical` avoids tutorial farms. In CodeRAG-Bench [46, §2.2, §4 Table 7, Tables 11 and 12], each of five corpora was retrieved alone with an OpenAI-embedding retriever and the first 500 tokens of each document, and the generated code was scored by pass@1. The tutorial corpus is drawn from GeeksforGeeks, W3Schools, tutorialspoint and Towards Data Science, taken from the ClueWeb22 crawl. GPT-4o on HumanEval reached 90.2 with tutorials, 90.9 with library documentation and 91.5 with Stack Overflow, against 75.6 without retrieval, and on ODEX 44.2, 47.6 and 40.3 against 44.6; the source names no test. The conditions are Python code generation on two benchmarks from a fixed crawl, with no agent choosing among sources. The skeptic judged the proposal weaker than stated and kept its bearing against the rule, as a weak one. The skeptic found missing that library documentation beats tutorials on ODEX for four of five models, that tutorial retrieval falls below no retrieval on ODEX, and that the HumanEval gains partly reflect posts and tutorials about the same problem that show the answer. A difference of one to four points with no test does not demonstrate equivalence, and the test does not exercise the rule's reasons for avoiding such sites, outdated or unversioned content. Whether these sites are tutorial farms in the rule's sense is the researcher's characterisation. The proposal is to test whether avoiding such sites changes answer correctness before the avoidance is kept as a claim, and to keep it as a decision meanwhile.

G-01 counts no named experts among its signs of a generated article. In Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer [34, Tables 1, 3 and 17, §3], five expert annotators, all heavy users of LLMs, and four nonexperts labelled 300 English non-fiction articles across five experiments; the generated articles were written by GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and o1-Pro from each human article's title, subtitle, length, publication and section. The experts' average true positive rate was 92.7% and false positive rate 4.0%, against 56.7% and 51.7% for nonexperts, averages of Experiment 1 alone, where the text gives the expert false positive rate as 3.3%. Of the experts' explanations, correct or not, 53.1% named vocabulary, 35.9% sentence structure, 11.7% names and titles and 7.2% factuality. Of the articles, 63.3% from GPT-4o and 70% from Claude-3.5-Sonnet include the name Emily or Sarah, and o1-Pro favours names of real people. The source compares no rate of named experts between human and generated articles and names no test. The conditions are human expert readers rather than an agent, and articles generated from titles rather than found on the web. The skeptic judged the proposal weaker than stated and kept its bearing against the rule for the cue of no named experts: generated articles routinely name generic experts, so the cue would miss them. The skeptic found that the proposal overstates the rest: the study's taxonomy holds names and titles and factuality, and the experts' guide calls many specific factual claims a sign of human writing, which agrees with the cues of generic phrasing and of no specific numbers or dates. Nothing in the study shows that the annotators saw bylines, so the cue of no named author could not appear in it. The proposal offers to replace the cue list with the clues the study found, or to drop the cues and keep the action alone: a claim from an article whose primary source the agent cannot find is marked low-confidence whoever wrote it.

The owner is asked one decision on this test: whether G-01 keeps "no named experts quoted" among its signs of a generated article. If the cue stays, G-01 keeps a sign that the generated articles of this study did not show. If it goes, G-01 keeps its other three cues and its action on the claim.

`SKILL.md § Phase 5 presenting` has counterarguments follow with their weight. In Koehler's Study 2 [22], 393 US adults recruited on Mechanical Turk were analysed; each saw one of four statements of an economic experts' panel, two with high consensus and two with low, with the table of the panel's percentages. The balance condition added one comment from an agreeing expert and one from a disagreeing expert. A 2 × 2 ANOVA on the combined measure of perceived agreement gave a condition by consensus interaction of F(1, 389) = 11.01, p = .001, partial η² = .028, and balance mainly lowered perceived consensus on the high-consensus issues. On the item about twenty experts alone the interaction was not significant (p = .107). In Schmid, Schwarzer and Betsch [36, Experiments 1–3], 887 German university students in three lab experiments watched a mock television discussion about a vaccine against a fictitious disease with five advocates beside one denier or three beside three. The effect of outnumbering on attitude change was null in each experiment: F(1, 97) = 0.11, p = .737; F(1, 382) = 0.27, p = .605; F(1, 388) = 2.10, p = .148. The skeptic judged the proposal to stand as stated, with its bearing against the rule. The skeptic found missing that Koehler's agree-first order of comments raised policy endorsement (F(1, 186) = 8.50, p = .004), which favours leading with the strongest position, and that in Schmid's experiments the advocate's rebuttal reduced the denier's effect, which favours open contradiction. Schmid's weight is a head count of guests, with one advocate speaking in Experiments 1 and 2, not a stated share, and its nulls carry no equivalence test. The readers were lay adults and students reading a panel table or watching a mock debate, not readers of an agent's research answer. The proposal is that, where the sources show a strong consensus, the agent states the share with its source and keeps a lone dissent out of the quoted examples, and that this variant is tested against the rule as it stands.

## Tested findings no rule covers

Eleven proposals are tested findings that no rule of the skill covers, by the skeptic's reading. Each candidate below is a hypothesis; none is in the skill. None of the eleven is put to the owner: the one question the owner is asked concerns G-01 and stands under Tests against a rule.

- `SKILL.md § Phase 4 factual`. Candidate: a step that counts, before the answer is sent, how many cited sources fall in the preferred classes raises their share more than the preference stated in the prompt. Test [13, §4.6, §5.7]: with a trusted-domain list in the prompt, 21% of 1,183 citations of Gemini 3 Pro fell on listed domains against 12% of 1,330 without it, and the source names no test. Conditions: 287 questions on EU affairs, each answered twice in April 2026, with 275 and 274 answers completed. Missing, by the skeptic: the source tests a list of named domains, not source classes; its citations were not reviewed by experts; the citation mix depends on the configuration; and the proposed check is untested. Verdict: stands.
- G-04. Candidate: the decisive claims of each top pick are checked against a source independent of the seller before it is recommended, which lowers the rate of flawed picks. Test [49, Tables 5 and 7, §4.2.2]: a seller page rewritten as an "independent buyer guide" put a flawed product into the top three in 80.7%, 77.6% and 92.6% of cases against 3.4%, 8.1% and 12.7% for a truthful rewrite with Gemma 4 31B IT, Qwen3.6-27B and Devstral Small 2 24B, with 95% intervals of about ±0.8 to ±1.9 points. Checking important claims against supporting and conflicting evidence before ranking lowered that rate by 29.7, 39.2 and 17.7 points, averaged over eight attack variants, leaving it above the levels without the attack; the reading of the source records no interval for this reduction. Conditions: 600 shopping cases in six verticals built from shopping-research traces, synthetic seller pages, open-weights models of 24B to 31B, and the conflicting truthful source in the same packet as the attack. Missing, by the skeptic: 39.2 points is the maximum, for Qwen; the reduction is averaged over all eight variants and not over the buyer-guide variant alone; and that G-04's two cues are absent from such pages is the researcher's inference, since the source does not test them. Verdict: stands.
- `SKILL.md § Phase 4 local`. Candidate: every answer on a local or bureaucratic process cites the issuing authority's page for the user's jurisdiction. Test [24, §4.2 Table 3, §5 Figure 4]: across ten search agents, skipped searches and failed retrieval of the official page averaged 72.1% of failures (34.5% and 37.6%), and with that page supplied 2.3% to 4.8% of the failed questions stayed wrong; the source names no test for the failure shares. Conditions: civic questions at federal, state or provincial, and municipal levels in Australia, Canada and the US, with question and answer pairs generated by Claude Opus 4.6. Missing, by the skeptic: 72.1% is an average of per-model shares that range from 48.9% to 92.9%; both Claude agents searched on 99% of questions or more, so for agents like the skill's the failures are mostly failures of retrieval; and the paper presents its remedies as implications, not as tested interventions. Verdict: stands.
- G-02. Candidate: the date filter is anchored to the date the user's question concerns and to the date of the relevant change, and a fixed window back from today is kept only for questions about the present. Test [33, §4.5, §5.2]: on German statutory questions whose facts predate an amendment, split at a fixed midpoint date of 2014-10-30, web search scored about 0.20 to 0.24 higher on the recent than on the older questions, at p < 0.05 in three of six model and question-type cells (p = 0.013, 0.015 and 0.034) and not in the other three (p = 0.052, 0.226 and 0.657), and §4.5 names no test. Retrieval filtered to the law's versions valid at the question's as-of date beat web search on all four measures (Welch t-tests, p < 0.05), and the ablation that removes the filter is reported as a substantial drop without numbers. Conditions: ChatGPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5 and DeepSeek V3.2, scored by an LLM judge. Missing, by the skeptic: the split is a calendar midpoint, not the amendment; no arm used a filter anchored to the day of the search, so the proposal implies a comparison the source never ran; and the skeptic did not verify against `SKILL.md` that G-02 counts its window back from today, which this note does not verify either. Verdict: weaker.
- G-02. Candidate: the change and its effective date are found at the issuing source first, each other source is then marked as before or after it, and a page's date is checked to be the date of its content. Test [3, §3.2 Table 7, §3.5 and the appendix on the re-ranker]: a re-ranker by similarity and document year over one up-to-date and two outdated documents per item lowered superseded answers by 4.6 to 10.0 points with correct dates, three of four cells at p < .05 by exact paired McNemar tests; with dates absent three cells gained 1.0 to 4.0 points, none significantly, and one lost about 1.1; under incorrect dates its selection of the up-to-date document fell to zero. Given the dates alone, large models showed applicability gaps of .12 [.04, .22] and .14 [.06, .24], against 1.00 and .94 [.86, 1.00] when a stated boundary said when the evidence stops applying, as item-clustered bootstrap 95% intervals. Missing, by the skeptic: the result under incorrect dates concerns retrieval, not answers; the re-ranker is not a date filter in a web search; no Claude model took the applicability test; and the proposed two-step procedure is untested. Verdict: stands.
- `SKILL.md § Phase 5 confidence`. Candidate: confidence marks stay at the level of whole findings, each beside its source link, and a test measures whether the marks lower the share of users who open the cited sources. Test [45, §5.2]: uncertainty marked on each reasoning step, against none, lowered link clicks to 0.318 [0.213, 0.422] from 0.557 [0.450, 0.664], p < .05, OR = 0.42, and web searches to 0.247 [0.184, 0.310] from 0.342 [0.278, 0.406], p < .05, in mixed-effects models with Wald tests, while marks on the whole output or on each word lowered neither. - `SKILL.md § Phase 7`. Candidate: a test on research reports with planted errors measures whether running part of the appraisal on another model family adds coverage. - G-08. Candidate: a large fetched page is saved to a file and its passages are extracted on demand, with search across the saved pages. Test [25, Table 2]: pages kept in a per-question cache on the filesystem and read by grep across the cache and line-numbered reads beat a browser that releases each page on the next open by 44.5 against 42.0, 52.5 against 50.5 and 70.5 against 66.0 BrowseComp accuracy, on 200 sampled questions, best of three runs; the source names no test. Conditions: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one harness with no context management, scored by an LLM judge.
