# Rooted — Claude Code plugins rooted in evidence

[![License: Apache-2.0](https://img.shields.io/badge/license-Apache--2.0-blue.svg)](LICENSE)
[![GitHub stars](https://img.shields.io/github/stars/rootbr/rooted?style=social)](https://github.com/rootbr/rooted/stargazers)
[![Rules cited from](https://img.shields.io/badge/rules%20cited%20from-66%20papers%20%C2%B7%206%20standards-success)](#references)

Skills for the full evidence-based preparation pipeline: research any topic with source verification, map its complete question space before you start, then write AI agent context that actually works — grounded instructions and disciplined reasoning instead of vibes. [Research shows](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988) LLM-generated context files do not generally improve agent task success while raising inference cost by 20–23% — and the repository overviews they typically contain are specifically unhelpful. Rooted structurally refuses them: every rule traces to a peer-reviewed paper or an RFC. Software-development and code-review skills apply the same discipline to everyday coding.

- **Evidence-based, not LLM-generated.** Every rule traces to a peer-reviewed paper, RFC/spec, or a hands-on test — not to "ask the model to write a skill."
- **Lean context over static dumps.** For example, instead of an `/init`-style project-structure blob in `CLAUDE.md` (a file class shown not to improve success while raising cost ~20%, with repository overviews specifically found unhelpful [[15]](#references)), orientation happens on demand via [`overview.sh`](overview.sh) with an explicit stop-instruction. The agent doesn't preemptively slurp the whole tree into context, so it doesn't fill its working memory with project content it doesn't need and later lose focus on the actual task.

## Methodology

Every rule, checklist item, and review criterion traces to one of:

- **Academic research** — arxiv.org, peer-reviewed papers, Google Scholar, PubMed
- **Technical standards** — RFCs, official specifications, language/framework documentation
- **Human-tested** — double-checked and reviewed by a human before commit

## Plugins

### evidence-based-authoring

Five evidence-based skills. Three form a preparation pipeline — research → explore → write: gather verified sources, map every angle of a topic, then produce agent instructions grounded in evidence. `/appraising-research` adds a critical-appraisal gate over a finished report — grading evidence quality, surfacing hidden assumptions, hunting competing hypotheses and blind spots, and mapping who benefits. The last, `/reading-companion`, is an active-reading companion for a book you study yourself — grounded in learning-science research.

| Skill | Description |
|--|--|
| `/researching-topics` | Research any topic on the web with source verification and critical analysis |
| `/discovering-subtopics` | Build a maximum-breadth question map across any topic using 10 complementary methods — structural decomposition, perspective shifting, causal chains, and more |
| `/appraising-research` | Critically appraise a finished research report — evidence grading, hidden assumptions, competing hypotheses, premortem/red-team, and beneficiary/funding analysis — then rank what to re-check, with adversarial debate on the highest-stakes findings |
| `/auditing-ai-context` | Audit the prose an agent reads and the prose an agent writes: CLAUDE.md, SKILL.md, prompts and KB cards; design docs and the comments attached to code; a drafted answer against the house output-style rules. Purges superseded-design traces after a rework |
| `/reading-companion` | Active-reading companion for a book you study yourself — classify it, scaffold a per-book workspace, then explain intent, translate fragments, walk proofs, and capture your own notes; amplifies recall, never substitutes for it |

### Output-style and present-design research

Sources behind the `S-` and `D-` rule cards — what an agent writes, as opposed to what it reads. Each entry keeps the narrowing its rule card carries.

36. Haviland & Clark (1974). *What's New? Acquiring New Information as a Process in Comprehension.* [doi:10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80003-4) — Given-new: a sentence whose antecedent is already given is comprehended faster (835 vs 1016 ms, Δ181 ms, p < .025). Undergraduates and two-sentence pairs, so transfer to expert technical prose is an extrapolation.
37. Cleveland & McGill (1984). *Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to the Development of Graphical Methods.* [doi:10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080) — Two experiments, 51 subjects each: length errors 40%–250% larger than position, gross errors at 5.3× the position rate. The six-rank ordering of encodings is **hypothesized, not measured**, and excludes hue.
38. Larkin & Simon (1987). *Why a Diagram is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words.* [doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1101_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1101_4) — A diagram's advantage is computational, not informational: it indexes by two-dimensional location, and fails without the operators to read it. The paper counts only the sentential side (138 elements), so no speedup ratio follows from it.
39. Gibson (2000). *The Dependency Locality Theory: A Distance-Based Theory of Linguistic Complexity.* [tedlab.mit.edu PDF](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_2000_DLT.pdf) — Difficulty is indexed by the maximal number of incomplete syntactic dependencies (1 / 3 / 5 across the worked series); the same words reordered out of the nesting are easier. The load is peak open dependencies, not nesting depth.
40. Moreno & Mayer (2002). *Verbal Redundancy in Multimedia Learning: When Reading Helps Listening.* [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.94.1.156](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.94.1.156) — With no concurrent graphics, narration plus identical on-screen text beats narration alone (d = 0.78 retention, 1.62 transfer); 74 college students, scored by retention and transfer tests.
41. Sternadori (2008). *Cognitive Processing of News as a Function of Structure: A Comparison Between Inverted Pyramid and Chronology.* [PhD dissertation, University of Missouri-Columbia](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/f2eb/ad1fa4020d0647314314457ec2c003516087.pdf) — Recognition and cued recall null (p = .94; p = .26); comprehension trends *toward* the inverted pyramid (p = .08); secondary-task reaction time favours it (396.4 vs 412.7 ms, p = .05). One dissertation, printed news stories, whole-text reading.
42. Peelle, Troiani, Wingfield & Grossman (2010). *Neural Processing during Older Adults' Comprehension of Spoken Sentences: Age Differences in Resource Allocation and Connectivity.* [PMC2837088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2837088/) — Heard centre-embedded sentences: object-relative 0.843 against subject-relative 0.935 proportion correct, F(1,38) = 45.47, p < .001. Six-word sentences, so the figure transfers to no other length.
43. Mayer & Fiorella (2014). *Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning: Coherence, Signaling, Redundancy, Spatial Contiguity, and Temporal Contiguity Principles.* [doi:10.1017/CBO9781139547369.015](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139547369.015) — Redundancy principle, 16 of 16 tests, median d = 0.86 (range 0.19–1.91) — for material **with** graphics; the authors name material lacking graphics as a boundary condition where the effect is "eliminated or even reversed".
44. Yavchitz, Ravaud, Hopewell, Baron & Boutron (2014). *Impact of Adding a Limitations Section to Abstracts of Systematic Reviews on Readers' Interpretation: A Randomized Controlled Trial.* [doi:10.1186/1471-2288-14-123](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-14-123) — A limitations section appended to an abstract left all three interpretation outcomes unchanged: confidence in the results 4.6 (SD 2.5) without against 4.4 (SD 2.3) with, difference 0.2 (95% CI −0.4 to 0.7), p = 0.5. A null result, on 300 reader-experts the authors call unrepresentative of all readers.
45. Wolfer (2016). *The Impact of Nominalisations on the Reading Process: A Case-Study Using the Freiburg Legalese Reading Corpus.* [doi:10.17169/langsci.b108.298](https://doi.org/10.17169/langsci.b108.298) — Nominalisations raise total reading time (β = 0.131, t = 8.911) more than finite verbs do (β = 0.053 vs 0.016). Eye-tracking measured **reading time, not comprehension**, which stayed flat at 84% / 88% / 87%; 80 readers with no legal background, German court decisions.
46. Wen, Nagy, Bavota & Lanza (2019). *A Large-Scale Empirical Study on Code-Comment Inconsistencies.* [doi:10.1109/ICPC.2019.00019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICPC.2019.00019) — 13%–20% of code changes trigger a class- or method-comment change (7% method / 13% class). Java only, ≥500 commits, ≥10 stars, and the authors guard that the remaining ~80% are not thereby inconsistent.
47. Ferreira (2021). *In Defense of the Passive Voice.* [doi:10.1037/amp0000620](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000620) — A review essay with **no data of its own**: omitting the agent is usually correct, so an agentless passive is flaggable only where the agent is known and material to the reader.
48. Clinton-Lisell (2022). *Listening Ears or Reading Eyes: A Meta-Analysis of Reading and Listening Comprehension Comparisons.* [doi:10.3102/00346543211060871](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211060871) — 46 studies, N = 4,687: listening against reading g = 0.07 (p = .23, n.s.); reading wins only when self-paced (g = 0.13) and for inferential comprehension (g = 0.36).
49. Tan, Wagner & Treude (2022). *Detecting Outdated Code Element References in Software Repository Documentation.* [arXiv:2212.01479](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01479) — Most of 3,000+ GitHub projects carry, at some point in their history, a documentation reference to a code element whose every source instance was deleted; validated by filing issues that led to fixes.
50. Lanham et al. (2023). *Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning.* [arXiv:2307.13702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702) — Reasoning stated step by step *before* the answer improves performance; larger models produce less faithful stated reasoning.
51. Singhal et al. (2023). *A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF.* [arXiv:2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716) — Optimizing for response length is a significant factor behind RLHF gains; a purely length-based reward reproduces most of the downstream improvement over SFT.
52. Steyvers et al. (2024). *What Large Language Models Know and What People Think They Know.* [arXiv:2401.13835](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13835) — Users grow more confident with longer explanations even where the extra length does not improve accuracy; matching explanations to internal confidence narrows the calibration gap.
53. Wendler et al. (2024). *Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers.* [arXiv:2402.10588](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10588) — Multilingual transformers pivot through an abstract concept space lying closer to English than to other languages. Scoped to the Llama-2 family, and it implies no accuracy gain from restating a request in English.
54. Wallace et al. (2024). *The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions.* [arXiv:2404.13208](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208) — Hierarchy following is obtained by a training method, not by declaring the hierarchy in the prompt; generalizes to unseen attacks at minimal capability cost.
55. Kim et al. (FAccT 2024). *"I'm Not Sure, But…": Examining the Impact of Large Language Models' Uncertainty Expression on User Reliance and Trust.* [arXiv:2405.00623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623) — Pre-registered, N = 404: first-person uncertainty lowers agreement with the system and raises reader accuracy by cutting overreliance; uncertainty from a general perspective is weaker and not significant.
